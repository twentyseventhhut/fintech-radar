AI could erode bankers’ reasoning skills if employees rely on models to do their analytical work, warns Chris Churchman, who leads Marquee, Goldman's digital platform for institutional clients.

A Goldman Sachs partner leading one of the bank's flagship artificial intelligence projects warned that AI's spread across Wall Street risks hobbling the thinking capabilities of the next generation of financiers.
"There's a huge danger here that in the era of AI, we outsource our reasoning to these models, and we have cognitive atrophy that stops us being able to reason from first principles ourselves," said Chris Churchman, who leads Goldman's digital platform for institutional clients called Marquee .
The comments came during the latest episode of the firm's "Exchanges" podcast, according to a transcript provided exclusively to CNBC.
Just as people lost navigation and memorization skills with modern inventions, bankers risk losing analytical abilities if algorithms handle all the heavy lifting, Churchman said. 
"Reasoning is still important," he said. "You still need to reason about [problems] and structure it into an argument, and now we're delegating reasoning."
Wall Street's push to enmesh AI into all of its trading and banking processes could be a kind of devil's bargain: It will make the industry more profitable today while potentially eroding the talent it needs for tomorrow. With AI taking over more of the routine work that has traditionally taught young bankers and traders how to think and make decisions, firms risk sacrificing the culture that turns junior employees into seasoned Wall Street talent.
It could even reduce the need for junior bankers in the first place. Last year, CNBC reported that Wall Street firms were examining ways of using AI to lower the ratio of junior bankers to senior employees. 
Banks need to find a balance between using AI and preserving Wall Street's apprenticeship culture, said Churchman, who ran currency trading at UBS before joining Goldman in 2021.
"You learn by doing, and a lot of knowledge is tacit, it was never written down," he said.
Goldman needs "to make sure we don't lose that tacit and intuitive knowledge that some of our best people have today [and] to ensure the next generation have it too," Churchman said. 
For instance, junior traders learn by fielding client pricing requests under supervision of experienced risk takers, Churchman said. 
"We can absolutely automate that," he said, "but then do we get the senior traders that fully understand?"
Systems must be designed so that employees still call the shots in high-stakes, high-uncertainty decisions rather than becoming passive operators, Churchman said. 
Even Goldman, one of the world's top investment banks, hasn't yet "figured out" how it will manage the transition the company has begun, said Churchman, who is also co-chair of the firm's Global Banking and Markets AI working group.
 Error-free?
Also in the podcast interview , Churchman shared lessons from implementing AI into Marquee, which is used by hedge funds and other institutional clients to access Goldman's market data, research, risk analytics and trade execution services.
The Marquee AI platform is only available to Goldman employees for now, he said.
The toughest challenge, from a technical standpoint, is in ensuring that AI answers are 100% factual and can be audited, he said. While consumer AI chatbots warn users of possible mistakes, in high finance, the tolerance for errors is low.
Churchman said that in developing the firm's AI platform for clients, the software made a startling admission. 
"When we challenged it hard, at least it was honest," Churchman said. "It was like, 'Look, in the end, I'm better at sounding thorough than being thorough.'"

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
Not a deal, launch, or data release — a **soundbite from a podcast**. On Goldman's own "Exchanges" podcast (transcript given exclusively to CNBC), **Chris Churchman** — a GS partner who runs **Marquee** (the digital platform hedge funds / institutional clients use for market data, research, risk analytics, trade execution) and co-chairs the Global Banking & Markets AI working group — warned of a "huge danger" of **"cognitive atrophy"**: if bankers outsource reasoning to models, the next generation loses the ability to reason from first principles. He framed Wall Street's AI push as a potential "devil's bargain" — more profitable today, eroding tomorrow's talent — and flagged the threat to the **apprenticeship model** (juniors learn tacit knowledge by doing, e.g. fielding client pricing requests under senior supervision).
- **Why this framing:** Churchman is the person *building* GS's client-facing AI, so a "we must preserve human reasoning" message is partly a credibility/trust play for a bank selling AI-touched analytics to institutional clients where **error tolerance is near zero**. He explicitly said the Marquee AI tool is **internal-only for now** and that the hard problem is making answers "100% factual and auditable."
- **The most honest line** — the model's own admission when challenged: *"in the end, I'm better at sounding thorough than being thorough."* That is the real substance: a GS insider conceding generative AI's accuracy gap in a high-finance context.
- **Date flag (de-PR'd):** The CNBC article is dated **2026-08-24**; it entered our pipeline **2026-10-12** — this is a ~7-week-old opinion piece being re-surfaced by an aggregator channel, not news.

(3) **"why now":** the deskilling debate is going mainstream — the Jan-2026 Anthropic Economic Index flags deskilling for occupations where AI absorbs the most cognitively demanding tasks, and ~half of business leaders already report observing it (per ScienceDirect/survey coverage, as of 2026). So a GS partner publicly naming "cognitive atrophy" is a notable de-PR moment: the same firm cutting junior ranks for efficiency is also warning the cuts erode its own talent pipeline. **Risk flags.**
1. **Talent-pipeline erosion (the stated risk).** If AI handles the routine work that historically trained juniors — and GS has already cut the junior cohort to lift the efficiency ratio — the second-order effect is a thinner bench of seasoned risk-takers in 5–10 years. Short-term margin gain, long-term talent liability; the firm is explicitly trading one for the other.
2. **Accuracy / auditability in high-stakes finance.** Churchman's own admission that the model "is better at sounding thorough than being thorough" is a direct flag: in trading/banking the error tolerance is near-zero, so un-audited AI output carries reputational, regulatory and P&L risk. Marquee's AI stays internal-only precisely because of this — a tell that client-facing reliability isn't there yet.
3. **Narrative vs. arithmetic mismatch.** The efficiency ratio improved, but Q2 2026 operating expenses were +26% YoY ($11.67bn) — the ratio fell only because revenue rose 39%. So the "AI is cutting absolute cost" story is not yet visible in the expense line; the gain is operating leverage from a trading boom, not proven AI savings. Risk that AI-productivity claims are credited for what is a cyclical revenue surge.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** `(analysis)` This reframes the Wall-Street-AI trade from a pure cost/headcount story to a **talent-durability** one: the bull case (AI lifts efficiency, one analyst does the work of three) and the insider warning (cognitive atrophy, lost tacit knowledge) are now coming from the *same* firm. Falsifiable thesis to watch: if GS keeps compressing the junior-to-senior ratio while the efficiency ratio holds below ~60%, the deskilling warning is being ignored in practice — watch future GS 10-Qs for headcount mix and the comp-to-net-revenue ratio, and whether Marquee's AI ever ships client-facing (the real test of whether the accuracy problem is solved). Thesis breaks if GS visibly re-invests in junior training/apprenticeship headcount despite AI.
