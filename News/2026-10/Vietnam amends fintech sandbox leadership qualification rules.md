# Vietnam amends fintech sandbox leadership qualification rules

> [!info] 2026-10-06 · 1 упоминаний · 1 источника(ов) с текстом
> Каналы: This Week in Fintech

## Агрегированный текст (из дайджестов)

[This Week in Fintech] Vietnam's State Bank amended fintech sandbox rules, converting dual qualification requirements to either/or standard for executive leadership.

Vietnam's government moved on October 6, 2026, to dismantle one of the most complained-about barriers to entry in its year-old fintech regulatory sandbox: the rule requiring a company's chief executive or legal representative to hold both a relevant university degree and at least two years of management experience in banking or finance. Decree No. 382/2026/NĐ-CP , signed by Deputy Prime Minister Nguyễn Văn Thắng and effective immediately, converts that "both" requirement into an "either/or" — meaning a technically accomplished founder without a banking career, or a seasoned finance executive without a university degree, can now lead a sandbox participant without being disqualified. The change arrives less than sixteen months after the sandbox launched, a revision speed that is unusually fast by global regulatory standards.
What Changed and What Didn't
The sandbox — formally established by Decree 94/2025/NĐ-CP, issued April 29, 2025 , and effective July 1, 2025 — covers three fintech solution categories: peer-to-peer (P2P) lending, credit scoring, and open banking through shared application programming interfaces, commonly called Open APIs. Under the original decree, a fintech company's general director or legal representative was required to hold a university degree and two years of management experience in finance or banking. Both conditions were mandatory.
Decree 382 converts this into a choice. A general director now qualifies if they hold a degree in economics, finance, banking, accounting, auditing, law, or information technology — the recognized disciplines have been expanded — or if they have at least two years of managerial experience in finance, banking, payment intermediation, or information technology. A computer science graduate with no banking management background can now lead a sandbox participant. So can a payment-industry veteran without a university degree.
The amendment also widens the recognized experience pool to include payment intermediary roles and technology management, alongside the original banking and finance categories. This means executives from Vietnam's large e-wallet and digital payment sector — who may not have held titles at banks or credit institutions — now count as qualifying.
Four other areas were modified. First, the State Bank of Vietnam (SBV) is now required to retrieve verification data — including academic credentials, judicial records, and business registration records — directly from Vietnam's national databases rather than requiring applicants to submit paper copies of documents the government already holds. Second, applications can now be filed via courier through a private logistics company or through an authorized representative acting under a power of attorney, not just by post, in person, or through official digital portals. Third, the requirement for a company's board or shareholders to formally adopt a resolution approving the application's core technology description document has been removed. Fourth, and practically significant for pre-commercial teams, an applicant can now demonstrate their proposed solution through either a simulation model or a working proof-of-concept prototype rather than a fully operational system.
What the amendment does not change is equally important. The sandbox still admits only Vietnamese-owned legal entities — foreign-invested enterprises remain excluded from fintech company status under the original decree's definitions. Cross-border supply remains prohibited. P2P lending is still restricted to Vietnamese đồng transactions. Borrower loan cap per platform is set at 100 million đồng (approximately $3,861 USD). Loan terms under P2P sandbox agreements cannot exceed two years. P2P sandbox participants must maintain real-time integration with Vietnam's National Credit Information Center (CIC). The SBV retains full authority to revoke participation certificates.
How Vietnam's Sandbox Mechanism Works
A regulatory sandbox, in fintech terms, is a controlled testing environment in which companies pilot financial products and services under direct regulatory supervision, with legal permission to operate and an agreement to generate data that helps the regulator eventually write permanent rules. The concept originated with the UK's Financial Conduct Authority in 2015 and spread rapidly through Asia — Singapore's Monetary Authority launched one of the region's most influential programs in 2016, followed by Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and the Philippines . Vietnam was a notable late arrival.
Under Vietnam's framework, a company applies to the SBV, which coordinates with other ministries and may conduct an on-site inspection before issuing a Certificate of Sandbox Participation. The certificate specifies the approved solution, the testing scope, the duration, and operating conditions. Testing must begin within 90 days of certificate issuance. Sandbox certificates are valid two years with two extensions — each capped at one year — for a maximum testing horizon of approximately four years.
Throughout the testing period, participants are required to file periodic reports and respond to SBV data requests. The authority can also require additional monitoring tools be put in place at any time. The results of sandbox testing are explicitly intended to inform a permanent legislative framework for fintech — each testing company's operational data is, in effect, regulatory evidence for Vietnam's policymakers.
Why P2P Lending Carries Stricter Controls Than Other Categories
Of the three categories eligible for sandbox testing, P2P lending operates under the tightest restrictions — and the history of the Vietnamese market explains why. Before the sandbox existed, around 100 P2P lending platforms operated in Vietnam with no dedicated legal framework. The SBV itself warned banks to avoid P2P partnerships and advised consumers to be cautious. Many platforms registered as consultancy firms to avoid financial regulation; the central bank documented cases of fraudulent capital mobilization, loan-sharking, and what it described as fertile ground for money laundering and personal data theft.
The sandbox's P2P requirements — real-time CIC integration, borrower debt caps, VND-only denominations, a prohibition on credit guarantees — are direct design responses to this documented harm landscape. The executive qualification rules that Decree 382 has now loosened were, in the original decree's logic, one more layer of protection: requiring a banking professional in leadership implied some institutional accountability. The amendment's shift to an either/or standard signals that the SBV believes the structural safeguards (CIC integration, borrower caps, ongoing monitoring) are the load-bearing consumer protections — not the executive's specific career path.
Vietnam's Broader Regulatory Reform Push
Trương Thanh Đức, director of Vietnam's ANVI law firm, has described the policy challenge precisely: "We cannot regulate fintech companies as we did to banks, or leave them unregulated. That's why we need a regulatory sandbox to test regulations."
The fintech sandbox is not operating in isolation. Over roughly fourteen months, Vietnam has assembled one of the most active digital technology regulatory stacks in Southeast Asia. The Law on Science, Technology and Innovation took effect October 1, 2025. The Digital Technology Industry Law took effect January 1, 2026 — Vietnam's first comprehensive framework covering AI, semiconductors, and digital assets. Vietnam's dedicated Vietnam AI Law took effect March 1, 2026, after passing the National Assembly in December 2025, placing sandbox mechanisms at the center of how Vietnam intends to govern emerging AI systems. A series of implementing decrees establishing Vietnam's International Financial Centers in Ho Chi Minh City and Danang were issued in late 2025.
Deputy Prime Minister Nguyễn Văn Thắng, who signed Decree 382 on October 6, 2026, has been active in Vietnam's technology governance agenda. He led Vietnam's delegation to the Vietnam Leadership Programme at Columbia University in August 2026, where discussions focused specifically on AI and innovation, and separately met Bybit CEO Ben Zhou to advance Vietnam's nascent cryptocurrency pilot market.
The fintech sandbox's amendment, in this context, is not a narrow technical fix. It is the first live iteration of a framework that Vietnam's government is watching closely. The results will shape permanent fintech legislation — and the loosened qualification rules are designed to ensure there are enough participants to generate meaningful data.
What the Amendment Means for the $19.8 Billion Market
Vietnam's Vietnam fintech market sizing report valued the sector at an estimated $19.8 billion in 2025, with projected compound annual growth of roughly 17 percent through 2032. Digital payments represent approximately 45 percent of fintech market activity. The three sandbox-eligible categories — P2P lending, credit scoring, and Open API data sharing — address the parts of the market most directly linked to Vietnam's financial inclusion goals.
Over 77 percent of Vietnamese adults now hold bank accounts, up from approximately 31 percent in 2014. The remaining underbanked rural population — concentrated in rural areas and among small-business owners — is the intended end beneficiary of the sandbox: if P2P platforms and alternative credit-scoring tools prove viable in testing, they give regulators the evidence to write permanent laws that allow those tools to reach borrowers the traditional banking system has not served.
Nguyễn Văn Thân, Vinasme chairman on SME funding gaps, has specifically called for a sandbox framework that allows alternative finance models to reach small businesses that have no viable funding channel outside traditional bank lending.
Whether the amendment produces a surge in applications will become apparent in the months ahead. The SBV retains full discretionary authority over how many companies receive certificates in any given period. But by narrowing the distance between a tech-founded startup's reality and the regulatory threshold for admission, Decree 382 makes the case for applying substantially easier to make internally — and that may be the first necessary condition for the sandbox to generate the data it was designed to produce.
Frequently Asked Questions
What fintech solutions can be tested in Vietnam's regulatory sandbox?
Three categories are eligible for testing under Decree 94 and its amendment: peer-to-peer (P2P) lending, credit scoring, and open banking data sharing through Open APIs. All testing is restricted to Vietnam's territory; cross-border supply is not permitted. Testing certificates are issued by the State Bank of Vietnam and are valid for up to two years, with the possibility of two one-year extensions.
How does the new "either/or" executive qualification rule actually change startup eligibility?
Under the original Decree 94, a fintech company's general director or legal representative was required to hold a qualifying university degree and have at least two years of management experience in finance or banking. Decree 382 replaces that with an alternative standard: the individual needs to satisfy either the degree requirement (now extended to include finance, banking, accounting, and auditing alongside the original economics, law, and information technology) or the experience requirement, which has been expanded to include payment intermediation and information technology management roles alongside banking and finance. A technically proficient founder who has run a payment-technology team for two years but lacks a traditional banking career can now qualify.
Can foreign companies enter Vietnam's fintech sandbox?
Not directly as fintech companies. Under the current framework, fintech company status requires that the company be Vietnamese-owned — no foreign investment capital. International firms that want sandbox access typically have two options: establish a separate Vietnamese legal entity, or form a partnership with a Vietnamese credit institution or fintech company already eligible to participate. This constraint was not changed by Decree 382.
Why is Vietnam moving so quickly to refine its fintech regulatory framework?
Vietnam's fintech sandbox is not merely a standalone innovation policy — it is explicitly positioned as a learning mechanism designed to generate the empirical data needed for permanent fintech legislation. The speed of Decree 382 (issued less than sixteen months after Decree 94 launched the sandbox) reflects structured feedback from prospective applicants about specific barriers, combined with Vietnam's broader ambition to establish itself as a regional digital finance hub. That ambition is backed by a sequence of major legislation: the Law on Digital Technology Industry (January 2026), the Law on Artificial Intelligence (March 2026), and International Financial Centers in Ho Chi Minh City and Danang — all coming into force within the same fourteen-month window as the sandbox itself. The amendment signals that the regulatory framework is designed to evolve in response to real-world friction, not sit static while the market waits for it.
