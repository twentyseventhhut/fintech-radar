# Amazon and Klarna adjust payments in Austria under EU rules

> [!info] 2026-10-06 · 1 упоминаний · 1 источника(ов) с текстом

Amazon is preparing another change for customers in Austria shortly after the introduction of the country’s parcel tax. In the coming weeks, the company will discontinue its monthly invoicing option, which allowed customers to combine several purchases and pay for them together at the end of the month.

 Customers who currently use monthly invoicing will instead be moved to standard invoice payments. Each order, including subscription orders, will receive a separate invoice, with customers given 14 days to make the payment. Amazon is already informing affected users about the change. 

The company said the decision is linked to new consumer credit rules taking effect on Nov. 20. The requirements also extend to payment arrangements involving smaller amounts, making the existing monthly invoicing model more difficult to maintain without adding complexity for customers.
Amazon said adapting monthly invoicing to the new requirements would have made the payment process more complicated. It therefore decided to switch customers to the traditional invoice model, which the company described as simpler and more transparent.
Standard invoice purchases will remain available
The change doesn’t mean Amazon is removing invoice payments altogether. Customers will still be able to receive their orders before paying, but each purchase will now be billed separately instead of being grouped into a single monthly statement.

 The payment deadline for each invoice will be 14 days. The same structure will apply to subscriptions, which will also generate individual invoices rather than being included in a monthly total. 

The shift follows implementation of the new EU Consumer Credit Directive, which introduces tighter requirements for deferred-payment products and other forms of consumer credit.
Stricter rules for buy now, pay later services
The regulatory changes extend beyond Amazon. Buy now, pay later services and smaller deferred-payment arrangements will face stricter requirements under the new rules.

 Providers will have to give customers more detailed information before an agreement is concluded, including information about costs, interest and withdrawal rights. The information must be provided free of charge, and customers must give explicit consent before entering into the agreement. 

The rules also introduce stronger requirements around creditworthiness checks. If a provider concludes from a customer’s financial situation that the agreed payments are unlikely to be affordable, the credit must not be granted.
The aim is to reduce the risk of consumers accumulating debt through several small financing arrangements.
Klarna also preparing for new requirements
Klarna is also preparing for the regulatory changes. The company said it has already implemented several requirements, including credit checks and greater transparency around costs and contractual conditions.
Klarna’s “Pay in 30 days” option has not been discontinued because of the new rules. The feature was temporarily unavailable at some retailers recently, but Klarna attributed that disruption to an internal technical issue and said it has since been resolved.

## Контекст

<!-- enrichment:context -->
### What actually changed (verified)

Amazon is discontinuing its **monthly consolidated invoicing** option in Austria — the feature that let customers combine several purchases and settle them with one end-of-month statement. Affected customers are being moved to **standard per-order invoices**, each with a **14-day payment deadline** (subscriptions also generate individual invoices). Amazon is *not* removing invoice-on-delivery payment altogether; it is only removing the monthly-bundling layer, which it says had become too complex to adapt under the new rules. (Source: beinsure.com, in-note.)

The trigger is the **EU Second Consumer Credit Directive (CCD2 — Directive (EU) 2023/2225)**, which replaces the 2008/2011 directive. Member states had to transpose it by **20 Nov 2025**; the rules **apply from 20 Nov 2026**. Austria's transposition is the **Verbraucherkreditrechts-Änderungsgesetz / Verbraucherkreditgesetz 2026** (passed ~21 May 2026), which replaces the 2010 act on 20 Nov 2026.

### Why monthly invoicing specifically gets hit

CCD2's headline change for BNPL/deferred payment: it **removes the small-ticket exemption** (the old ~€200 / short-duration carve-out) and pulls **deferred-payment arrangements of essentially any amount** into regulated consumer credit. That means:
- **Mandatory creditworthiness/affordability checks** before the agreement (credit must be refused if repayment looks unaffordable);
- **Pre-contractual disclosures free of charge** (costs, interest, withdrawal rights) and **explicit consent**;
- a **14-day withdrawal right** and APR-style disclosure.

Monthly consolidated invoicing is awkward under this regime because a rolling monthly tab across many small orders is hard to map to per-agreement disclosure/consent/affordability-check mechanics — hence Amazon's retreat to a simpler, clearly-bounded per-invoice (14-day) model that is easier to treat as (or keep outside) regulated credit.

### Klarna's posture

Klarna says it has **already implemented** credit checks and enhanced cost/contract transparency, so it is positioning the change as business-as-usual rather than a product withdrawal. Its **"Pay in 30 days"** was *not* discontinued because of CCD2; a recent temporary outage at some retailers was attributed to an internal technical issue, now resolved. (Note: this is Klarna's own framing — treat as a claim, not independent confirmation.)

### Internal comps (prior notes)

- [[Santander launches Zinia BNPL in Spain for Amazon purchases]] — the inverse move: a bank-backed BNPL *expanding* at Amazon checkout in another EU market (2025-10); shows Amazon's EU checkout-credit footprint that CCD2 now reshapes. See also [[Amazon Spain shoppers can defer purchases with Santander's Zinia]].
- [[New York DFS proposes oversight rules for buy now, pay later]] — parallel BNPL-under-credit-law tightening in the US (2026-03); CCD2 is the EU analogue.
- [[Klarna faces class action lawsuit in the Netherlands]] — EU consumer-protection pressure on Klarna's BNPL (2026-07), same regulatory direction of travel.
- [[Central Bank of Oman introduces BNPL regulation]] — global trend of BNPL being folded into formal credit regulation (2026-03).

### Broader rollout implication

This is the **first visible merchant-side product retrenchment** tied to CCD2 go-live, and it lands in Austria specifically because Austria's national law bites on the same 20 Nov 2026 date. The same directive is transposing across Germany, Netherlands, Sweden, etc. — so expect comparable checkout re-engineering (or feature pruning) from large merchants and BNPL providers EU-wide as the €200 exemption disappears and per-transaction credit checks become mandatory "from €1 up."

4. **€200 exemption removal** — is that specifically how Austria transposed it, or a general CCD2 feature? National implementations can retain limited carve-outs (e.g. interest-free, short-term thresholds) — did Austria?

6. **Conversion/abandonment impact.** Mandatory €1-up credit checks + 14-day withdrawal disclosures add checkout friction. Any data on cart-abandonment or approval-decline effects? (Not in sources — flag as unknown.)
7. **Scope of "subscriptions get individual invoices"** — does this affect Prime/Subscribe-&-Save billing mechanics, or only third-party-seller subscription orders?
8. **Does this reduce Amazon's own balance-sheet credit exposure**, or is monthly invoicing underwritten by a third party (e.g. a financing partner) whose role changes under CCD2?
9. **Why Austria first and not Germany** (Amazon's larger DACH market)? Is Germany's transposition on a different timeline or does Amazon handle DE invoicing differently?
10. **Is this a withdrawal or a reshaping?** Amazon keeps per-order invoices — so consumer deferred-payment access largely persists. Is the "adjustment" materially smaller than the regulation-driven framing suggests?
11. **Klarna "Pay in 30 days" outage** — Klarna blamed a technical glitch, not CCD2. Is that fully credible given the timing right before go-live, or is there regulatory de-risking being downplayed?
12. **Competitive effect.** Does CCD2 advantage licensed/credit-native players (Klarna, Santander/Zinia) over merchants running informal invoicing (Amazon)? Could this push Amazon toward a partner-BNPL model in the EU?
13. **Any enforcement teeth / penalties** specified in the Austrian act for non-compliant BNPL, and what's the supervisory authority (FMA)?
14. **Is the Nov 20 "consumer credit rules" the beinsure article references the same as CCD2**, or an unrelated Austrian consumer-credit amendment conflated in reporting? (Cross-check suggests same; legal sources name Verbraucherkreditgesetz 2026.)

### Importance: 3/5

Operationally concrete and a genuine regulatory inflection (first merchant-side CCD2 retrenchment, large brand), with clear EU-wide read-through as the directive goes live across member states. Capped below 4 because: it is one country (Austria), the actual consumer impact is modest (invoice payment persists, just un-bundled), the sourcing is thin/single-outlet, and Klarna's "nothing really changed" framing undercuts the drama. Signal value is more as a **bellwether for EU BNPL checkout re-engineering** than as a standalone market-moving event.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** BNPL / deferred-payment in the EU, viewed through the regulatory lens. The trigger is CCD2 (revised EU Consumer Credit Directive, Directive (EU) 2023/2225): transposed into national law by 20 Nov 2025, applicable/enforceable from **20 Nov 2026** — exactly the date Amazon cites for ending monthly invoicing in Austria (per beinsure, 2026-10-06). CCD2 pulls most BNPL and short-term/low-amount deferred-invoice products into consumer-credit law for the first time, ending the "grey area" that let them run with minimal oversight (per Oliver Wyman, Feb-2025; Signicat; HLC). Core new duties: mandatory documented creditworthiness checks (down to ~€1 of credit, per checkalle.de), pre-contract disclosure of costs/interest/withdrawal rights free of charge, and explicit consumer consent. Only a narrow carve-out survives (interest-free deferral ≤50 days with no external creditor), which is why Amazon is switching to per-order standard invoices with 14-day terms rather than adapting the monthly-pooled model. **Why now:** the compliance cliff is a hard date, not a trend — EU market size itself is still growing (Europe BNPL quoted at ~$406bn by 2031 / Germany BNPL ~$145bn by 2031 per ResearchandMarkets, 2026 — treat as third-party forecasts, not verified TAM).

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs: GMV (volume), take rate (revenue/GMV), and credit-loss rate (% of GMV). Key players in DACH/EU invoice-BNPL: Klarna, PayPal Pay Later, Riverty (Austria + 7 markets), RatePay, Scalapay, Alma, plus Amazon's in-house invoicing. Basis of competition: checkout conversion + merchant distribution + cost of credit/funding. Recent dated moves: PayPal extended BNPL installments in France citing the same CCD2 regime ([[PayPal expands BNPL with longer installments in France]], 2026-06); a Klarna Dutch class action landed 2026-07 ([[Klarna faces class action lawsuit in the Netherlands]]). Protagonist position: **Klarna is the EU incumbent and says it has already implemented CCD2 requirements (credit checks, cost/terms transparency)** and that its "Pay in 30 days" is NOT being discontinued (per note/beinsure). Moat = scale + banking license + merchant network; CCD2 is cited across sources as *favouring larger incumbents* (fixed per-check cost is trivial on a €1,000 basket but destroys margin on a €30 one, per analysts), so it is relatively more painful for sub-scale players — a structural tailwind for Klarna on share even as it is a headwind on economics.

**Comps & multiples.** IR-grounded protagonist numbers (PRIMARY evidence of EU exposure/regulatory stakes):
- Q1 2026 (release 2026-05-14): revenue $1.0bn (+44% YoY), GMV $33.7bn (+33%), transaction margin $389m, adj. operating profit $68m; **credit-loss provision 0.55% of GMV**. Non-US GMV $26.6bn = **~79% of group GMV outside the US**, i.e. Europe is the dominant base. Multiples (verifiable both-sides only):
- **Klarna (KLAR):** price ~$13.17, mkt cap ~$5.0bn (8 Oct 2026, investing.com/companiesmarketcap) vs FY26 revenue guide ~$4.08–4.16bn → **P/S ≈ $5.0bn / $4.1bn ≈ 1.2x**. Down from ~$15bn IPO debut ([[Klarna prices IPO at $40, debuts on NYSE near $15B]], 2025-09; now below $40 for all of 2026).

- Outlier flag: Klarna at **~1.2x P/S vs Affirm ~5.6x on similar ~$4bn revenue** looks *cheap* — but it is NOT a comparability like-for-like: Affirm is US-weighted (regulatory tailwind, not CCD2-exposed) and growing GMV faster (37% vs Klarna's cut guide), whereas Klarna's discount reflects the EU/German volume cut + CCD2 overhang. Qualitative comparison only (2 comps; distribution not computed). Scalapay/Riverty/RatePay private — no verifiable multiple, "no data".

**Risk flags.**
1. **Regulatory-cost + volume risk (concentrated on the EU base).** CCD2 forces per-transaction credit checks from ~€1 up: raises unit compliance cost and lengthens checkout, which analysts expect to compress small-ticket margin and dampen BNPL volume/availability. Second-order: Klarna's ~79% non-US GMV sits squarely in scope, and its single biggest market (Germany) is already the named reason for the FY GMV cut — regulatory friction stacks on top of soft German consumer demand.
2. **Market-concentration in Germany/DACH.** Klarna calls Germany "our largest market by volume"; the same CCD2 clock that forced Amazon's Austria change applies across DACH. One-market softness has already moved group guidance, so a CCD2 implementation drag there hits the group disproportionately.
3. **Disintermediation / self-provisioning by platforms.** Amazon is *replacing* a pooled-invoice (BNPL-like) product with its own 14-day standard invoice rather than routing to a licensed BNPL. If large merchants treat CCD2 as a reason to internalize short deferral under the ≤50-day carve-out, it shrinks the addressable BNPL checkout surface Klarna monetizes.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** `(analysis)` CCD2 is a share-vs-margin trade for EU BNPL: it likely *consolidates* volume toward licensed, scaled incumbents (Klarna, PayPal) while squeezing small-ticket economics and nudging merchants toward in-house deferral. Falsifiable thesis: if Klarna's post-20-Nov EU take rate holds and credit-loss stays ~0.5% while sub-scale rivals exit, the ~1.2x P/S is too cheap and re-rates. Trigger to watch: the Q4-2026 / FY release (first period reflecting live CCD2) — watch EU GMV, take rate and any merchant churn like Amazon's. Thesis breaks if EU volumes/take rate fall materially post-enforcement or more large merchants self-provision.

Revenue $1.042bn (+27% YoY; vs public consensus ~$993m → beat by ~$49m / ~+5%). GAAP EPS $0.01 vs consensus ~$(0.05) (beat). Driver: transaction-margin expansion + credit-loss improvement; net income turned positive. But FY2026 outlook was cut on weak German consumer spending — the thesis-relevant signal for this EU-regulatory story, since ~78% of GMV is ex-US (mostly Europe) and Germany is Klarna's single largest market by volume.

**Key figures (Q2 2026, reported, with growth).**
- Revenue: $1.042bn, +27% YoY.
- GMV: $36.6bn, +18% YoY (US $7.9bn, +27%, = 22% of GMV; ex-US $28.8bn, +15%, = ~78% of GMV — Europe is the bulk).
- Transaction margin dollars: $446m, +42% YoY; 42.8% of revenue (margin expanding faster than volume).
- Provision for credit losses: $192m, = 0.52% of GMV, improved from 0.56% of GMV in Q2 2025 (credit quality improving despite BNPL-loss-making narrative).
- Net income: $9m vs $(53)m loss in Q2 2025. Operating income: $27m vs $(46)m loss. Adjusted operating income: $91m, +214% YoY.
- Take-rate: ~2.85% (revenue ÷ GMV).
- Active consumers: 120m, +8% YoY. Merchants: 1.2m+, +54% YoY.

**By segment / driver.** Growth is margin-led, not volume-led: TMD +42% vs GMV +18%, so take-rate and unit economics drove the swing to profit, not headline volume. Ex-US (Europe-heavy) carries the business — transaction margin reached ~54% of revenue outside the US (mature markets near 60%) vs ~23% in the still-early US. Germany, the largest market by volume, decelerated (German retail sales <1% real growth in H1 2026), which is what capped the top line.

**vs expectations / prior period.** Beat: revenue $1.042bn vs public consensus ~$993.4m (+~5%); EPS $0.01 vs consensus ~$(0.05) (public consensus per Yahoo/Benzinga aggregation, as of the 2026-08-18 print). vs Q1 2026 (reported 2026-05-14): revenue $1.012bn → $1.042bn; GMV $33.7bn → $36.6bn; credit provision 0.55% → 0.52% of GMV; net income +$1m → +$9m — sequential profitability improving. YoY the story is a clean swing from loss to profit (net $(53)m → +$9m).

**Guidance / forward.** CUT. FY2026 GMV lowered to $149–151bn (from >$155bn at Q1); FY2026 revenue lowered to $4.08–4.16bn (from an implied >$4.34bn; vs public consensus ~$4.415bn → guidance misses the Street). Management attributed the cut to a "more measured view of primarily German volumes… consistent with reported trend across German retail" and did not assume a near-term recovery. Margin guidance was held/raised even as volume guidance fell — management is choosing profitability over growth. Tone: confident on profitability, cautious/defensive on European (German) demand.

**Thesis-flags (relevance to the Austria/EU-CCD story).**
1. EU demand, not EU regulation, is the near-term swing factor. The FY cut is demand-driven (German retail), not attributed to the new EU Consumer Credit Directive — Klarna did not quantify any CCD revenue/credit impact in the Q2 release. For a business ~78% ex-US / Europe-heavy, European macro is the dominant lever. Second-order: a Nov-20 tightening of BNPL/creditworthiness rules (the Austria story) lands on top of already-soft European volumes → incremental friction on the exact region that funds group profit.
2. Credit losses are improving (0.52% of GMV, down YoY), so the CCD's stricter affordability/creditworthiness checks hit a book that is already de-risking — the regulatory cost is more compliance/conversion friction than a loss-rate problem. Why it matters: the drag is on top-of-funnel approval/GMV, not on provisions.
3. Profit is margin-driven (TMD +42% vs GMV +18%). If EU regulation (mandatory pre-contract disclosures, explicit consent, harder credit checks) compresses conversion on low-ticket "Pay in 30 / monthly invoice" flows — exactly what Amazon is retiring in Austria — the volume base that the margin sits on could thin. Second-order: Klarna leaning into higher-margin ex-US/mature-market mix partly offsets this.
4. Guidance credibility: a cut two quarters into the year, with no assumed recovery, signals management sees European softness persisting — relevant for anyone modeling the CCD transition period through Nov-20 and into 2027.
