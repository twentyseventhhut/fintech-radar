KMCC to fine Google and Apple a combined 63.7 billion won over forced in-app payments


 
 The media regulator is set to dock the firms a total of $48.1 million for allegedly coercing developers to use their in-app payment systems, but the numbers pale in comparison to what the EU fined the tech giants for similar violations.
 
 
The Korea Media and Communications Commission (KMCC) is set to impose a combined 63.7 billion won ($48.1 million) in fines on Google and Apple for forcing app developers to use their in-app payment systems, three years after the regulator first announced its intention to penalize the two U.S. tech giants.
The KMCC will hold a plenary meeting Wednesday to approve corrective orders imposing fines of 42.9 billion won on Google and 20.8 billion won on Apple for violations of the Telecommunications Business Act, according to multiple sources on Monday.
The commission finalized the penalties during a closed-door meeting Monday after receiving a briefing from its administrative office on the proposed fines and the details of the corrective orders.

“There were no disagreements,” a KMCC official said.
In-app payment refers to a system that requires users to make purchases through Google’s or Apple’s payment systems rather than those operated by app developers.
The two companies have charged developers commissions of up to 30 percent, depending on their revenue, which brought complaints from developers that the fees were excessive.
In 2021, Korea became the first country in the world to pass legislation banning mandatory in-app payment systems through an amendment to the Telecommunications Business Act, widely known as the “anti-in-app payment law.” The amendment authorized the regulator to impose fines of up to 3 percent of an app marketplace operator's relevant revenue for violations.
Following the amendment, Google revised its policies to allow alternative payment methods that it had previously prohibited. However, critics argued that the change offered little practical relief because developers using external payment systems still faced additional costs, including fees charged by domestic payment gateway providers.
In October 2023, the Korea Communications Commission (KCC), the KMCC's predecessor, announced that it would impose fines of 47.5 billion won on Google and 20.5 billion won on Apple under the law.
The final decision was delayed for years, however, as the KCC faced prolonged operational disruptions, including a period when it operated with only two commissioners. The KCC was then dissolved in October last year.
“There was considerable deliberation over when to finalize the sanctions, particularly amid political upheaval following the change in administration and emerging trade tensions with the United States,” a source familiar with the matter said.
During the delay, the KCC recalculated the companies' relevant revenue and drew up a revised proposal in March last year to fine Google 42 billion won and Apple 21 billion won. It also summoned representatives from the companies' headquarters to hear their positions.
“I expect a decision to be made before the end of October,” KMCC Chairperson Kim Jong-cheol said during a parliamentary audit by the National Assembly's Science, ICT, Broadcasting and Communications Committee on Oct. 12.
Some experts have criticized the regulator for taking so long to reach a decision.
“Once illegal conduct is confirmed, corrective action or sanctions should follow promptly,” Lee Seong-yeob, a professor at Korea University's Graduate School of Management of Technology, said. “Delaying a decision over internal issues after spending years investigating and preparing sanctions will leave a blemish on the regulator's record.”
The relatively small size of the fines is also likely to draw scrutiny.
Last year, the European Commission fined Apple 500 million euros ($559 million), which was approximately 800 billion won at the time, for violating the Digital Markets Act through its in-app payment practices. In July this year, it also decided to impose a fine of 890 million euros on Google.
“Given the scale of these tech companies' revenue, the fines imposed in Korea are unlikely to deal a significant blow,” Choi Kyoung-jin, a professor at Gachon University's College of Law, said.
Experts also say the criteria used to calculate the fines could become a point of contention.
“There will also likely be disputes over how the fines are calculated, including whether they are based on revenue generated in Korea or at the companies' headquarters,” said another professor who requested anonymity.
The industry is also closely watching whether the sanctions could heighten trade tensions with the United States.
The concerns come amid an ongoing trade dispute following a fine of more than 600 billion won imposed on Delaware-based Coupang in June over a massive personal data breach, which drew objections from the U.S. government.
“Korea was the first country to introduce a ban on mandatory in-app payments, and U.S. companies are the first to face sanctions under the law, which raises concerns that another trade dispute could emerge,” Prof. Lee said. “The government also needs to fully explain the purpose and legal grounds for the sanctions.”

## Контекст

<!-- enrichment:context -->
**[0] What happened (one-paragraph fact base).** On 2026-10-28 (plenary meeting Wednesday), South Korea's Korea Media and Communications Commission (KMCC) — successor to the dissolved Korea Communications Commission (KCC) — moved to impose combined fines of ~63.7 billion won (~$48.1M) on Google (42.9bn won) and Apple (20.8bn won) for forcing app developers to use their proprietary in-app payment systems, a violation of the Telecommunications Business Act. The fines also carry corrective orders; a contested open question is whether those orders include an explicit "anti-steering" duty letting developers point users to cheaper external payment. The action lands three years after the KCC first announced (Oct 2023) its intent to fine the two (then ~47.5bn won Google / 20.5bn won Apple), a delay caused by the KCC operating with only two commissioners, its Oct-2025 dissolution, and political/trade turbulence. Source: koreajoongangdaily.com; corroborated by SBS, Nocutnews, Herald Business.

**[1] Why it matters / stakes.** Korea in 2021 became the FIRST country worldwide to legislate against mandatory in-app billing (the "anti-Google law," a Telecommunications Business Act amendment passed 180-0), capping penalties at 3% of relevant Korean revenue. This is the first enforcement outcome under that landmark law, so it sets the global template for how a direct statutory ban (vs. the EU's DMA or US private litigation) actually gets priced and enforced. The fine is small relative to the companies' revenue — experts (Choi Kyoung-jin, Gachon U.) expect it to deal no significant blow — which itself is the story: a first-mover law producing a modest sanction after a 3-year delay invites questions about deterrence. For fintech/payments, the real lever is the anti-steering corrective order and the 30% commission economics it threatens, not the headline won figure.

**[2] Key numbers & who's exposed.** Fines: Google 42.9bn won, Apple 20.8bn won, combined 63.7bn won (~$48.1M). Statutory cap: 3% of relevant Korean revenue. Commission rates at issue: up to 30%. Contrast with EU DMA: the European Commission fined Apple €500M (~$559M / ~800bn won) in 2025 and Google €890M in July 2026 (€460M self-preferencing + €430M Play Store anti-steering) — roughly 10-18x larger than Korea's. Prior Coupang precedent: >600bn won data-breach fine (June) already strained Korea-US trade ties. A disputed calculation question: whether "relevant revenue" is Korean-only or headquarters/global — the basis materially changes future exposure.

**[3] Prior art in the vault (cite).** This fits a widening global pattern of regulators attacking the Apple/Google app-store payment chokepoint: [[India antitrust threat puts Apple's in-app payments at risk]] (India's 2024 competition-law change enabling global-revenue-based penalties, a ~$38bn threat), [[Apple battles Australian banks over digital payments access]] (ACCC/bank fight over iPhone payment access), [[Brazil banks seek free access to iPhone NFC payments]] (CADE, NFC access), and the litigation/BNPL angle in [[Verdict expected soon in Klarna’s $8.3 billion antitrust lawsuit against Google]]. See also the merchant-side US outcome [[US judge dismisses antitrust suit against Apple, Visa, Mastercard]]. Korea is the regulatory bookend: the oldest statutory ban, now finally enforced.

**[4] What to watch next.** (a) Whether the KMCC plenary's corrective order includes the explicit anti-steering duty — the operative remedy, more important than the fine. (b) Appeals: both firms are near-certain to contest, especially the Korean-vs-global revenue basis for the calculation (flagged by experts as the main litigation vector). (c) Trade escalation: US House Republicans already criticized Korea's revised communications law; with the Coupang fine as backdrop, this could feed a Korea-US trade dispute. (d) Whether Korea's modest sanction nudges it toward the EU's larger-DMA-style penalties or stays symbolic. (e) Read-through to the parallel US track — Epic v Apple, where the 9th Circuit (Dec 2025) affirmed Apple's civil contempt over anti-steering and the matter is now before SCOTUS (Apple cert, 2026).

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector context — app-store payment-rail regulation goes global.** Korea's KMCC fine (combined 63.7bn won / ~$48.1m: 42.9bn on Google, 20.8bn on Apple) is the first enforcement anywhere under a dedicated anti-in-app-payment statute. Korea in 2021 became the first country to amend its Telecommunications Business Act to ban mandatory in-app billing, authorizing fines of up to 3% of an operator's relevant local revenue. The case is a symbol more than a financial event: the penalty is roughly an order of magnitude below Europe's. Under the Digital Markets Act (in force March 2024) the European Commission fined Apple €500m (~$559m) in April 2025 for anti-steering and in July 2026 moved to fine Google €890m — so Korea's ~$48m lands at ~10% of the EU's Apple figure despite covering the same conduct. The gap owes to the Korean law's narrow 3%-of-relevant-revenue cap and a years-long process (announced 2023, delayed through the KCC's dissolution and a change of administration).

**Commission / anti-steering economics.** The contested rail is the 30% standard in-app-purchase commission (tiered down for small developers and long-tenure subscriptions). The regulatory thrust worldwide is not to abolish the fee but to force open alternative payment methods and link-outs. Where that has happened, the gatekeepers have re-priced rather than surrendered margin: as of October 2026 Apple's unified EU schedule charges ~26% on Apple IAP, ~20% via an alternative processor and ~15% on link-outs, collapsing the earlier layered Core Technology Commission stack. Japan's Smartphone Software Competition Act pushed Apple to a 26% IAP rate (21% commission + 5% processing) from December 2025; in Korea, webshop/alternative checkout still carries a ~26% Apple fee versus 30% standard IAP. The economic point: "alternative payments" shave only a few hundred basis points because a residual commission (for access to the store/SDK) persists, and domestic payment-gateway fees eat much of the nominal saving — exactly the complaint Korean developers raised after Google's 2021 policy change offered "little practical relief."

**In-base comps (news DB).** This is a recurring, globally-syndicated theme in the vault: India's competition-law shift to global-turnover penalties exposed Apple to a reported ~$38bn maximum exposure (News/2025-11); Brazil's CADE is pressing Apple on iPhone NFC/payments access (News/2026-02); Apple is battling Australian banks over digital-payments access (News/2026-03); a US merchant antitrust suit against Apple/Visa/Mastercard over payment fees was dismissed (News/2025-07); and Klarna's ~$8.3bn antitrust claim against Google is pending (News/2026-02). Korea therefore sits in a widening ring of jurisdictions probing app-store and payment-rail gatekeeping, with penalty methodologies (local vs. global revenue base) emerging as the key swing variable in exposure.

**IR grounding — what's actually at stake for Apple.** App Store commissions sit inside Apple's Services segment, which is now the company's structural growth engine. Per Apple's filed results, FY2025 Services revenue surpassed $100bn for the year (up ~14%), reaching an all-time-record $28.8bn in the September 2025 quarter (up ~15% y/y) and representing ~28% of total company revenue; the App Store itself set an all-time record alongside Advertising, Cloud, Music, Payment Services and Video (Apple FY2025 Q4 8-K, SEC EDGAR a8-kex991q4202509272025.htm; see also 10-K filed 2025-10-31, drive_url https://drive.google.com/file/d/1Kkr5OOE_OMzBLosEyze6G9WPA5IGx2ZO/view). Against a $100bn+ annual Services base, a ~$21m Korean fine is immaterial — but the enforcement precedent and the forced opening of alternative/link-out payment rails is the real pressure on the high-margin App Store take rate that underpins Services growth. Google does not break out an equivalent line, but Play Store commissions sit in Google's advertising-adjacent platforms revenue and face the same re-pricing dynamic.

**Risk flags.**
1. **US–Korea trade friction.** US companies are the first targets under a Korea-first law; the fine lands amid an existing dispute (a >600bn won Coupang data-breach penalty drew US government objections) and risks escalating into a broader trade/Section-301-style flashpoint. This is a geopolitical, not a financial, risk.
2. **Fine-calculation contestability.** Experts flag likely litigation over whether "relevant revenue" is Korean or headquarters-level — the same global-vs-local base question that drives India's far larger theoretical exposure. The methodology, not the headline number, determines future severity.
3. **Enforcement credibility / deterrence gap.** A three-year delay and a penalty an order of magnitude below the EU's invite the criticism (voiced by Korean academics) that the sanction is too small and too late to deter — undercutting the law's signaling value.
4. **Re-pricing, not margin loss.** The EU/Japan playbook shows gatekeepers neutralize anti-steering rules by raising alternative-rail fees to ~20–26%; Korea's remedy may similarly fail to lower developers' all-in costs once domestic PG fees are added, limiting real economic relief.
5. **Appeal / payment-ecosystem spillover.** Both firms can litigate corrective orders, extending uncertainty; separately, forced opening of in-app rails feeds adjacent fights over NFC/wallet access (Brazil, Australia) that threaten Apple Pay/Payment Services economics more directly than the App Store fine itself.

## Earnings Review

<!-- enrichment:earnings_review -->
**Verdict (headline read).** Not an earnings print — a regulatory fine. Framed against Apple's App Store / Services line, the Korean penalty is IMMATERIAL. Apple's share of the combined 63.7bn won ($48.1M) fine is 20.8bn won (~$15.7M), a rounding error against Services revenue that set an all-time record of $30.98bn in the most recently reported quarter (Apple FY2026 Q2, ended Mar 28, 2026), up +16% YoY at a 76.7% gross margin. The fine equals roughly 0.05% of a single quarter's Services revenue (~0.01% annualized).

**Services revenue scale (filed figures).** Apple does not break out App Store in-app-payment revenue separately; it is reported inside the Services segment, whose drivers Apple names as advertising, the App Store and cloud services (Apple Q3 2025 Form 10-Q). Reported Services revenue, Apple FY2026 Q2 (quarter ended Mar 28, 2026): $30.98bn, +16% YoY — an all-time record, at 76.7% gross margin, with total company revenue of $111.2bn (+17% YoY) and diluted EPS of $2.01 (+22% YoY). Source: Apple FY26 Q2 earnings release / 8-K Exhibit 99.1.

**Materiality framing (the Korean fine vs Services).** The regulator (Korea Media and Communications Commission, successor to the KCC) is approving corrective orders fining Google 42.9bn won and Apple 20.8bn won for forcing developers onto in-app payment systems under the Telecommunications Business Act. Apple's ~$15.7M portion is negligible against ~$31bn/quarter (~$124bn+ annualized) of Services revenue; even the full combined $48.1M is ~0.15% of one quarter's Services line. The statute caps penalties at up to 3% of relevant in-Korea app-marketplace revenue, so the exposure is structurally bounded and small. For contrast cited in the news, the EU fined Apple €500M (~$559M) under the DMA for similar in-app-payment conduct — roughly 36x the Korean Apple fine — underlining how light the Korean sanction is.

**Thesis-flags (narrow).** (1) Precedent, not P&L: Korea is the first jurisdiction to enforce a mandatory-in-app-payment ban, and the fine's size is immaterial — the risk to the Services thesis is the regulatory template (EU DMA, U.S. storefront changes Apple flags in its 10-Q), not this cash outflow. (2) Take-rate pressure is the real watch-item: the up-to-30% commission that funds high-margin Services is being chipped at globally (alternative payment methods, anti-steering relief); Apple's 10-Q already discloses it "expects to make further business changes in the future" to how U.S. developers communicate alternative purchase options. The Korean 3%-of-revenue cap itself does not threaten the 76.7% Services gross margin; mandated commission/steering concessions, if they spread, would. (3) Trade-dispute overhang: the news flags U.S. government friction over Korean fines (cf. the Coupang data-breach penalty), a non-financial tail risk.

No full earnings angle beyond Services context — this is a fine, not a reporting event.
