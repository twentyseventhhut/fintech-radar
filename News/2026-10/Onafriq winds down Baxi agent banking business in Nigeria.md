# Onafriq winds down Baxi agent banking business in Nigeria

Fintech company, Onafriq, has announced plans to wind down its Baxi agent banking business in Nigeria, citing naira depreciation, rising operating costs and shrinking margins amid intensifying competition in the payment services market.

The decision affects the agent banking operation run by Baxi under Onafriq Nigeria Payments Limited, but does not amount to an exit from Nigeria, as the company will continue operating its cross-border payments network and card issuing services in the country.

In a statement shared with Nairametrics on Friday, October 16, 2026, the payments company said changes in the operating environment since 2024 had significantly altered the economics of its offline agent banking business, making the operation unsustainable under its existing model.

The development marks a strategic retreat from a segment of Nigeria’s financial services market that has helped extend access to financial transactions beyond traditional bank branches.

#### **Why Onafriq is winding down Baxi**

Onafriq attributed the decision to a combination of macroeconomic pressures and structural changes in Nigeria’s payments market.

The company said naira depreciation and inflation had affected transaction volumes, operating expenses and the sustainability of its offline agent banking model.

It also identified the rapid growth of instant account to account transfers as a factor changing the mix of transactions processed by agents, while increasing competition in the segment had compressed margins.

Dare Okoudjou, founder and chief executive officer of Onafriq, said the company entered Nigeria with strong conviction but had not achieved the outcome it intended.

*   “_We entered Nigeria with conviction and commitment, but did not achieve the outcome we set out for._
*   _“Closing this business was a difficult decision to arrive at, one that affects colleagues, agents, and business partners across Nigeria who poured their hearts and soul into building out the Baxi network,_” Okoudjou said.

Okoudjou said Nigeria remained central to the group’s mission, adding that Onafriq would continue serving the market through its payments network and card issuing services.

#### **Onafriq’s market entry**

Onafriq entered Nigeria’s agent banking market in 2021 through the acquisition of Baxi, also known as Capricorn Digital Limited. The company said it continued investing in the business through 2023 to expand and strengthen its agent network.

*   However, following a review of its long-term strategy and the business’s sustainability, the group concluded that winding down the operation was the appropriate decision.
*   Onafriq said agents onboarded through the Baxi platform had been transitioned to another principal bank.
*   It has also asked agents to transfer their funds to their preferred principal bank within 30 days, with its teams available to provide support during the transition.

The company added that employees whose roles are connected to the agent banking business would be consulted and supported, with a commitment to treating affected workers fairly.

#### **Corporate pullbacks from Nigeria**

Although Onafriq is not leaving Nigeria, its decision to discontinue Baxi’s agent banking operations comes against the backdrop of several international and domestic businesses that have scaled back or exited parts of the Nigerian market in recent years.

*   Just last week, Moove, the global mobility company founded in Lagos, announced plans to conclude its operations in Nigeriasix years after launching in the country.
*   As part of its exit, the company said it would transfer full ownership of eligible vehicles worth approximately N35 billion to existing customers at no cost.
*   Moove’s exit announcement came barely a month after e-hailing company Uber shut down its operations in Nigeria.

Uber said it left Nigeria to focus its investments on markets where it believes it can create the most value for drivers by providing earning opportunities at scale and enabling riders to travel seamlessly.

In recent times, international consumer goods and technology companies have reassessed their Nigerian operations amid currency volatility, inflation and difficulties repatriating earnings. Such decisions have included full exits, asset sales, changes in distribution arrangements and the discontinuation of specific product lines.

# Context-enrichment: Onafriq winds down Baxi agent banking business in Nigeria
_Analytical notes (not a post). Importance: 3/5._

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
On 2026-10-16 Onafriq (ex-MFS Africa) said it will **wind down the Baxi agent-banking business** run under Onafriq Nigeria Payments Limited. It is NOT an exit from Nigeria: cross-border payments and card issuing stay. Agents have been transitioned to "another principal bank" and have **30 days** to move their funds; affected staff "consulted and supported." Stated causes: naira depreciation + inflation (hurting volumes/opex), rise of instant **account-to-account (A2A)** transfers shifting the transaction mix away from agents, and compressed margins from competition. CEO Dare Okoudjou: "entered Nigeria with conviction… did not achieve the outcome we set out for."

**+ Why structured this way / what the PR hides.** The press frame is "macro made it unsustainable." But TechCabal (2026-10-19), citing Onafriq's UK-filed accounts approved 29 July 2026, reports Onafriq had a **non-binding memorandum of terms to SELL 100% of the agency business** to a prospective buyer — negotiations still open, no structure/price/timeline — months before the shutdown was announced. So the honest read: Onafriq first **tried to sell Baxi and failed (or couldn't close terms)**, then wound it down. "Difficult decision" PR masks a failed exit via sale. The 30-day fund-transfer window + migration to another principal bank is the orderly-shutdown mechanics of a regulated agent network, not a flourish.

## [1] Competitors / peers
Nigerian agent/super-agent banking is a **scale-and-density game dominated by three players**: OPay (~37% POS share, 1.5M banking agents, >$12B monthly volume claimed), Moniepoint (~20% share, ₦412T 2025 throughput, claims 8-of-10 in-person payments), PalmPay — plus Paga/Kuda. In Jan 2026 CBN upgraded OPay, Moniepoint, Kuda, PalmPay, Paga to **national microfinance licences**. Baxi (~90k+ agents at 2021 acquisition, >$1B processed in 2021) was a mid-tier super-agent that never reached that top-three density.

**+ Why the lay of the land / second order.** Two structural blows beyond macro: (1) **A2A transfers (NIP/instant transfer) disintermediate cash-in/cash-out agents** — the core Baxi value (moving cash) erodes as phones move money directly. (2) **CBN's exclusivity rule (effective 1 April 2026): a POS agent may serve only ONE principal.** That forces agents to consolidate onto the biggest, cheapest, most reliable network — a winner-take-most dynamic that squeezes sub-scale operators like Baxi out. So Baxi's problem is less "Nigeria is hard" and more "Baxi was never in the top tier and the rules + tech both now punish sub-scale." (analysis)

## [2] Company history / fit
Timeline: MFS Africa announced the Baxi (Capricorn Digital Ltd) acquisition **Oct 2021**, completed **~March 2022** after CBN approval — then the 2nd-largest Nigerian fintech acquisition at the time, giving a pan-African cross-border hub an **offline Nigerian cash on/off-ramp**. Rebrand MFS Africa → **Onafriq in 2022**. Continued investing through 2023. Now (2026) exits the agent line while keeping cross-border + card issuing.

**+ Why Onafriq acts this way.** Onafriq's identity is the **pan-African interoperability hub** (connecting mobile-money wallets/banks across ~40 countries). Baxi was a bet that owning physical distribution in Africa's biggest market would feed the hub. But offline agent banking is **capital- and ops-heavy, low-margin, hyper-local, and FX-exposed** — structurally off-strategy for an asset-light rails company. Onafriq's 2025–2026 bets have moved toward **cross-border + stablecoins** (see [[Onafriq stablecoins could reshape African payments]], [[Onafriq partners Conduit to scale stablecoin cross-border payments]]) and merchant rails ([[Yuno partners with Onafriq to unlock pan-African payments for merchants]]). Shedding Baxi is a **refocus on the rails/stablecoin layer** where its margin and multiple live. (analysis)

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
Not a product launch — a **retreat/write-down**. The newsworthy substance: a notable foreign-owned super-agent network **exiting** amid Nigeria's agent-banking shakeout, and the (under-reported) **failed-sale** backstory. Traction reality: Baxi had real scale at acquisition (90k+ agents, $1B+ 2021 volume) but evidently never converted it into durable profit under the new FX/competitive regime. No disclosed sale price, write-down figure, agent count at closure, or revenue — Onafriq is **silent on the economics and on the buyer's identity**.

**+ Why value destruction / who captures margin.** In cash-in/cash-out, margin accrues to **network density + float efficiency + fee scale** — i.e. OPay/Moniepoint. A sub-scale network pays the same regulatory/ops cost on thinner volume; A2A then shrinks the volume itself. So the "value-add" of owning offline distribution **commoditized and inverted**: it became a cost center. The second-order signal — even a well-capitalised pan-African player with a $1B-volume asset could not make Nigerian offline agent banking pay, and could not even sell it cleanly. (analysis)

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
Onafriq stays in Nigeria via cross-border + card issuing; expect it to lean harder into stablecoin/cross-border rails. Baxi agents migrate to another principal (consolidation into survivors). The event sits in a **broader Nigeria-pullback narrative** the source stacks alongside it: Moove winding down Nigeria ops (~N35B of vehicles transferred to customers), Uber shutting Nigeria — FX volatility, inflation, profit-repatriation friction driving exits/retrenchments.

**+ Why the market goes this way / counterintuitive.** The CBN exclusivity rule + A2A rise + national-MFB licences for the top five together **accelerate consolidation** to 3–4 mega-networks. Counterintuitive second-order: this can look like "de-risking" but it **concentrates systemic and pricing power** in OPay/Moniepoint/PalmPay, reducing agent choice and competitive pressure on fees — fragility and pricing risk, not just efficiency. For Onafriq, exiting a loss-making, off-strategy line is **net positive for the equity story** even though framed as a loss. Risk: signals that Onafriq's physical-distribution thesis failed, denting the "own the full stack in Africa" narrative. (analysis/hypothesis)

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** Nigeria's PoS/agent-banking rails are large but margin-thin: ~8.36m registered PoS terminals (~5.90m active) as of Mar-2025, with Q1-2025 PoS transactions a record ₦10.51tn (~$7.15bn), +301.67% YoY (per NIBSS data, via Nigeria fintech coverage, as of Mar-2025). Structure is consolidating fast around three principals — Moniepoint, OPay, PalmPay — after the CBN Oct-2025 rule barring agents from holding terminals of multiple principals, forcing exclusivity from 1-Apr-2026 (per TechCabal, 7-Oct-2025). Why now: (1) naira depreciation + inflation since 2024 have inverted the unit economics of offline cash-in/cash-out agency, where revenue is a thin per-transaction fee but FX-exposed hardware, logistics and float costs balloon; (2) the shift to free/cheap instant account-to-account transfers (NIBSS Instant Payments) is structurally eroding the cash-handling volume agents monetize — a secular disintermediation of the agent layer itself, not a cyclical dip. Barriers in agency banking are low (commodity hardware, price competition), so scale/float economics decide survival — favoring the mega-principals and squeezing sub-scale entrants like Baxi.

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs: terminals deployed, active-agent count, transaction value processed, per-transaction take/margin. Key players and basis of competition (distribution + price, not product): Moniepoint — claims ~1m+ terminals and ~80% of PoS activity, ~₦412tn processed in 2025 (self-reported, via industry review 2026); OPay — ~60m+ users, exploring a US IPO near $4bn (filed 2026, see [[OPay files for US IPO as revenue more than doubles]], [[OPay hires banks for US IPO at about $4 billion valuation]]); PalmPay — reportedly raising above a $1bn valuation. The CBN exclusivity rule is actively redistributing agents: OPay has been bleeding PoS agents to Moniepoint as the deadline nears ([[OPay loses Nigeria PoS agents to Moniepoint amid CBN deadline]], 16-Mar-2026). Onafriq's position in agent banking: niche/sub-scale and now exiting — it bought Baxi (Capricorn Digital) in 2021, invested through 2023, and is transitioning agents to another principal. Onafriq's actual moat is elsewhere: a pan-African cross-border network (500m+ mobile-money wallets, 200m+ bank accounts across 30+ countries; stablecoin/PAPSS rails — see [[Onafriq partners Conduit to scale stablecoin cross-border payments]], [[Onafriq and PAPSS to launch cross-border payments from Ghana]], [[Yuno partners with Onafriq to unlock pan-African payments for merchants]]). It is retreating from a business where it never had scale to defend the one where it does (analysis).

**Comps & multiples.** This is a business-closure, not a deal — no transaction multiple to compute; "no data" on Baxi's revenue/valuation (never disclosed). For scale context on the protagonist: Onafriq's last disclosed round was a Series C of ~$100m (2021) extended to ~$200m (2022), with a reported ~$350m valuation in Jun-2022 (round valuation, NOT market cap; private, dated, likely stale given the 2024+ naira repricing) (per FT Partners / CB Insights). Internal comps on the same consolidation dynamic: [[OPay loses Nigeria PoS agents to Moniepoint amid CBN deadline]], [[OPay files for US IPO as revenue more than doubles]], [[Opera values OPay at around $3.1 billion]], [[Moniepoint raises $90M to complete $200M Series C]]; FX-driven foreign-unit losses as a read-across to naira pressure on offline economics: [[Flutterwave UK reports £2.27 million loss in 2024]]. Distribution not computed (no comparable disclosed agent-banking multiples); qualitative comparison only.

**Risk flags.** (1) Structural disintermediation — instant A2A transfers eroding the cash-in/cash-out volume the agent model monetizes; this is the deeper threat and it hits every sub-scale operator, so Baxi's exit is a leading indicator, not an idiosyncratic stumble. (2) FX/macro concentration — a model whose costs are FX-linked while revenue is naira per-transaction fees is structurally short the naira; further depreciation keeps repricing the whole segment's economics, not just Baxi's. (3) Regulatory tailwind for the big three, headwind for the rest — CBN exclusivity from Apr-2026 hard-wires winner-take-most dynamics; capital-light challengers get starved of agents, so expect more quiet wind-downs/consolidation.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** (analysis) A scale-player exiting offline agency is a signal the segment is past peak for sub-scale operators: the value is migrating up-stack to A2A/instant rails and to the few principals with float scale and FX buffers. Falsifiable thesis: over the next 12 months expect further exits/asset sales by non-top-3 agent-banking operators as Apr-2026 exclusivity bites — watch for whether any mid-tier principal retains its agent base post-deadline (if a #4/#5 holds share, the consolidation thesis is wrong). Onafriq's own tell: doubling down on cross-border/stablecoin/card issuing is where it thinks the durable margin is.
