# Coast raises $35M to track employee spending on company cards

[Connecting the Dots in Fintech] 🇺🇸 New York startup Coast raises $35 million to track employee spending on company cards. Thomvest Ventures led the growth equity round, which will expand spend controls, vendor payments, and automated accounting.

## Первоисточники

### fortune.com
<https://fortune.com/2026/10/22/fintech-startup-coast-35-million-track-workers-company-credit-cards>
*631 слов · direct*

Getting the job done means trusting workers to spend company money—and keeping track of where it goes. In industries like plumbing and construction , employees often carry company cards to buy fuel and supplies, while office staff sort through receipts to check that spending stays within bounds. Coast, a New York-based startup, wants to make this process more transparent by pairing company payment cards with software that helps businesses control spending and track purchases made on the job. 
On Thursday, the company, founded in 2020 by former Wall Street engineer Daniel Simon, announced a $35 million growth equity round that closed in October. It plans to use the funding to build out a platform that helps businesses manage employee spending, facilitate payments to vendors, and automate accounting tasks. 
“If you run a plumbing business or an HVAC… and you need to give the technician who works for you the company credit card when they take the van out to go to their job… you need really specialized control, security, visibility, [and] reporting,” Simon, who serves as Coast’s CEO, told Fortune .
It works like this: A worker picks up a company card and checks in by text or app. That links the card to their identity and the spending limits their employer has set. Coast can then use vehicle location data to check whether the company van is at the gas station where the card is used—and block the purchase if it isn’t. Once a purchase goes through, the software sends the transaction details to the company’s accounting system, so office staff have fewer records to enter and match by hand.
Thomvest Ventures led the growth equity round, joined by new investors Intuit Ventures and Small World Capital. Returning backers included ICONIQ, Accel, Insight Partners, Better Tomorrow Ventures, Avid Ventures, Vintage Investment Partners, Synchrony , RXR, and The Fintech Fund. The funding follows Coast’s Series B, which raised $40 million, and an earlier $92 million funding round. Coast declined to disclose its latest valuation.
 The workers behind the AI boom 
Though artificial intelligence promises to automate office work, its growth depends on workers who build data centers and upgrade power grids. U.S. demand for skilled trades is growing three times faster than for desk jobs, with openings for heating, ventilation, and air conditioning technicians up 78% and broader trade postings up 30%, according to a March report by staffing company Randstad USA. Its research also found that 102 workers leave the trades for every 100 who enter, putting pressure on businesses to do more with fewer people. Coast sees an opportunity to sell payment tools to these businesses in a market it estimates at $120 billion.
“You are taking time that might have otherwise been spent [on] back office work and giving that back to the people in the trades to work on what they are really focused on,” Don Butler, managing director at Thomvest Ventures, told Fortune .
Simon first saw an opportunity to tap into this market during the height of the pandemic, shortly after he sold his previous payments startup, Bread, for $500 million in 2020. The sale marked the latest milestone in a career that began on Wall Street, where Simon built trading software for investment banks and hedge funds before earning law and business degrees from Yale University.
But while banks and tech companies used modern software, he saw plumbers, electricians, and heating and cooling technicians rely on payment tools designed before smartphones existed.
Today, Coast has about 135 employees across its New York headquarters and a Utah office, and serves thousands of businesses, including Tristate Plumbing, Texas Solar Outfitters, and Verde Property Services. The company is not yet profitable and says it prioritizes growth, with revenue reinvested in its products and efforts to attract customers.

# Context-enrichment: Coast raises $35M to track employee spending on company cards
_Analytical notes (not a post). Importance: 2/5._

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
Coast (NYC, founded 2020 by ex-Bread CEO Daniel Simon) closed a **$35M growth-equity round in October 2026**, led by **Thomvest Ventures**, with new investors **Intuit Ventures, Small World Capital and Alpha Square Group**, and returning backers ICONIQ, Accel, Insight Partners, Better Tomorrow Ventures, Avid Ventures, Vintage Investment Partners, Colle Capital, Synchrony, RXR and The Fintech Fund. **Valuation was not disclosed** — a tell. This follows a $6M seed, a $27.5M Series A, and a **$40M Series B (July 2024, ICONIQ-led)**; total equity now **~$226M**.
- **Why "growth equity," no valuation, and a down-sized cheque ($35M < the prior $40M Series B)?** In a 2026 market where Ramp is at $32B and Navan has IPO'd, a smaller, label-shifted round with a withheld mark most likely signals a **flat-to-soft valuation** the company would rather not print. "Growth equity" framing dresses up what is effectively a bridge/extension to keep scaling an unprofitable business. (analysis)
- **The actual product (de-PR'd):** payment cards + software for the *physical/trades economy* (plumbing, HVAC, construction, transport). The differentiated bit is **fraud control via telematics**: a worker "checks in" to link a card to identity + employer-set limits, and Coast cross-references **vehicle-location data** against the merchant (is the van actually at that gas station?) to block off-site misuse. Everything else — vendor payments, AP, "automated accounting," "AI-powered expense automation" — is **announced roadmap**, not shipped-and-adopted capability.
- **Traction given (real):** ~135 employees (NY HQ + Utah), "thousands of businesses" (named: Tristate Plumbing, Texas Solar Outfitters, Verde Property Services). **Not profitable**; reinvesting revenue into growth. No GMV, no card-spend volume, no revenue figure disclosed — the metrics that would prove the model are absent.

## [1] Competitors / peers
Two overlapping battlefields, and Coast sits awkwardly between them:
- **Legacy fleet/fuel cards (its core):** WEX (~21% trucking share, accepted at ~95% of US fuel stations, launched a unified fuel+EV-charging card Jan 2026), **Corpay/FleetCor** (Allstar, Keyfuels, Fuelcard Company brands — see [[Corpay names Matt Hanks VP marketing for UK Fleet]]), EFS/Comdata (~23%/17% trucking), and U.S. Bank. These are entrenched, acceptance-heavy incumbents. Commercial fleet-fuel-card market ~$13B (2026).
- **Modern spend-management (where it is migrating):** Ramp, Brex, Navan — a market the corpus itself calls crowded ([[This Week in Fintech Crowded corporate card and spend market]]). Ramp is at $32B and $1B+ ARR ([[Ramp in talks to raise $750 million at over $40 billion valuation]], [[Ramp pushes AI agents across its expense management tools]]); Navan IPO'd at $6.2B ([[Navan raises ~$920M in IPO at $6.2B valuation]]); Brex was bought by Capital One ([[Brex was acquired by Capital One for $5.15bn in the largest bank-fintech]]).
- **Coast's position:** *catching up* on both axes but with a **defensible wedge** — it targets SMB trades/field-service, a segment Brex abandoned (2022) and that Ramp/Navan serve only loosely (their mix skews mid-market/enterprise). **+ Why this gap exists and the second-order dynamic:** the horizontal spend-management players optimize for office/SaaS spend; the physical-economy use case (vans, fuel, job-site supplies, telematics-linked controls) is a genuinely different product surface. Coast's moat is **vertical specialization + telematics fraud data**, not scale. The risk: it is too small to win the incumbents' acceptance game and too niche to matter to the Ramp/Brex giants — until one of them decides the trades vertical is worth a feature or an acquisition.

## [2] Company history / fit
Simon sold Bread (BNPL/checkout) for ~$500M to Alliance Data in 2020, then built Coast on the insight that trades businesses still run on **pre-smartphone payment tools**. Trajectory: 2020 founded → $6M seed → $27.5M Series A → $40M Series B (Jul 2024) → $35M growth equity (Oct 2026). **+ Why the company acts this way:** fuel/fleet cards are a **commodity interchange/rebate business** with thin, incumbent-dominated economics. To escape the take-rate trap, Coast must bolt **software (spend controls, AP, accounting automation)** onto the card to earn a SaaS-style multiple and lock-in — the exact "commodity rails → needs a software multiple" pressure that drove Ramp/Brex. The Oct-2026 round's use-of-funds (AP, accounting, fleet/field-service integrations, AI) is this software-margin pivot made explicit.

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
- **Genuinely new:** telematics-gated card authorization (block a purchase if the vehicle isn't at the merchant). That is a concrete anti-fraud/anti-leakage mechanism incumbents' legacy fuel cards don't natively do — a real value-add for fleet operators where fuel/card fraud is a chronic cost.
- **Not new / PR:** "AI-powered expense automation," vendor payments, AP, automated accounting — these are **table stakes** Ramp/Brex/BILL already ship at scale ([[BILL launches AI agents to automate accounts payable]]). Framing them as the funding thesis is catch-up, not novelty.
- **Traction is thin/undisclosed:** "thousands of businesses" and three logos, no spend volume, no revenue, no retention, admittedly unprofitable. **+ Who captures the margin:** today Coast earns on **interchange/fuel rebates** (commodity, shared with networks/issuers — Synchrony is both investor and likely issuing partner). The durable margin would come from **software subscriptions + the telematics-data moat**, but that is unproven. The $120B TAM is a **top-down estimate of the physical-economy spend pool**, not Coast's serviceable market — classic PR anchoring to flatter a small round.

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
Plan: expand from fuel/fleet cards into a full **financial operating system for the trades** (corporate cards, AP/bill pay, accounting automation, integrations with fleet/field-service platforms). Tailwind cited: skilled-trades demand growing ~3x desk jobs (Randstad), HVAC openings +78%. **+ Why the market may or may not go this way:** the structural bet — that the physical economy is an underserved fintech vertical — is sound, and strategic investors (Intuit, Synchrony) suggest distribution/ERP and issuing angles. **Counterintuitive second-order risk:** a withheld valuation + sub-prior-round cheque in a frothy 2026 spend-management market implies **investor caution**, not momentum; the trades wedge that keeps Coast safe from Ramp/Brex also caps its TAM and makes it a plausible **tuck-in acquisition** (cf. Brex→Capital One, OakNorth→Monite) rather than a standalone compounder. The AI/automation story is the weakest part — it competes head-on with players 100x its scale.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** Coast sits at the intersection of two markets: commercial **fleet/fuel cards** and broader **spend management**. Coast estimates its serviceable market at ~$120bn (per Fortune, as of 2026-10-22) — a founder/VC-supplied TAM for payment tools sold to trade/service businesses, not an independently audited figure, treat with skepticism. The underlying fleet-card market is real and growing: the commercial fleet *fuel* card market was ~$13.3bn in 2026, forecast ~$18.2bn by 2030, ~8.6% CAGR (per The Business Research Company, via search, 2026); a broader "fleet card" definition puts 2025 at ~$45bn growing ~8% (per Dataintelo, via search). Structure is a **barbell**: the fleet/fuel-card end is consolidated around two scaled incumbents — Corpay (ex-FleetCor) with $4.53bn net revenue in 2025, ~47% from vehicle payments, and WEX with $2.66bn FY2025 revenue, ~52% from its Mobility/fleet segment (per search, 2025) — while the software-first spend-management end (Ramp, Brex) is a fast-growing disruptor layer. Entry barriers: card-network/issuing relationships, fraud/underwriting, merchant acceptance (fuel is a specialized MCC), and distribution into blue-collar verticals. Why now: demand for skilled trades growing ~3x faster than desk jobs, HVAC openings +78% (per Randstad report cited by Fortune, March 2026) — labor scarcity pushes trade businesses to automate back-office, Coast's wedge.

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs: purchase/spend volume (interchange base), take rate on interchange, SaaS attach/software ARR, card decline/fraud rate. Economics are **interchange-led** — Ramp reportedly earns ~70% of revenue from card interchange (per ValueAddVC, via search), the same primary engine Coast rides; software fees are secondary. Key players by layer: (a) fleet-card incumbents Corpay, WEX — scale, acceptance, telematics integrations; (b) horizontal spend-management Ramp (>$2bn annualized revenue, >$200bn annualized purchase volume, per search 2026) and Brex (acquired by Capital One for $5.15bn, closed April 2026 — per search), now with a bank balance sheet behind it; (c) adjacent AP/accounting automation (BILL, in-base [[BILL launches AI agents to automate accounts payable]]; [[Ramp adds AI agents to Bill Pay platform]]). Basis of competition: **vertical depth vs horizontal scale**. Coast's position: **niche specialist** — vehicle-location/telematics-gated card controls (block a fuel purchase if the van isn't at the station), purpose-built for trades. Moat `(analysis)`: switching costs via embedded telematics/accounting integrations + vertical data, but NOT scale — it is an order of magnitude smaller than Corpay/WEX on the fuel side and Ramp/Brex on software. Coast expanding fuel-card → corporate card + Bill Pay/AP puts it on a collision course with Ramp/Brex downstream.

**Comps & multiples.** Private, valuation undisclosed — Coast declined to disclose (per Fortune). $35M growth round; total raised just under $226M (per Finovate/search; note text says "$40M Series B + earlier $92M"). No revenue disclosed ("not yet profitable, prioritizes growth"), so **Coast's own multiple = no data / [UNSOURCED]**.
- **External peers:** Ramp ~$44bn valuation vs ~$1.5bn annualized revenue ≈ `$44bn / $1.5bn ≈ 29x` revenue (round valuation, not market cap; per search 2026) — richer tier but justified only by ~100% growth and interchange scale. Brex acquired at $5.15bn (per search). Corpay/WEX are public and trade on EBITDA, not growth multiples — a different, mature cohort; EV/EBITDA not computed here (no clean free source, [UNSOURCED]).
- **Internal comps** (in-base, spend-management/expense): [[Navan raises ~$920M in IPO at $6.2B valuation]] — $6.2bn IPO cap on $613M LTM revenue (up 32%, $188M loss) ≈ `$6.2bn / $613M ≈ 10.1x` revenue, a sobering public re-rating (~30% below its 2022 $9.2bn private mark); [[DualEntry raises $90 million Series A for AI ERP]]; [[Campfire raises $65 million Series B for AI ERP]]; [[Qashio acquires Saudi expense platform Sanad Cash]]. Distribution not computed (Coast's own figures absent); qualitative read: **$35M is a modest growth round, not a mega-round** — consistent with a sub-unicorn niche player, well below the Ramp/Brex tier.

**Risk flags.**
1. **Disintermediation from above.** Horizontal spend-management (Ramp/Brex, now Capital-One-backed) and incumbents (Corpay/WEX) can bolt vertical controls onto far larger distribution; Coast's telematics-gating is a feature, not an unassailable moat — second-order: its differentiation could be commoditized.
2. **Interchange dependence / rail risk.** Like Ramp, economics lean on interchange earned via an issuing-bank partner — exposed to interchange-rate pressure, regulation, and partner-bank terms; not disclosed by Coast ("who's silent about economics").
3. **Vertical/customer concentration & cycle sensitivity.** Narrow blue-collar trades focus (construction, HVAC, oil & gas) concentrates exposure to construction/energy cycles; fuel spend is commodity-price-linked, making top-line volatile.
4. **Opacity.** Undisclosed valuation + undisclosed revenue + "not profitable" — can't verify the $120bn TAM claim or capital efficiency; the modest round size may itself signal a cautious market.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** `(analysis)` Coast is a **vertical-wedge bet in a consolidated market** — the thesis is that trades businesses are underserved enough that specialized controls + embedded telematics create durable switching costs before horizontal players reach down. Falsifiable: if Ramp/Brex or Corpay/WEX ship credible telematics-gated fuel controls into the trades segment within ~12–18 months (watch for such launches), Coast's edge erodes and it becomes an acquisition target rather than a category leader. Trigger to watch: next round size/valuation disclosure and whether Bill Pay/AP gains traction vs. the core fuel card.
