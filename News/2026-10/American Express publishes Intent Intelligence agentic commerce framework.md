# Context-enrichment: American Express publishes Intent Intelligence agentic commerce framework
_Analytical notes (not a post). Importance: 3/5._

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
On 13 Oct 2026 Amex **published** (made publicly available) its "Intent Intelligence" framework — a documented, recommended approach for capturing, authenticating and preserving a cardholder's *purchase intent* before an AI agent transacts. This is a **spec/framework, not a new live product**: it is the newly-broken-out "intent" component of the **Amex Agentic Commerce Experiences (ACE) Developer Kit** that Amex already launched on **14 Apr 2026** (the ACE kit bundles five services — agent registration, account enablement, intent intelligence, payment credentials, cart context — plus "Amex Agent Purchase Protection"). So the 13 Oct item is Amex *documenting and opening up* one existing ACE pillar and pitching it as an industry-wide pattern — not a from-scratch launch.

Four stated elements: (1) create/confirm intents; (2) establish proof of intent; (3) secure the payment credential to use; (4) apply intent ID + proof to the purchase flow. Mechanically: a **Create Intent API** captures the instruction + constraints (merchant name, max budget, product preferences); an **Intent ID** labels the record; a **Proof of Intent Token** carries the constraints and is used to mint a **restricted payment credential**. The distinctive design choice Amex emphasises: make the authorised intent available **to the issuer earlier in the lifecycle — before the agent executes** — so intent acts as a control through the whole transaction, not just a checkout-time check.

**Why structured this way / what it reveals:** Amex is a **closed-loop** network (issuer + acquirer + network in one). That lets it push intent-as-control *upstream to the issuer* — something open-loop schemes (Visa/Mastercard) structurally can't do as cleanly because issuer and acquirer are separate parties. So "intent visible to the issuer early" is not marketing fluff; it is Amex playing its one real structural advantage. The flip side (the PR is silent on it): closed-loop = small merchant/cardholder base, so Amex *needs* to position Intent Intelligence as an open "framework/standard" and lean on standards bodies, because it cannot win on footprint. Note the vagueness markers: "recommended approach", "aims to help advance a common industry approach", "in talks with payments partners" — i.e. **named production partners/merchants are not disclosed**.

## [1] Competitors / peers
The issuer/network cohort has moved fast, all 2025–2026:
- **Visa — Trusted Agent Protocol / Intelligent Commerce** (Oct 2025, [[Visa launches Trusted Agent Protocol for AI commerce]]): extends Visa Token Service with agent credentials + a signed-intent payload; agent presents a verifiable credential, merchant validates via Visa's directory.
- **Mastercard — Agent Pay + Verifiable Intent** (Verifiable Intent co-built with Google, Mar 2026, [[Mastercard launches Verifiable Intent trust layer with Google]]): new "Agentic Token" class bound at provisioning to an agent identity + cardholder mandates (spend ceilings, MCCs, time windows); auth carries the token + agent flag. First live agentic payment reported mid-2026 ([[PhotonPay completes first live agentic payment with Mastercard]]).
- **Google AP2 / OpenAI-Stripe ACP / x402** — the protocol layer ([[Fintech Wrap Up ACP vs AP2 agentic payments comparison]], [[PayPal adopts Agentic Commerce Protocol, expands in ChatGPT]]): AP2 uses user-wallet verifiable credentials + mandates; ACP is the ChatGPT-checkout rail; x402 is internet-native crypto payments. Both Visa and Mastercard joined AP2 (Sep 2025).

**Amex's position: catching up, differentiated on angle.** Amex is 6–12 months behind Visa/Mastercard on the first move (ACE = Apr 2026 vs Visa Oct 2025). But its angle — **issuer-side proof of intent, pushed early in the lifecycle, over a closed loop** — is genuinely distinct from token-at-provisioning (Mastercard) and signed-intent-at-acceptance (Visa). **Why the landscape looks this way (2nd order):** everyone is racing to be the *trust/authorisation* layer because the agent and the checkout UX are being captured by OpenAI/Google/Perplexity — the networks' defensive move is to own "did the human really authorise this, and who eats the fraud". Amex, lacking scale, is explicitly trying to convert its spec into a **standard** (chairs the EMVCo agentic-commerce working group; engaging x402 Foundation) so that even non-Amex volume references its intent model — a standards-capture play, not a volume play.

## [2] Company history / fit
Amex has sequenced this deliberately through 2026: **Apr** — ACE Developer Kit + industry-first Agent Purchase Protection; **May** — agreed to acquire agentic-expense startup **Hyper** (OpenAI-CEO-backed) to fold AI automation into a planned expense platform ([[American Express to acquire agentic expense startup Hyper]]); **Oct** — publishes/opens the Intent Intelligence pillar and pushes it toward standards bodies. This fits logically: Amex monetises premium spend and merchant fees, so if agents intermediate purchasing, Amex must stay the *paying/authorising* party, not get demoted to a dumb credential behind an agent. **Why Amex acts this way:** its moat is a premium closed-loop network with high fees; agentic commerce threatens to commoditise the card into a token an agent calls. Documenting intent + offering purchase protection + chairing EMVCo is Amex defending its right to sit at the authorisation/fraud-liability chokepoint — the part of the stack that justifies its economics.

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
**Genuinely newish:** the *issuer-side, pre-execution* proof-of-intent model (intent as an issuer-held control before the agent buys) is a real design delta vs Visa (acceptance-flow signed intent) and Mastercard (provisioning-time token). The Proof-of-Intent-Token → restricted-credential chain is concrete, not hand-waving.
**But traction is thin — this is the anti-PR gate:** it is a **published framework, not demonstrated adoption**. No named merchants, no transaction/GMV numbers, "in talks with payments partners". The underlying ACE kit is ~6 months old with no disclosed live volume either. Contrast: Mastercard already has a *live* agentic payment on record (PhotonPay). **Who captures the margin (2nd order):** value accrues to whoever owns the authorisation + fraud-liability decision. Amex's closed loop lets it own that end-to-end for *its own* cards — strong defensively. But the open-loop rails (Visa/Mastercard) and the agent platforms (OpenAI/Google) set the *cross-network* standard; if EMVCo/x402 standardise on a Visa/Mastercard-flavoured model, Amex's framework becomes a proprietary variant used only on Amex volume — i.e. the value-add is real inside Amex's walls but may not become the industry primitive. The central question is therefore not "is Amex in agentic commerce" (yes) but **"will Amex's intent model be adopted beyond Amex's own closed loop, or stay a boutique spec?"**

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
Watch for: (a) named ACE/Intent partners and first disclosed live agentic transactions on Amex; (b) EMVCo output — if the agentic-commerce WG (Amex-chaired) ships a spec embedding proof-of-intent, that is the real win; (c) whether OpenAI ACP / Google AP2 reference Amex intent natively. Regulatory backdrop: fraud liability for agent-initiated purchases and "did the human consent" are the open questions all of these frameworks are racing to pre-answer before regulators do. **Why the market goes this way (2nd order, counterintuitive):** publishing an *open* framework looks generous but is a defensive standards-capture move by the smallest-footprint network; Amex's best outcome is that intent-as-a-standard neutralises the agent platforms' power to disintermediate issuers. Risk: being a framework-publisher without volume means Amex sets terms nobody is obliged to follow — standards influence without distribution is fragile.

### Red-team / challenge questions

1. **Is this actually new, or a re-wrap of the April ACE launch?** Partly a re-wrap: Intent Intelligence is the "intent" pillar of the ACE Developer Kit (launched 14 Apr 2026). The 13 Oct 2026 news is Amex *publishing/opening* that pillar as a standalone, would-be-industry framework — a genuine new development (public spec + standards push), not a reprint. **Fresh.**
2. **Is it a spec, a standard, or a live API?** A published *framework/spec* exposed via the ACE Developer Kit (Create Intent API, Intent ID, Proof of Intent Token, restricted credential). Not an adopted industry standard; Amex is lobbying EMVCo/x402 for that status. (answered)
3. **Any named production partners or merchants?** No. PR says "in talks with payments partners" and "AI platform partners" — no names, no counts. (open — material gap)
4. **Any transaction / GMV / adoption numbers?** None disclosed for Intent Intelligence or the broader ACE kit. (open)
5. **What is the real mechanism delta vs Visa and Mastercard?** Amex = issuer-side proof-of-intent surfaced *before* the agent executes, over a closed loop; Visa = signed-intent payload at acceptance via VTS agent credentials; Mastercard = Agentic Token bound at provisioning. Delta is real. (answered)
6. **Does the closed-loop model actually confer an advantage or limit it?** Both: closed loop enables early issuer-side intent control end-to-end, but caps reach to Amex volume only — hence the standards push. (analysis)
7. **Who eats fraud liability on an agent purchase under this framework?** The framework is about proving authorised intent so the *issuer* can treat it as cardholder-approved; explicit liability allocation vs merchants/agents is not spelled out in the PR. (open)
8. **Is Amex ahead or behind?** Behind on first-mover timing (ACE Apr 2026 vs Visa Oct 2025, Mastercard earlier), differentiated on angle. (answered)
9. **Does any rival already have a LIVE agentic transaction Amex lacks?** Yes — Mastercard has a reported live agentic payment ([[PhotonPay completes first live agentic payment with Mastercard]]); Amex shows only "AI-assisted transactions with partners", unquantified. (answered)
10. **Will the intent model be adopted beyond Amex's own cards?** Unknown — hinges on EMVCo WG output (Amex chairs it) and whether AP2/ACP reference it. This is the weight-deciding question. (open)
11. **How does it relate to Google AP2 / OpenAI-Stripe ACP / x402?** Amex frames ACE as "interoperable with existing/emerging protocols" and engages the x402 Foundation; concrete integration not demonstrated. (open)
12. **Is "Proof of Intent Token → restricted credential" novel cryptography or repackaged tokenization?** Appears to be constrained/tokenized credentials scoped by intent — evolutionary, not a new primitive. (analysis)
13. **Does the Hyper acquisition connect here?** Hyper is agentic *expense* (B2B spend), ACE/Intent is consumer agentic *commerce* — adjacent AI-agent bets, not the same product. (answered)
14. **What breaks the value-add?** If industry standardises on a Visa/Mastercard-flavoured model, Amex's framework becomes a proprietary variant with no distribution leverage. (analysis)
15. **Is there any regulatory forcing function?** Agent-initiated-purchase consent and fraud liability are unresolved; these frameworks are pre-empting regulation, but no cited rule yet. (open)

Importance: 3/5 — A real, differentiated design (issuer-side, pre-execution proof of intent over a closed loop) from a major network, and an explicit standards-capture play via EMVCo. But it is a *published framework*, not demonstrated adoption: no named partners, no volume, and it trails Visa/Mastercard on timing and on a live transaction. Strategically notable, operationally unproven — mid-weight.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** Agentic commerce — AI agents transacting on a consumer's behalf — moved from concept to a live standards race in 2025–26. Six-plus overlapping specs now define the stack (per web, as of Apr 2026): OpenAI/Stripe's ACP (checkout, Shared Payment Token bound to merchant+amount, single-use), Google's AP2 (payment-consent via Intent/Cart/Payment Mandates; announced 16 Sep 2025 with 60+ partners incl. Amex, Mastercard, PayPal, Adyen, Worldpay; later donated to FIDO), Anthropic's MCP, Google A2A, plus network on-ramps — Visa's Intelligent Commerce Connect (8 Apr 2026, protocol-agnostic) and Mastercard Agent Pay. ACP and AP2 are different layers (checkout vs consent), not direct rivals; a full purchase can use both. Why now: agents can misfire and transact against mis-interpreted instructions, so the unresolved question is *authorization* — proving the human actually approved the cart, with liability/dispute handling. Amex's own 2025 scale, per its FY2025 annual report (via SEC/EDGAR): worldwide billed business ~$1,670bn, record revenue ~$72bn (+10% YoY), 86.6m proprietary cards-in-force — a closed-loop network that is both issuer and acquirer, which is the structural wedge for this launch.

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs here are not GPV/take-rate but *trust-layer primitives*: who owns the consent record, who issues the credential the agent spends with, and who holds fraud/dispute liability. **Risk flags.**
1. *Standards fragmentation / adoption.* Intent Intelligence is a published *framework* ("intended to inform implementation"), not a live, adopted standard — it competes for mindshare against AP2 (60+ partners incl. Amex itself) and ACP. A proprietary issuer framework risks being subsumed by, or reduced to a profile within, the FIDO/EMVCo/network standards it overlaps. Why: if agents standardize on AP2/ACP + network on-ramps, Amex's spec becomes an internal implementation detail, not an industry lever.
2. *Scale/network reach.* Closed-loop is the pitch but also the ceiling — Amex's ~$1.67tn billed business is a fraction of Visa/Mastercard open-loop volume, so a framework that works best inside Amex's own issuer+acquirer loop has limited gravitational pull on merchants and third-party issuers. Why: standards win on ubiquity; a framework only Amex can fully implement may not set the industry default.
3. *Economics/liability unstated.* Amex cites "thousands" of weekly agentic transactions (small, unaudited) and touts dispute support, but is silent on fraud-liability allocation, merchant fees and who pays when an agent misfires despite a valid Proof-of-Intent Token. Why: without clear liability economics, merchant/issuer uptake stalls regardless of the spec's elegance.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** `(analysis)` The agentic-commerce fight is shifting from "can an agent pay?" (solved by tokens) to "can it *prove* the human approved it?" — the consent/authorization layer — and the issuer side is now entering a contest previously framed as networks vs platforms. Falsifiable thesis: Intent Intelligence matters only if its Proof-of-Intent primitive is adopted *outside* Amex. Trigger/what to watch: a non-Amex issuer or a network (Visa/Mastercard) referencing or interoperating with the EMVCo agentic spec Amex chairs; conversely, if AP2/ACP ship their own consent attestations with broad partner lists and Amex's framework gets no external implementers within ~2–3 quarters, the thesis is wrong and this stays a closed-loop feature.
