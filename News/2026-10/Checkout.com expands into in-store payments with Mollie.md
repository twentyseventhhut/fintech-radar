Checkout.com expands into in-person payments as merchant demand drives next phase of growth

The global payments company is bringing its expertise on performance, resilience and uptime to in-person payments with a software-led, device-agnostic proposition that works with the hardware merchants choose.
Sustained profitability has enabled Checkout.com to invest in new areas like in-person payments with the same conviction as when it was founded 15 years ago.
European fintech leader Mollie is partnering with Checkout.com to further accelerate its rapidly growing in-person payments proposition.
 LONDON, UK — October 20 — Checkout.com, a leading global payments company, today announced its expansion into in-person payments, in response to significant demand from enterprise merchants seeking the performance and resilience of its online acquiring platform in stores.
Mollie, the European payments platform serving more than 350,000 businesses, is working with Checkout.com on its in-person payments offering, leveraging its expertise, terminal knowledge and hardware with Checkout.com's acquiring platform. 
Checkout.com has taken a software-led, device-agnostic approach to in-person payments. This means the solution is not tied to a single terminal or hardware provider. The modularity of Checkout.com's platform enables merchants to connect to terminals they already use or choose hardware that fits their operations, giving them greater flexibility.
Through the long-term partnership, Mollie combines Checkout.com's acquiring platform with its local expertise, software and merchant relationships, to serve businesses selling both online and in person. Mollie retains control of its customer experience and hardware strategy, with Checkout.com’s solution running directly on Mollie's offering.
Checkout.com’s in-person payments solution lets merchants process in-person and online transactions through one acquiring relationship. Both channels use the same Unified Payments API, platform, contract and settlement infrastructure, while a shared dashboard gives payment teams a common view and makes reconciliation simpler.
"This expansion follows our earlier announcement of sustained profitability and growing margins, giving us the capacity to invest in new technologies and segments. Our merchants know Checkout.com for our speed, reliability and pace of innovation, and they asked us to bring that to in-person payments. Our job is to go where they need us most," said Antoine Nougué, Chief Revenue Officer at Checkout.com. 
“In-person payments is one of the fastest-growing areas of our business, driven by hundreds of thousands of European merchants looking to unify their physical and digital sales,” said Rogier Schoute, CPO of Mollie . “Our obsession has always been delivering the simplest, highest-performing product for our customers. When it comes to enterprise-grade processing and infrastructure, we deliberately partner with industry leaders like Checkout.com. Combining our proprietary hardware, point-of-sale software, and rapid in-person growth with Checkout.com’s enterprise infrastructure ensures our merchants get the best of both worlds: complete flexibility on the shop floor, backed by world-class reliability.”
Checkout.com will process $480 billion in global ecommerce payments in 2026. Its multi-region infrastructure and direct scheme connectivity reflect years of investment in a single platform built for scale and resilience with sub-500ms latency. Over the past 12 months, the company has launched more than 150 merchant-facing features, supported by 500 code releases a day.
In-person payments will launch first in the UK and EEA, followed by the US. More schemes, markets and capabilities will be added next year.

## [0] What happened (fact)

On **20 October 2026** (London), **Checkout.com announced its entry into in-person (in-store) payments**, and named **Mollie** as its launch/partner merchant. Crucially, the actual relationship is the inverse of the headline framing: **Mollie is the merchant-facing party** that will **embed Checkout.com's acquiring platform behind its own in-person payments product**. Mollie keeps control of the customer experience, proprietary hardware (terminals, POS software) and merchant relationships; Checkout.com supplies the enterprise-grade acquiring rails (single Unified Payments API, contract, settlement, shared dashboard across online + in-store). Checkout.com's approach is pitched as **software-led and device-agnostic** (not tied to one terminal vendor). Rollout: **UK and EEA first, then the US**, with more schemes/markets "next year." (Sources: company newsroom; [thePaypers](https://thepaypers.com/payments/news/checkoutcom-launches-in-person-payments-and-partners-with-mollie); [Finovate](https://finovate.com/checkout-com-expands-into-in-person-payments/); [Electronic Payments International](https://www.electronicpaymentsinternational.com/news/checkout-com-moves-into-in-person-payments-with-mollie/))

## [1] Who the players are

- **Checkout.com** — London-based global online acquirer, ~15 yrs old. Will process **~$480bn in e-commerce volume in 2026**; ~**$750m annualised net revenue** (+28% YoY TTM, per [Tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2026/09/29/checkout-com-says-annualised-net-revenue-hits-750m-as-releases-selective-group-financial-figures)); returned to full-year EBITDA profitability in 2025 ([[Checkout.com returns to profitability, passes $300B volume]]). Operates in ~56 countries with ~10 acquiring licences; US is fastest-growing region (~20% of volume). It had been a pure online/card-not-present player — this is its **first move into card-present/in-store**.
- **Mollie** — Dutch SMB-focused PSP serving **350,000+ businesses**; FY2025 net revenue €147m (+29%, [[Mollie posts 29% net revenue growth to €147 million]]). Has been building in-person fast: Tap terminal in NL ([[Mollie rolls out tap payment terminal in the Netherlands]]) and UK ([[Mollie expands UK in-person payments with Tap terminal and app]]), completed all **30 EEA markets** with a €350m investment ([[Mollie commits €350 million to complete 30-country EEA expansion]]), and is mid-acquisition of **GoCardless (~€1.05bn)** ([[Mollie to acquire GoCardless for about €1.05B]]).

## [2] Why it matters / significance

Omnichannel acceptance (one platform for online + in-store) is now **table stakes** in European acquiring (Adyen unified commerce; Stripe Terminal/Tap to Pay). For **Checkout.com**, in-store is a credible adjacent-revenue lever after a brutal valuation reset — **$12bn vs the $40bn of its 2022 round** ([[Checkout.com now valued at $12B, down from $40B]], [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/09/26/fintech-checkoutcoms-valuation-falls-to-12-billion.html)) — and lets it answer enterprise merchants asking for in-store without building hardware. For **Mollie**, outsourcing enterprise-grade processing to a specialist lets it scale in-person faster while keeping its SMB brand, hardware and UX. The structure is a classic **infrastructure-vs-distribution split**: Checkout supplies rails, Mollie owns the merchant.

## [3] Open questions / what to watch

- Economics/revenue split are undisclosed — is Checkout a white-label processor (low margin) or a true co-sell partner?
- Does this cannibalise Checkout's own ambition to go direct into in-store, or is Mollie a lighthouse reference to attract enterprise merchants?
- Mollie already had in-person acquiring (Tap, terminals) — which acquirer did it use before, and is this a **migration** of existing in-store volume onto Checkout rails (sticky) or only incremental new volume?
- Execution risk: card-present is a different operational discipline (terminal certification, scheme compliance, chargebacks, uptime at POS) than CNP — Checkout is new to it.

7. **Hardware reality.** Checkout is "device-agnostic / software-led" and owns no terminals; Mollie brings hardware. Is this genuinely a capability or just a rebrand of Mollie's existing terminals?
8. **Exclusivity / term.** "Long-term partnership" — exclusive? Can Mollie add other acquirers; can Checkout sign rival PSPs?
9. **Competitive gap.** Adyen (unified commerce) and Stripe (Terminal/Tap to Pay) have shipped omnichannel for years. Is Checkout+Mollie catching up rather than leading?
10. **Execution risk in card-present.** CNP acquirer entering CP: terminal certification, scheme in-person rules, POS uptime, chargeback profile — all new disciplines. What's the evidence they can deliver?
11. **Source independence.** Most coverage is driven by the Checkout.com press release; is there any independent merchant or volume confirmation?
12. **Mollie's distraction.** Mollie is simultaneously integrating GoCardless (~€1.05bn) and finishing 30-EEA expansion — is bandwidth a risk?
13. **Geographic overlap.** Launch UK+EEA — exactly where Nexi/Worldline/SumUp/Teya already have dense in-store POS footprints. How does Checkout+Mollie win share?
14. **"$480bn in 2026" figure** — that's e-commerce volume, unrelated to in-store traction. Don't conflate it with in-person scale.

Importance: 3/5 — A strategically logical, well-sourced move: Checkout.com monetises its acquiring platform in a new (in-store) channel and Mollie accelerates in-person without building enterprise rails. But it is announcement-stage, with no disclosed economics, no live-volume proof, and it's a catch-up to Adyen/Stripe's long-shipping omnichannel. Notable for both firms' trajectories (Checkout's post-reset diversification; Mollie's up-market push) rather than market-moving on its own.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector: European omnichannel acquiring / in-store POS.** The deal plants Checkout.com — historically an online-only enterprise acquirer — into in-person payments via a channel partnership with Mollie, targeting the "unified commerce" segment (one acquiring relationship, one API, shared settlement across online + physical). This is the strategically contested patch of European acquiring: enterprise merchants increasingly want a single provider stitching e-commerce and in-store, and that demand is precisely what every scaled PSP is chasing. Checkout.com's framing ("significant demand from enterprise merchants seeking the performance and resilience of its online acquiring platform in stores") is a follower move — it is late to physical acceptance and using Mollie's existing terminal hardware, POS software and merchant base to accelerate rather than build from scratch.

**Market size / growth.** The European POS-terminal market is a large, growing pool — ~USD 35bn (2026) scaling toward ~USD 66bn by 2034, with unit shipments growing ~12.5% CAGR (Mordor Intelligence / Market Data Forecast, 2026). But the pool is crowded and the top end is concentrated: the top five terminal vendors took ~52% of 2024 shipments. Checkout.com itself will process ~$480bn in global e-commerce payments in 2026 (its own figure; see Earnings Review) — so in-store is incremental TAM expansion on an already-large online base, not a pivot.

**Comps / competitive set (skeptical read).**
- **Adyen** — the benchmark for enterprise unified commerce. Adyen built out physical POS off its online platform years ago and is the default for multi-country, multi-channel enterprise merchants (the exact buyer Checkout.com now courts). Checkout.com is attacking Adyen's core franchise from behind; Adyen's in-store product is mature, Checkout.com's launches "first in UK and EEA, followed by the US," with "more schemes, markets and capabilities added next year" — i.e. a v1.
- **Stripe (Terminal)** — the other scaled online-first acquirer that extended into in-person; direct strategic analogue to this move.
- **Worldline / Ingenico** — incumbent terminal leader (~28–30% share by some estimates) but structurally troubled and losing ground; a share-donor, not the growth threat.
- **SumUp, Viva.com, myPOS, Flatpay (the "Tap Pack")** — dominate micro/SME in-store with cheap hardware and fast onboarding. Different buyer than Checkout.com's enterprise focus, so limited head-to-head, but they define pricing expectations at the low end.
- **Mollie's own in-person line** — Mollie already sells Tap terminals + Tap-to-Pay-on-iPhone across the EEA and UK (e.g. UK Tap launch, Mar 2026; Tap-to-Pay in ES/AT/IT/UK). This deal supplies Mollie the *enterprise-grade acquiring back-end* it chose not to build ("we deliberately partner with industry leaders"), so the two are complementary here rather than competing — but it also means Mollie's SME-skewed base may not map cleanly onto Checkout.com's enterprise acquiring economics.

**Structure note.** This is a channel/infrastructure partnership, not an M&A or equity deal — no disclosed financial terms, revenue share, or volume commitment. Mollie "retains control of its customer experience and hardware strategy, with Checkout.com's solution running directly on Mollie's offering," and separately Checkout.com launches its *own* device-agnostic in-person product. So Checkout.com gets both a direct enterprise offering and a distribution channel via Mollie's 350k+ merchants.

**Risk flags (5).**
1. **Late entrant into a mature, concentrated segment.** In-store unified commerce is Adyen's and Stripe's established turf; Checkout.com arrives with a v1 ("launch first in UK/EEA … more capabilities next year"). Execution lag against entrenched enterprise incumbents is the primary risk.
2. **No disclosed economics.** No deal terms, volume commitment, or revenue split. "Long-term partnership" is directional; the actual contribution to either party's volume/revenue is unquantified and unverifiable from the release.
3. **Hardware-agnostic = thin moat / commoditized layer.** Checkout.com explicitly does not own terminals ("not tied to a single terminal or hardware provider"). Its differentiation rests on software/acquiring performance (sub-500ms latency, 150+ features, 500 releases/day — self-reported, unaudited), which competitors also claim. In-store acceptance is price-competitive and hardware-driven; a software-only acquirer may struggle to defend margin.
4. **Channel-conflict / dependency risk for Mollie.** Mollie outsources enterprise acquiring to a company that is simultaneously launching a competing direct in-person product. If Checkout.com's direct enterprise push succeeds, interests could diverge over the same large-merchant segment.
5. **Buyer-base mismatch.** Checkout.com targets enterprise; Mollie's 350k+ merchants skew SME/mid-market. The partnership's value depends on Mollie's base actually demanding enterprise-grade in-person acquiring — unproven, and Mollie already has a self-built in-person line serving that base.

4. **Disclosure gap / de-PR.** Both tout growth areas ("fastest-growing", "sustained profitability") while staying silent on in-person-specific revenue, volume, deal terms, and any fresh valuation — treat the partnership as strategically material but financially unquantified until a results print.
