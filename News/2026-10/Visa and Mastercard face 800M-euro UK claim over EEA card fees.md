## Контекст

<!-- enrichment:context -->
**What happened.** A new opt-out collective ("class") action seeking roughly **€800M** (≈£700M in parallel reporting) was filed against **Visa and Mastercard** at the **UK Competition Appeal Tribunal (CAT)** on **9 October 2026**. The proposed class representative is **Ann Pope**, a former Senior Director at the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA), represented by competition firm **Geradin Partners**. The proposed class is ~**126,000 UK-based businesses** that accepted online/remote payments from customers holding **EEA-issued cards** at any point since **October 2020**.

**The theory.** After Brexit, the EU caps on cross-border (inter-regional) interchange ceased to apply to UK↔EEA transactions. From ~October 2021 Visa and Mastercard raised fees on these online EEA-card transactions roughly **five-fold**. The claim alleges those post-Brexit increases were an unlawful, anti-competitive overcharge on UK merchants.

**Why €800M vs £700M.** Coverage uses both figures ([[thepaypers]] headline €800M; The Lawyer / Global Legal Post / Geradin's own release cite £700M). This is the same single claim quoted in two currencies, not two separate cases. Status: **filed / proposed** — the CAT must still grant a Collective Proceedings Order (certification) before it proceeds; it is not yet a judgment or settlement.

**How this fits the broader interchange war (internal context).**
- [[Visa and Mastercard face new UK class action over card fees]] (2025-07) — a separate collective claim over *domestic* consumer debit/credit MIFs; this EEA-card online claim is a distinct, newer front focused on cross-border post-Brexit fees.
- [[UK tribunal rules Visa and Mastercard fees breach competition law]] (2025-07) — the CAT's liability finding that MIFs restricted competition; the legal foundation these follow-on damages claims rely on.
- [[UK PSR opens feedback on cross-border interchange fee review]] (2026-03) — the regulatory track on the *same* cross-border/EEA fees (PSR considering a cap), running in parallel to this litigation.
- [[FCA opens competition probe into PayPal, Mastercard and Visa]] (2026-05) — adds to the stack of live UK scrutiny of the schemes.
- [[Visa and Mastercard reach revised interchange settlement]] (2026-01) — the US merchant swipe-fee settlement; shows the same contested-economics fight on the other side of the Atlantic.

**Precedent / scale check.** The landmark **Merricks v Mastercard** consumer claim (filed 2016; Supreme Court 2020 ruling opened UK opt-out collective actions) **settled for £200M** (CAT approved Feb 2025) — against an initial ~£14bn ask — after the CAT found EEA MIFs had no causative influence on the UK *domestic* fees at the core of that case. That outcome is a cautionary data point: headline claim values in UK interchange cases have historically compressed massively. Note, though, that the present claim is squarely about **cross-border EEA fees** — the exact category Merricks' domestic theory lacked — so its causation story is more direct.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** Card-interchange litigation in the UK is now a structural, recurring cost line for the two networks, not a one-off. The new filing is a proposed opt-out collective action at the UK Competition Appeal Tribunal (CAT) seeking ~EUR 800m for ~126,000 UK businesses that accepted online/remote payments from EEA-issued cards since Oct 2020 (class rep Ann Pope, ex-CMA; firm Geradin Partners) — per thepaypers/mLex, Oct 2026. The theory is post-Brexit specific: EU interchange caps (0.2% debit / 0.3% credit) stopped binding UK-EEA transactions once the UK left the bloc in 2020, and the schemes then raised cross-border fees ~5x (to ~1.15%/1.5%) even though processing cost was unchanged (UK stays in SEPA). Why now: the CAT already ruled (June 2025) that multilateral interchange restricts competition under UK/EU law; a wave of follow-on damages claims is the predictable second-order effect of that liability finding. Structure: a duopoly (Visa + Mastercard) sitting on the most regulated chokepoint of the value chain — interchange — so litigation/regulatory risk is the dominant "market" variable here, not TAM growth.

**Competitive landscape.** Sector runs on payments volume (TPV/GDV), take rate, and — for this theme — litigation provision as a share of earnings. Both networks are defendants jointly; competition is on scheme rules, not price (interchange is set, not negotiated by merchants), which is exactly the antitrust complaint. Recent dated moves: CAT ruled fees breach competition law (27 Jun 2025); Court of Appeal granted Visa permission to appeal (17 Mar 2026); UK FCA opened a separate antitrust probe into Visa, Mastercard and PayPal (6 May 2026); this EEA-fee claim filed Oct 2026. Parallel front: a £1.5bn Apple Pay CAT claim (Jan 2026) shows the same opt-out collective model now aimed across the card/wallet stack. Position: both schemes are on the defensive in the UK but the economics are immaterial at group level (see comps) — this is a tail/legal-reserve story, not a revenue story.

**Comps & multiples (exposure vs earnings — IR-grounded).** The EUR 800m claim is split across two issuers, so size it against each. Visa FY26 Q3 (qtr ended 30 Jun 2026): net revenue $11.6bn, GAAP net income $5.6bn; payments volume crossed $4tn (per Visa Q3-2026 earnings release, 28 Jul 2026). Mastercard Q2 2026 (qtr ended 30 Jun 2026): net revenue $9.3bn, net income $4.4bn (per Mastercard 2Q26 earnings release, 30 Jul 2026). EUR 800m ≈ $0.9bn gross, undivided and pre-liability-finding:
- vs Visa single-quarter net income: $0.9bn / $5.6bn ≈ 0.16x — i.e. ~16% of ONE quarter's profit, and only a fraction would land on Visa even if the full claim succeeded (analysis).
- vs Mastercard single-quarter net income: $0.9bn / $4.4bn ≈ 0.20x (analysis).
Context from Visa's own reserving discipline: in H1 FY26 (6 mths to 31 Mar 2026) Visa booked $894m of additional U.S.-interchange accruals and deposited $625m into its U.S. litigation escrow (Visa FY26 10-Q, Legal Matters). So a sub-$1bn UK exposure is well within the networks' normal annual litigation-reserve cadence — immaterial to valuation, material only as evidence the UK liability chain keeps compounding. Internal comps (same theme, UK interchange): [[Visa and Mastercard face new UK class action over card fees]] (Jul 2025, multi-billion-£ merchant claim), [[UK tribunal rules Visa and Mastercard fees breach competition law]] (the Jun 2025 liability finding this claim builds on), [[FCA opens competition probe into PayPal, Mastercard and Visa]] (May 2026), [[Apple Pay GBP1.5bn class action launched in UK over fees]] (Jan 2026, same opt-out CAT mechanism). Trading multiples (EV/EBITDA, P/E) not computed — no clean free EV/consensus source [UNSOURCED]; not load-bearing since the claim is immaterial to either market cap.

**Risk flags.**
1. Liability precedent, not the dollar amount — the June 2025 CAT finding that interchange restricts competition converts this from a contested-liability case into a damages-quantification case; each new follow-on claim (this EUR 800m, plus the >100 outstanding UK merchant claims Visa still carries) compounds a reserve line, so the real risk is cumulative UK exposure, not this single filing.
2. Post-Brexit fee-hike is the narrow, defensible-against-Visa/MC fact pattern — fees rose ~5x while SEPA processing cost was flat; that specificity makes the EEA claim harder to defend than generic interchange cases and invites copycat classes for other post-2020 fee increases (second-order: pricing power on cross-border is the exposed flank).
3. Multi-front UK pressure — simultaneous FCA antitrust probe (May 2026) + CAT damages claims + Court of Appeal remedy uncertainty means the UK could force a structural fee cap (as the EU did), which would hit recurring cross-border take rate, a far larger NPV risk than any one settlement.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** The opt-out CAT collective-action machine has found product-market fit against payment rails: post the 2025 liability ruling, every historical UK fee increase is now a packageable claim (analysis). Falsifiable thesis: UK interchange shifts from episodic settlements to a standing, quantifiable reserve + eventual regulatory fee cap on EEA/cross-border rates. Trigger to watch: the Court of Appeal ruling on Visa's appeal of the June 2025 CAT decision — affirmation would green-light mass quantification (bearish on UK cross-border take rate); reversal would blunt the whole follow-on wave and make this EUR 800m claim far weaker. What breaks the thesis: a Court of Appeal reversal or an FCA finding of no infringement.

**Thesis-flags (litigation scale — the reason this pick gets an earnings layer).**
1. **Reserves are modest and were drawn down, not built up.** Mastercard accrued only **$177M** for the U.S. MDL interchange cases at 31 Mar 2026 (down from **$637M** at 31 Dec 2025 — i.e. paid down, not topped up). It disclosed **>£0.3bn (≈$0.4bn) of unresolved UK/Pan-European damages claims** with **no specific accrual** against them — so a fresh £700M/€800M claim lands on top of an already-unreserved UK/EEA pile. Why it matters: a new unreserved claim could force a future provision; second-order, each new UK front raises the probability Mastercard eventually books a charge against this category.
2. **Visa's European exposure sits in the VE-territory structure, and it's small-but-active.** Visa accrued **$28M** of VE-territory covered losses in the six months and holds purpose-built loss-absorbing preferred stock (Series B $7M / Series C $116M book value at 31 Mar 2026) plus a separate **$329M** quarterly general litigation provision. Its U.S. litigation escrow fell to **$665M** (from $2.990bn) after ~**$2.95bn** was paid to opt-out merchants — showing how these cases ultimately convert to cash, just over years. Why it matters: the mechanism to absorb a UK/EEA loss exists and is funded, but the €800M claim is not yet reflected in any of these balances.
3. **Immaterial alone, compounding in aggregate.** €800M ≈ ~0.5 quarters of either company's net income; in isolation it is headline, not cash, risk — and UK interchange claims have a track record of collapsing at settlement (Merricks £14bn→£200M, per context). The thesis risk is the *stack*: this claim + the 2025 domestic-MIF class action + the PSR cross-border cap review + the FCA probe, all on overlapping fee pools. Second-order: a regulated cross-border cap would durably compress the highest-margin inter-regional interchange line that is currently driving both companies' cross-border revenue outperformance.
4. **Timing/causation nuance.** Both filings predate this claim; it is *filed, not certified* (needs a CAT Collective Proceedings Order). Near-term there is **no earnings or reserve impact** — expect no accrual move until/unless certification advances. Watch the next 10-Qs (Visa fiscal Q3, Mastercard Q2) for any addition to the UK/EEA unreserved-claims disclosure.
