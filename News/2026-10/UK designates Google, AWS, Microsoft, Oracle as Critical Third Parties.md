# UK designates Google, AWS, Microsoft, Oracle as Critical Third Parties

## Первоисточники

### fca.org.uk
<https://www.fca.org.uk/news/statements/uk-financial-regulators-overseeing-critical-third-parties-announced-treasury>
*791 слов · direct*

The Bank of England (the Bank), the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA) and the FCA will start overseeing the first critical third parties (CTPs) on Monday 13 July 2026, following designation by the Treasury.
CTPs are technology and other service providers whose services underpin the UK financial system. Today, the Treasury has announced its first designations of 4 global cloud services and technology providers: Amazon Web Services EMEA SARL, Google Cloud EMEA Limited, Microsoft Ireland Operations Ltd, and Oracle Corporation UK Limited.
As many firms rely on these services, disruption or failure could affect multiple firms or markets at the same time, potentially impacting UK financial stability and services used by millions of consumers and businesses.
For the first time, the three regulators will jointly oversee these CTPs under a new, proportionate regime, focused on the resilience of the critical services they provide to the UK financial sector. The regulators will work together with the CTPs to address system-level risks and reduce the risk of disruption to the services they provide spreading across the UK financial system. This will strengthen system-wide resilience and improve coordination and information sharing across the UK financial sector.
CTPs must identify and manage risks to their critical services effectively, and maintain open, timely communication with regulators and the firms that rely on them, particularly during major incidents.
Together, these changes support a more resilient environment for firms to operate in, which will, in turn support financial stability and confidence in UK financial markets.
This regime complements, but does not replace, existing outsourcing and operational resilience rules for regulated firms who remain responsible for managing their own third-party arrangements including due diligence, risk management and contingency planning.
Sarah Breeden, deputy governor for financial stability at the Bank, said: 
'As critical third parties become increasingly embedded in the operations of financial institutions, they can introduce new forms of systemic risk. Our proportionate approach to overseeing these providers will ensure that these dependencies are managed in a way that safeguards financial stability.'
Katharine Braddick, deputy governor for prudential regulation and CEO of the PRA, said:  
'By bringing critical third parties into the scope of oversight, we are ensuring that the infrastructure underpinning UK financial services is robust enough to support UK financial stability and confidence. This directly supports the PRA’s objective to promote the safety and soundness of regulated firms.'
Nikhil Rathi, chief executive at the FCA, said:  
'Critical third parties provide essential services which support innovation and growth. At the same time, when the same providers serve thousands of firms, a single failure can reverberate across the financial system. Operationalising this regime strengthens our ability to tackle those risks and improve overall resilience, ensuring the UK remains a safe and attractive place to do business.'  
Treasury is responsible for deciding which third party providers are designated as CTPs, and any future designations or de-designations. The regulators will periodically review whether CTPs continue to meet the designation criteria, making recommendations to Treasury, and evaluate the effectiveness of the oversight approach.  
The Bank, PRA and FCA will continue to work closely with Treasury, the financial services industry and designated CTPs as the regime is implemented, while supporting innovation, and UK growth and competitiveness.
Notes to editors
The regulations will come into effect on Monday 13 July 2026. Please refer to the government’s website for Treasury’s press release and CTP regulations published. 
You can find more information on the regulators’ oversight regime on FCA’s website and PRA’s website .
Treasury is responsible for deciding which third party suppliers should fall under the CTP regime, generally based on recommendations from the regulators. The scope of entities under the regime will continue to evolve as further designations are considered.
Designation under this regime is not the same as authorisation by the regulators. Oversight is limited to the resilience of the services they provide to UK financial firms.
Financial Services and Markets Act (FSMA) 2000 as amended by FSMA 2023 gave the Bank, PRA and FCA new powers to oversee critical third parties, strengthening the resilience of the services they provide to regulated firms and financial market infrastructures.
In November 2024, the FCA, the Bank and PRA introduced the final rules and policy for the CTP regime. These came into effect on 1 January 2025 and apply immediately to CTPs once designated by Treasury.
Alongside the final rules and policy, the regulators published their approach to regulatory oversight and a supervisory statement setting out expectations for CTPs on how to interpret and comply with the regulators’ new rules.
CTPs may also be regulated under similar regimes in other jurisdictions, including the EU’s Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA). The regulators have signed a Memorandum of Understanding to support coordination and information sharing on oversight of CTPs.

## [0] What happened (one-paragraph fact base)
HM Treasury made the UK's first-ever Critical Third Party (CTP) designations under the Financial Services and Markets Act 2023, naming four global cloud/technology providers whose services underpin UK finance: Amazon Web Services EMEA SARL, Google Cloud EMEA Limited, Microsoft Ireland Operations Ltd, and Oracle Corporation UK Limited. The Bank of England, the PRA and the FCA began jointly overseeing these CTPs on Monday 13 July 2026, under rules that took effect 1 January 2025 (final rules/policy published November 2024). Oversight is strictly about the resilience of the critical services the providers supply to UK financial firms and FMIs — it is NOT authorisation, and it does not relieve regulated firms of their own outsourcing/operational-resilience obligations. The regulators signed an MoU to coordinate on CTP oversight (including alignment with the EU's DORA). (Sources: [FCA statement](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/statements/uk-financial-regulators-overseeing-critical-third-parties-announced-treasury); [Bank of England](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/news/2026/july/uk-financial-regulators-to-begin-overseeing-critical-third-parties-announced-by-hmt); [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/uk-financial-system-strengthened-with-new-safeguards-for-major-technology-providers))

## [1] Why it matters (systemic framing)
Cloud concentration is the core concern. AWS, Microsoft and Google together control more than 60% of the global cloud market (AWS ~30%), so a single provider failure can hit thousands of regulated firms simultaneously — a textbook systemic-risk vector. The CTP regime is the UK's answer: direct prudential-style oversight of providers that are too embedded to fail quietly. Deputy governor Sarah Breeden explicitly framed CTPs as capable of "introduc[ing] new forms of systemic risk." This makes the UK one of the first jurisdictions to put hyperscalers under direct financial-stability oversight (alongside the EU under DORA). ([CyberCube on cloud concentration](https://www.cybcube.com/news/aws-outage-highlights-systemic-risk-from-cloud-concentration); [DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/uk-treasury-designates-big-four-us-hyperscalers-as-critical-third-parties/))

## [3] Powers, limits and the DORA comparison
Key limitation widely flagged by law firms: the UK CTP regime (FSMA Chapter 3C) confers NO power to levy financial penalties on a designated provider. The enforcement toolkit is a censure statement plus prohibition/limitation notices (i.e. the regulators can restrict or bar a CTP from providing services, but cannot fine it). By contrast, the EU's DORA — which designated 19 critical ICT third-party providers on 18 Nov 2025, also including Amazon, Microsoft and Google — can impose periodic penalty payments of up to 1% of a provider's average daily worldwide turnover. The UK deliberately started narrow (4 providers) with no statutory cap on future designations; Treasury can add/remove CTPs on regulators' recommendations. ([The Industry Spread — no-fine power](https://theindustryspread.com/uk-critical-third-parties-regime-cannot-fine-aws-microsoft/); [Bratby Law UK vs DORA](https://bratby.law/critical-third-parties-regime-uk-dora/))

## [4] Context that sharpens it (the AWS-outage catalyst)
The timing is not academic. On 20 October 2025 a ~9-hour AWS US-EAST-1 outage (DynamoDB DNS failure cascading to IAM/EC2) took down Snapchat, Fortnite, Roblox, Coinbase, Ring — and in the UK hit Barclays, Lloyds, Bank of Scotland and even HMRC, generating 11M+ outage reports. That event reopened the UK debate on classifying hyperscalers as critical infrastructure and gave the CTP regime real-world urgency. CTP obligations now include scenario/resilience testing, phased incident reporting to regulators and affected firms, self-assessments and skilled-person reviews. ([TechTarget on AWS concentration risk](https://www.techtarget.com/it-strategy/feature/AWS-cloud-outage-reveals-vendor-concentration-risk); [Data Center Knowledge](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/outages/aws-outage-exposes-dangerous-over-reliance-on-us-cloud-giants))

## Red-team / challenge

1. **Is this genuinely new on 2026-10-22?** No. The designation and 13 July 2026 go-live were already reported and enriched in [[UK to oversee critical third-party cloud service providers]] (2026-07-13). This "This Week in Fintech" item is a retelling. **Verdict: stale / duplicate.**

2. **Could the Oct item carry NEW facts (e.g. a fifth designation)?** Open-but-no — the embedded source is the original FCA go-live statement naming the same four providers; no new designation, figures, or enforcement action appears. Nothing distinguishes it from the July story.

3. **When were the designations actually announced?** Treasury announced on/around 10 July 2026; legal effect 13 July 2026 via The Critical Third Parties (Designation) Regulations 2026. Confirmed by FCA/BoE/GOV.UK.

4. **Are the four entities named precisely?** Yes: AWS EMEA SARL, Google Cloud EMEA Ltd, Microsoft Ireland Operations Ltd, Oracle Corporation UK Ltd. Note Microsoft/Google are Ireland/EMEA legal entities, not UK-domiciled — a jurisdictional nuance for enforcement reach.

5. **Does designation = regulation/authorisation of the provider?** No. Oversight is limited to the resilience of services provided to UK finance; providers are not "authorised" and firms keep their own outsourcing duties. Avoid overstating it as "regulating Big Tech."

6. **Can regulators actually fine these providers?** No — a commonly missed point. FSMA Chapter 3C gives no financial-penalty power; tools are censure + prohibition/limitation notices. Overstating "teeth" would be wrong.

7. **How does this compare to the EU?** DORA designated 19 critical ICT providers (18 Nov 2025) and CAN fine up to 1% of daily worldwide turnover. UK is narrower (4) but can expand; UK is lighter on penalties. The framing "UK first to oversee hyperscalers" is contestable given DORA runs in parallel.

8. **Why these four and not, say, IBM/Salesforce/SAP?** Treasury picks based on systemic footprint/regulator recommendations; the four hyperscalers dominate UK financial cloud. Rolling regime means more may follow. Exclusions are a judgement call, not permanent.

9. **Is the AWS Oct-2025 outage causally linked?** Plausibly a catalyst (it hit UK banks + HMRC and reopened the critical-infrastructure debate), but the legal powers predate it (FSMA 2023; rules effective Jan 2025). Don't claim the outage caused the regime — it accelerated salience.

10. **Does the regime reduce concentration risk?** Open. Oversight improves incident response/resilience standards but does not force multi-cloud or break up market share (~60%+ held by three firms). Systemic concentration persists; this manages, not removes, it.

11. **Extraterritoriality / US reaction risk?** Open. All four are US-parented; direct UK oversight of US cloud giants raises coordination questions (hence the MoU and DORA alignment). Potential friction not resolved by this note.

12. **Operational impact on firms now?** Modest near-term: regime "complements, does not replace" existing outsourcing/op-resilience rules; firms still own due diligence and contingency planning. No immediate cost shock evident.

13. **Numbers to be skeptical of?** "60% market share / AWS ~30%" are analyst estimates, not official; cite as approximate. Outage "11M+ reports" and "$X00M damages" are third-party estimates.

14. **Any mention-count / sourcing weakness?** Only 1 mention, single channel (This Week in Fintech), and the "source" text is the July FCA statement — low incremental information for an October entry.

15. **Bottom line relevance to a fintech audience?** Important structural development, but already covered. For this October note the marginal value is a duplicate reminder, not new intelligence.

Importance: 2/5 — The underlying CTP regime is genuinely significant (4/5 on its own merits, as scored on the July note), but this 2026-10-22 entry is a stale re-report of the 13 July 2026 designation already enriched in [[UK to oversee critical third-party cloud service providers]], adding no new facts, figures, or enforcement developments.

**Sector & drivers.** Subsector: cloud infrastructure for financial services / operational-resilience regulation. The global cloud infrastructure market reached $143.4bn in Q2 2026 spend, up ~43% YoY — the fastest growth in eight years (per Synergy Research, via CloudZero/Statista, as of Q2 2026). Structure is highly consolidated: AWS ~28%, Azure ~20%, Google Cloud ~15% — the "big three" hold ~63% of worldwide cloud infrastructure revenue; Oracle ~4% (Synergy, Q2 2026). Entry barriers are extreme (capex-heavy data-centre buildout, scale economics, network/switching-cost lock-in), which is precisely what makes the sector a concentration-risk target. "Why now": the designation operationalises powers granted by FSMA 2023; final CTP rules (PS16/24, SS6/24) were set in Nov 2024, took effect 1 Jan 2025, and the first four designations went live 13 July 2026. A 2024 BoE/FCA survey found Microsoft, Google and Amazon together accounted for ~73% of UK financial-sector cloud services (via mofo.com) — the empirical basis for "single-supplier failure = system-wide event."

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs: revenue run-rate, operating margin, backlog/RPO, market share. Key players and basis of competition: AWS (scale/breadth), Microsoft Azure (enterprise/Office bundle + OpenAI), Google Cloud (data/AI, fastest-growing at ~82% YoY per Synergy), Oracle (database installed base, OCI). Competition is on AI capacity, price, and regulated-workload trust. Recent moves: the EU moved first — the ESAs designated 19 critical ICT third-party providers under DORA on 14 Nov 2025 (published 18 Nov), with each assigned a Lead Overseer from Jan 2026 (PwC Legal). The UK regime (live 13 July 2026) and EU DORA regulators signed an MoU for coordinated oversight. Protagonist position (all four hyperscalers): dominant and entrenched; the designation is a consequence of market power, not a competitive threat to it. Moat = scale + switching costs + network effects (analysis); regulation here reinforces incumbency since only the largest, most-relied-upon providers clear the systemic-importance bar.

**Comps & multiples.** This is a regulatory event with no transaction value, so no deal multiple applies — "no data" on a comp spread. **Risk flags.**
1. Concentration / single-point-of-failure (regulator's own thesis): ~73% of UK financial-sector cloud on three providers means one outage can cascade across many firms simultaneously — the designation names but does not dilute the concentration; it only adds oversight of resilience, not substitutability.
2. Scope limitation / expectation gap: oversight is explicitly "not authorisation" and "complements, does not replace" firm-level outsourcing rules — firms remain fully liable for their own third-party risk. Markets may over-read the regime as a backstop it isn't.
3. Compliance drag + multi-jurisdiction overlap: hyperscalers now face parallel UK CTP + EU DORA regimes (plus the MoU); duplicative incident-reporting and resilience-testing raise cost-to-serve regulated clients, though the MoU is designed to limit divergence.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** This is the start of hyperscalers being supervised like financial-market infrastructure, not merely as vendors (analysis). Thesis: as cloud dependence deepens, expect the designated list to expand (Treasury can add CTPs) and resilience/exit-plan requirements to harden into a recurring cost line for providers and a procurement criterion for banks. Watch: first joint BoE/PRA/FCA supervisory review outcomes, any multi-cloud/exit-mandate for regulated firms, and whether a future cloud outage triggers the regime's enforcement powers. Falsifier: if regulators keep the list at four and add no binding resilience mandates, the regime is reputational signalling rather than structural change.
