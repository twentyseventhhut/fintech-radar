# Bridgit expands Macquarie facility past A$1.7B in debt funding

> [!info] 2026-10-17 · 1 упоминаний · 1 источника(ов) с текстом

## Агрегированный текст (из дайджестов)

[This Week in Fintech] Bridgit, an Australian bridging-loan platform, secured an undisclosed increase in its Macquarie credit facility, bringing total debt funding above A$1.7 billion ($1.17 billion).❝

Investment come as fast-growing challenger lender chases unicorn status
 
 Macquarie Bank has increased its credit facility to bridging finance lender Bridgit, lifting the fintech’s total debt funding to more than $1.7 billion as more Australian homeowners buy their next home before selling their current one.
 
The Sydney-based lender announced on Friday that Macquarie’s Commodities and Global Markets Group had expanded its existing credit line.
 Citi and Macquarie are Bridgit’s two funders. The size of Macquarie’s increase was not disclosed.
“We’re pleased to increase this funding facility to Bridgit as it continues to meet growing demand for Australian homeowners,” said Alhad Bhalerao, division director at Macquarie.
Bridgit’s is one of the fastest-growing challenger lenders in the market. Loan submissions rose 28% in the September quarter compared to the June quarter, despite a broader slowdown in the property market. Its lending grew 168% between the 2025 and 2026 financial years.
“Bridgit is fast becoming a category owner in the bridging and equity release space. Australian homeowners are increasingly choosing to buy now and sell later and even more so given the broader downturn in the property market,” said Aaron Bassin (pictured), Bridgit’s founder and chief executive.
The fintech, which passed $1 billion in loans earlier this year , said it has supported more than $8 billion in property transactions for more than 10,000 homes. 
Its AI-enabled platform has cut approval times to as little as 120 seconds. That growth mirrors bridging finance’s shift from niche product to mainstream option in the broker channel.
 Read more: Bringing bridging to the masses 
“Property is Australia’s largest asset class with over $9 trillion trapped in home equity. At Bridgit, we’re building the finance ecosystem to match. Until now, property-backed finance in Australia has been built for banks, not for the people who own the asset. We’re reinventing access to property wealth, revolutionising how people unlock, use and create it,” Bassin said.
Buy now, sell later becomes a trademark
Earlier in 2026, Bridgit registered “Buy Now, Sell Later” as a trademark . The registration gives it exclusive rights to a phrase brokers have long used with upsizing clients.
Bassin said the new funding moved the business closer to a public listing.
“We’ve built Bridgit around technology that makes accessing home equity faster and simpler. We’ll continue investing in our platform, growing our core products and developing new ones. Our ambition is to build the business with the scale, profitability and governance to access public markets over time.
“This investment is another important step in that journey as well as positioning us in our aspiration to becoming Australia’s next fintech unicorn.”

_Analytical notes (not a post). Importance: 2/5._

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
Macquarie (via its Commodities and Global Markets Group) increased an EXISTING warehouse/credit facility to Bridgit; the **increment was not disclosed**. The only hard number is the aggregate: total debt funding now "more than A$1.7B" (~US$1.17B), across Bridgit's two funders — **Macquarie and Citi**. So the A$1.7B headline is a *committed debt-warehouse capacity* figure (lending firepower), **not** equity, not a valuation, not a drawn balance. This is routine warehouse upsizing for a non-bank lender, dressed up as an "investment."
- **Why framed this way:** a non-bank bridging lender funds loans off wholesale warehouse lines, not deposits; growth = securing bigger/cheaper lines. Disclosing the increment would reveal the real loan-book run-rate and marginal cost of funds, so Bridgit anchors on the flattering cumulative A$1.7B instead. The MPA headline ("Macquarie, Citi invest A$1.7B") conflates a debt facility with "investment" — it is leverage capacity, not cash raised. (analysis)
- Operating metrics given: loan submissions +28% QoQ (Sep vs Jun quarter); lending +168% FY25→FY26; >A$8B in property transactions supported across >10,000 homes; passed A$1B in cumulative loans earlier in 2026; AI approvals "as fast as 120 seconds / 4 minutes"; claims ~85% of broker market reach and ~2% of AU owner-occupier purchases (ex-FHB) in Q4 FY26.

## [1] Competitors / peers
- **AU bridging / equity-release:** Assetline Capital (non-bank, bridging + construction), plus the Big Four + regionals (CBA, Westpac, ANZ, Bankwest, St George/BankSA/Bank of Melbourne) which offer traditional bridging. Bridgit's edge vs banks is speed (minutes vs weeks) and a broker-channel-first "Buy Now, Sell Later" product; vs private lenders, it is regulated and cheaper. (money.com.au, finder)
- **Model peers (non-bank, warehouse-funded):** [[LendInvest secures GBP250M from Castlelake for bridging loans]] (UK bridging/BTL, £250M Castlelake forward-flow — same playbook: fintech lender scaling via a single institutional warehouse), [[Kroo Bank enters forward-flow funding partnership with Glenhawk]] (UK). Bridgit is the AU analogue.
- **Why the landscape is this way:** bridging was a niche, high-friction bank product; fintechs win by collapsing underwriting time and owning the broker distribution layer. Second-order: the moat is **distribution + funding cost**, not technology — any incumbent with a warehouse line and a faster portal can compete, so the "category owner" claim rests on broker lock-in, which is fragile. (analysis)

## [2] Company history / fit
Founded Sydney 2021 by Aaron Bassin (CEO, corporate-finance background) and Nick Jacobs (ex-broker). Capital trajectory: A$14.6M Series A, A$7.7M raise (Buy Now Sell Later product), A$125M facility boost, then >A$1B loans in 2026, now >A$1.7B total debt capacity. 2025: appointed Nick Ognenovski CRO ([[Bridgit appoints Nick Ognenovski as Chief Revenue Officer]]) to build the go-to-market/broker engine. In 2026 it trademarked "Buy Now, Sell Later." Logical fit: a monoline bridging lender's only lever to scale originations is bigger warehouse capacity — this raise is exactly on-strategy and is explicitly framed as a step toward an IPO / "AU's next fintech unicorn."
- **Why:** monoline + warehouse funding means growth is capital-constrained, not demand-constrained — so periodic facility upsizes are the recurring, necessary event, and each one is PR'd as a milestone. (analysis)

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
**Not novel as an event** — a facility increase is BAU for a non-bank lender. The real signal is the **traction**: +168% YoY lending and +28% QoQ submissions *despite* a property-market slowdown, >A$8B supported, >10,000 homes. That counter-cyclical growth is the genuinely interesting datapoint (bridging demand rises when homes are slow to sell, so downturn ≠ headwind for this niche).
- **Who captures the margin:** Bridgit earns the spread between its warehouse cost of funds (set by Macquarie/Citi) and the borrower rate, minus losses. With undisclosed increments and undisclosed cost of funds, the durable question is **unit economics / net interest margin and loss rates**, which are not revealed. The A$1.7B is capacity, not profit. Macquarie/Citi hold the senior, cheapest, lowest-risk slice; Bridgit holds first-loss equity/mezz risk. (analysis)
- Trademarking a generic broker phrase ("Buy Now, Sell Later") is a marketing moat, not a product moat.

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
Bassin explicitly positions this as moving "closer to a public listing" and toward unicorn status. Near-term: keep upsizing warehouses, deepen broker penetration (claims ~85%), launch adjacent equity-release products. Risks: (1) AU property downturn / rising defaults hitting a leveraged, monoline book; (2) funding-cost sensitivity — margin compresses if wholesale rates rise or funders tighten; (3) concentration on just two funders (Macquarie, Citi); (4) IPO ambition depends on proving profitability, not just origination growth.
- **Second-order:** counter-cyclical demand is a double edge — more volume in a downturn also means loans against assets that are harder to sell on time, raising extension/default risk exactly when the book is largest. Capacity growth can mask deteriorating quality. (hypothesis)

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** Australia's non-bank (non-depository) lending sector revenue is projected at ~A$51.6bn in 2026-27, having grown at ~12.7% CAGR over the five years through 2026-27 (per IBISWorld, via web). Non-bank lenders now account for 10.7% of the value of new home lending, up from 4.8% in September 2019, and non-bank home lending surged ~65% YoY to A$10.49bn in the June quarter even as the overall mortgage market contracted (per brokernews.com.au). Structure: bridging/equity-release is a thin, previously-niche sub-vertical within property finance, and it is lightly consolidated — big-four banks dominate the broad mortgage pool but have largely vacated short-term bridging because their processes are not built for it, leaving specialist non-banks to own the slice. Entry barrier is capital/funding access (warehouse + securitisation lines), not technology. "Why now": the broader property downturn structurally FAVORS "buy now, sell later" — owners who can't sell first turn to bridging to transact anyway, so Bridgit's 28% QoQ submission growth comes despite (not with) the housing slowdown (analysis). Second-order: a cyclical tailwind that could reverse if transaction volumes seize up or arrears rise.

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs for a warehouse-funded non-bank lender: loan book / originations, funding capacity utilisation, net interest margin, LVR mix, and loss/arrears rate. Key players in AU bridging/specialist property finance: funding.com.au, La Trobe Financial, Credit Connect Group, Assetline Capital (per web); Bridgit frames the big-four banks as its real competitors by volume, winning share on speed. Basis of competition: speed and distribution (broker channel) more than price — Bridgit's platform cuts approval to as little as 120 seconds and it has trademarked "Buy Now, Sell Later." Recent move (2026-10-17): Macquarie's Commodities & Global Markets Group expanded its existing facility (size undisclosed), taking Bridgit's total debt funding above A$1.7bn across its two funders, Citi and Macquarie. Position: niche category leader / ahead within bridging — it claims to have supported >A$8bn in property transactions across >10,000 homes and zero losses to date, with lending +168% FY25→FY26. Moat (analysis): intangibles (brand/trademark) + the funding relationships themselves (two bank funders is a scale advantage over smaller specialists); thin switching costs at the borrower level given single-use, short-dated loans.

**Comps & multiples.** This is a debt-facility expansion, not an equity round, so there is NO valuation/multiple to anchor to — EV/Revenue, P/S, etc. = no data. Reported equity raised to date is ~A$198M (per PitchBook/web); the A$1.7bn figure is cumulative debt-funding capacity, NOT a valuation or market cap, and should not be confused with one. Internal funding comps (facility size as a scale read-through, not valuation): [[LendInvest secures GBP250M from Castlelake for bridging loans]] (£250M / ~$315M bridging-specific line, UK); [[Younited secures €400 million warehouse facility from Citi]] (€400M warehouse from Citi, Europe consumer lending — same Citi warehouse model); [[WISR reports cash NPAT improvement on loan book growth]] (ASX-listed AU non-bank consumer lender, closest listed-AU read on the funding-to-book dynamic). Against these single-line deals, Bridgit's >A$1.7bn cumulative capacity is large for a bridging specialist — consistent with it being the category scale leader rather than an outlier `(analysis)`. Multiple distribution not computed — no comparable valuation figures.

**Risk flags.**
1. Funding concentration — only two funders (Macquarie, Citi), both banks. A pullback or re-pricing by either would directly throttle originations; a bridging monoline has no deposit base to fall back on, so the funding line IS the business.
2. Cyclical/credit risk masked by growth — "zero losses to date" is unproven through a full downturn. Bridging loans are short-dated and repaid from the sale of the old property; if the property market freezes (the very "downturn" Bridgit cites as a tailwind), sale timelines stretch and peak-debt/LVR risk and arrears can spike quickly.
3. Narrow, replicable niche — the moat is speed + brand, not structural. Big-four banks or a better-funded non-bank could re-enter bridging; the "Buy Now, Sell Later" trademark protects a phrase, not the product economics. Watch whether undisclosed facility pricing is quietly worsening (PR is silent on the rate/terms of Macquarie's increase).

**What this changes (idea-lens).** Reinforces the secular shift of AU bridging from niche to mainstream broker product, with one name consolidating the sub-vertical ahead of a mooted IPO/"unicorn" ambition `(analysis)`. Falsifiable thesis: if Bridgit sustains book growth AND discloses loss rates through the current downturn, an equity round/listing at a genuine unicorn valuation becomes plausible within ~12-18 months; it breaks if arrears emerge or a funder trims the line (watch next: a priced equity round that finally sets a valuation, or any change in facility terms).
