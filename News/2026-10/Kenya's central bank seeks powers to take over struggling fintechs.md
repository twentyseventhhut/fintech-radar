The Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) is seeking sweeping powers over digital payment companies, including raiding their premises without notice and ousting top executives if the firms breach the law and run into financial difficulties.

The National Payment System Bill, 2026, gives CBK officers full access to these companies’ premises to inspect records, interview employees and access computers and data where necessary to establish compliance with the law.

It also gives the regulator power to direct the ouster of an officer or employee it considers to have caused or contributed to a breach or deterioration in the company's financial stability.

This is in an effort to tighten the safety of Kenyans’ cash and the country’s financial ecosystem as mobile money platforms, digital banks, fintechs and payment gateways process growing volumes of transactions.

“Conduct offsite surveillance of payment service providers and payment system operators; and in any case where a payment service provider or payment system operator is part of a group, undertake consolidated supervision of the payment service provider or payment system operator and the group,” the Bill says.

“Direct the removal any officer or employee of a payment service provider or payment system operator who, in the opinion of the Central Bank, has caused or contributed to any contravention of this Act or to any deterioration in the stability of the payment service provider or payment system operator,” it adds.

Payment service providers (PSPs) handle customer-facing transactions, while payment system operators (PSOs) own the underlying infrastructure for settling funds between financial institutions.

Kenya has 43 CBK-licenced payment service providers as of June 2026, among them top firms like M-Pesa, Airtel Money, Equitel, Pesapal and Loop.

The proposal is a shift from the existing 2011 law, which does not spell out whether CBK officials need to notify payment firms before raids.

“A person authorised by the Central Bank shall have power to with or without notice, enter and inspect the premises of a payment service provider or a payment system operator and its agents for the purposes of ensuring compliance with this Act,” the Bill says.

The Bill demands that companies assist CBK officials during inspections. It makes it an offence to obstruct an officer, refuse to provide requested records or information, or provide false or misleading information.

The CBK would gain powers to intervene directly in the management of a payment company where it fails to meet obligations to customers, defaults on financial obligations to other payment providers or the apex bank, ignores a CBK directive or breaches the proposed law.

The CBK could also appoint “a competent and suitably qualified person to the board of directors to hold office as a director.” That director shall be protected from removal without CBK approval.

The proposals say the regulator could take such action during a crisis where intervention is considered necessary to safeguard financial stability, protect customers and preserve confidence in the financial system.

The manager would initially serve for up to 12 months, with the High Court able to approve a further term of up to 12 months

The proposed law also expands grounds on which the CBK could suspend or revoke a payment licence, including failure to meet minimum capital or infrastructure requirements and failure to implement corrective measures ordered by the regulator.

“The Central Bank may suspend or revoke a licence granted under this Act if the payment service provider or payment system operator, without the approval of the Central Bank, amalgamates with another person,” says the Bill, referencing the process of combining separate companies into one entity.

“… transfers, assigns or encumbers its licence to another person and fails to manage its agents in a manner consistent with this Act.”

The CBK wants to strengthen the stability of Kenya's rapidly expanding digital payments sector.

Payment services are becoming more interconnected, with mobile money, banks, fintechs and payment gateways processing growing volumes of transactions.

Kenya had 54 million mobile money subscriptions as of June 2026, up 13.2 percent from 47.7 million in June last year, according to data from the Communications Authority. The leading mobile money platform, Safaricom’s M-Pesa, transacted Sh41.7 trillion in the year to March 2026.

The proposed powers would give the CBK tools to act before problems at a payment company threaten customers’ cash or spread to other parts of the financial system.

They mirror countries like Ireland, Thailand and the UK, where the regulators can access payment providers’ premises, inspect records, and seize data unannounced.

Rwanda’s central bank can disqualify or request the immediate removal of a payment firm’s CEO or director if they fail to uphold rules, go bankrupt, or are convicted of offences involving dishonesty or fraud.

Kenya’s Bill is undergoing public participation until October 9.

# Context-enrichment: Kenya's central bank seeks powers to take over struggling fintechs
_Analytical notes (not a post). Importance: 3/5._

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
This is a **draft Bill at the public-participation stage, not an enacted law**. The National Payment System Bill, 2026 — jointly drafted by the CBK and the National Treasury and published ~22 Sep 2026 — would replace the 15-year-old National Payment System Act, 2011. Public participation ran to **9 Oct 2026** (the businessdailyafrica piece says 9 Oct; it is dated 1 Oct 2026, so the headline "seeks powers" = a proposal out for comment, nothing in force).

De-PR'd substance of the resolution/intervention powers:
- CBK officers may **enter and inspect PSP/PSO premises with or without notice**, interview staff, access computers/data; obstruction/false info becomes an offence.
- CBK may **direct removal of any officer/employee** it deems to have caused a breach or a deterioration in the firm's stability.
- In a crisis CBK may **appoint a statutory manager** (up to 12 months, extendable up to another 12 by the High Court) and a protected director who cannot be removed without CBK approval — i.e. **take over management and oversee a sale** of a failing payment firm.
- Expanded grounds to **suspend/revoke a licence**: failure to meet minimum capital/infrastructure, failure to implement corrective measures, or amalgamating/transferring the licence without CBK approval.

→ **Why framed as "take over struggling fintechs":** that is the single most dramatic clause, but it is one slice of a much broader overhaul. The same Bill introduces **open finance / mandatory customer-data sharing** (with consent) and **rules on how e-money float is held** (Safaricom/Airtel cannot treat wallet balances as their own money; funds held in trust, investable only in Kenyan govt securities / interest-bearing trust accounts). The resolution powers are the "stick"; the data-sharing and trust-fund rules are the structural core. News framing anchors on the raid/ouster clause because it is the most sensational, which **understates** that this is really a payments-sector constitution rewrite.
→ **Why now:** 54m mobile-money subscriptions (Jun 2026, +13.2% y/y); M-Pesa alone moved Sh41.7tn in the year to Mar 2026. The system is systemically important ("too big to fail"), yet the 2011 Act predates digital banks, BNPL, gateways and super-app wallets. CBK explicitly wants tools to **act before a failure spreads** — a resolution regime, mirroring Ireland/Thailand/UK (unannounced inspection) and Rwanda (forced CEO removal).

## [1] Competitors / peers (regulatory precedents, not corporate)
- **Rwanda** — central bank can already disqualify/force immediate removal of a payment firm's CEO/director for rule breaches, bankruptcy or fraud convictions. Kenya is explicitly copying this.
- **Ireland / Thailand / UK** — regulators can enter premises, inspect records and seize data unannounced. Kenya's "with or without notice" clause is modelled on these.
- **Nigeria (CBN)** — already fines/sanctions PSPs (see [[Zest Payments fined CBN N2.7M over delayed 2023 audit report]]); Nigeria is also building public payment rails (see [[Nigeria launches open-source National Payment Stack]]). Kenya's move is the governance analogue.
- → **Position:** Kenya is **catching up to peer bank-grade resolution regimes**, not pioneering. The novel part for Kenya is extending *bank-style* intervention (statutory managers, protected directors) to *non-bank payment firms* — the same regime that already governs deposit-takers.
- → **Second-order:** bringing PSPs under a quasi-banking resolution regime raises the compliance/capital bar and favours scaled incumbents (M-Pesa, Airtel Money, Equitel, Pesapal, Loop — 43 licensed PSPs as of Jun 2026) over sub-scale startups, mildly **consolidating** the market.

## [2] Company/regulator history / fit
CBK under Gov. **Kamau Thugge** has an escalating enforcement track record:
- **2025:** revoked PayU Kenya's payment licence (Oct 2025) after the Prosus-owned firm entered liquidation — see [[Central Bank of Kenya revokes PayU Kenya license]] / [[Central Bank of Kenya strips PayU Kenya's payment license]]. That episode showed CBK could only act *after* collapse; the new Bill is designed to let it act *before*.
- **Digital-credit regime:** CBK has licensed ~281 Digital Credit Providers since the Mar 2022 DCP Regulations (900+ applications), after years of complaints over predatory rates, data misuse and aggressive debt collection. The NPS Bill extends that "bring the shadow sector under supervision" logic from lenders to the whole payment stack.
- Parallel reform track: Kenya's VASP/crypto bill (see [[Kenya National Assembly approves VASP bill at committee stage]], [[Kenya's crypto bill passes assembly stage]]) — same period, same "regulate the fast-growing digital-finance frontier" impulse.
→ **Why CBK acts this way:** a systemically important mobile-money system sits on a pre-smartphone-era law; a single large-wallet failure would be a national crisis (see [[Cofounders Notebook If M-Pesa breaks, Kenya stops]], [[This Week in Fintech The case for M-PESA becoming a bank]]). The structural pressure is **systemic-risk containment**, not routine conduct policing.

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
- **Really new for Kenya:** a **resolution regime for non-bank payment firms** (statutory manager, protected director, forced sale) + **unannounced inspection** + **open-finance data-sharing mandate** + **wallet-float investment rules**. Individually these exist elsewhere; as a bundle for Kenyan PSPs they are new.
- **Traction = ZERO.** This is a draft under public participation (closed 9 Oct 2026); **no new powers are live, nothing has been "taken over".** It must still clear drafting, Parliament and assent. Treat every clause as "proposed", not operative.
- → **Where the real value/risk sits:** the headline ouster/raid power is politically loud but the **open-finance data-sharing** and **wallet-interest / trust-fund** clauses are what could actually reshape economics — forcing Safaricom/Airtel to open customer data to third parties (threatening M-Pesa's data moat) and to pass float returns/interest to users or into govt securities. The margin question shifts from "will CBK seize a fintch" to **"who captures the value of M-Pesa's data and float once it is pried open"** — likely banks, new TPPs and the Treasury (via govt-securities placement), at Safaricom's expense.

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
- **Process risk:** public participation to 9 Oct 2026, then Cabinet/Parliament; material amendment or dilution of the harshest clauses is plausible given incumbent lobbying (Safaricom is Kenya's largest company and M-Pesa ~Sh41.7tn/yr throughput gives it heavy political weight).
- Former CBK gov Patrick Njoroge has publicly pushed for wallet-holders to earn returns on trust funds — signalling the trust-fund clause has establishment backing, harder to strip out.
- **Risks / open:** "with or without notice" raids + forced executive removal raise due-process and investor-confidence concerns for foreign-owned PSPs (the PayU exit is a cautionary data point on Kenya as a market). Over-reach could chill non-bank entry; under-enforcement leaves systemic risk.
- → **Counterintuitive second-order:** a stronger resolution regime can *attract* responsible capital (clearer failure path = lower systemic uncertainty) even as it *raises* the bar for sub-scale fintechs — net effect is likely **consolidation toward a smaller set of well-capitalised, bank-adjacent payment firms**, with M-Pesa's dominance partly offset by forced data-opening.

## Top challenge / red-team questions

1. **Is it enacted or just proposed?** — PROPOSED. Draft National Payment System Bill, 2026; public participation closed 9 Oct 2026. No powers are live. The headline "seeks powers" is literally a Bill out for comment. (answered)
2. **Is it really new, or a re-run of the PayU revocation?** — FRESH. Distinct event. PayU (Oct/Dec 2025) was an *actual licence revocation after collapse*; this is a *forward-looking resolution Bill* so CBK can act *before* collapse. Same regulator (CBK/Thugge), different action. (answered)
3. **Is "take over struggling fintechs" the real story?** — Only one clause. The Bill also mandates open-finance data-sharing and rewrites wallet-float/trust-fund rules; the resolution power is the loudest but not the structurally biggest piece. News framing understates scope. (answered)
4. **How broad is the inspection power?** — "With or without notice" entry, staff interviews, data/computer access; obstruction = offence. Modelled on Ireland/Thailand/UK. (answered)
5. **What exactly triggers a takeover?** — Failure to meet customer obligations, default to other providers/CBK, ignoring a CBK directive, or breaching the Act; statutory manager up to 12m (+12m via High Court). (answered)
6. **What's the prior art?** — Rwanda (forced CEO/director removal), Ireland/Thailand/UK (unannounced inspection). Kenya is catching up, not pioneering. (answered)
7. **Who does it hit hardest?** — Sub-scale and foreign-owned PSPs (higher capital/compliance bar, due-process exposure); 43 licensed PSPs as of Jun 2026. Likely mild consolidation toward incumbents. (answered)
8. **What does it mean for M-Pesa/Safaricom specifically?** — Biggest real impact is NOT the takeover clause but open-finance data-sharing (threatens M-Pesa data moat) + trust-fund/wallet-interest rules (forces float into govt securities / returns to users). (answered — this shifts the central question)
9. **Who captures the value once M-Pesa data/float is opened?** — Banks, new TPPs, and the Treasury (via mandated govt-securities placement of float) — at Safaricom's margin. (analysis)
10. **Will the harsh clauses survive Parliament?** — Open. Safaricom's political weight (Sh41.7tn/yr throughput, largest listed firm) makes dilution plausible; but ex-gov Njoroge backs the trust-fund/returns clause, so that part is stickier. (partly open)
11. **Any BNPL-specific angle (per the search query)?** — Not in this Bill specifically; BNPL/digital credit sits under the separate DCP regime (~281 licensed DCPs since Mar 2022). This Bill governs PSPs/PSOs, not lenders. (answered — corrects the query framing)
12. **Due-process / investor-confidence risk?** — Unannounced raids + forced executive removal raise concerns for foreign PSPs; PayU's exit is a cautionary signal on Kenya's market. (open on net effect)
13. **Does it mirror bank resolution regimes?** — Yes: statutory manager + protected director + forced sale is the deposit-taker playbook now extended to non-bank payment firms. (answered)
14. **Scale justification?** — 54m mobile-money subs (Jun 2026, +13.2% y/y); system is "too big to fail." Systemic-risk containment is the real driver. (answered)
15. **What's the downside trigger for the thesis?** — If Parliament guts the resolution/data clauses, the Bill becomes mostly a capital/licensing tidy-up; if passed intact, it is a genuine payments-sector constitution rewrite. (open)

Importance: 3/5 — A substantive, systemically-relevant regulatory overhaul of Kenya's payments sector (open finance + resolution regime + wallet-float rules), affecting M-Pesa/Airtel and 43 PSPs. Capped at 3 because it is a DRAFT at public-participation stage (no powers live, no traction, Parliament/assent still pending and dilution plausible), it is a regional (single-country) story, and the specific "take over struggling fintechs" headline is the least structurally important clause while following well-trodden peer precedent (Rwanda/UK/Ireland). Fresh, not a PayU re-run.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** Subsector: payments/mobile-money regulation (regtech layer over PSPs and payment system operators), not a company deal — no valuation/round/metrics attach. Scale of what's being regulated: Kenya had 54M mobile-money subscriptions as of June 2026 (+13.2% YoY from 47.7M), and M-Pesa alone processed ~Sh41.7tn (≈KES41.68tn, ~46bn transactions) in the year to March 2026 — a flow larger than Kenya's ~KES15tn GDP (per Safaricom FY26 / techweez, as of 2026-05-07). Structure: concentrated at the top (43 CBK-licensed PSPs as of June 2026; M-Pesa, Airtel Money, Equitel, Pesapal, Loop named) but with a long tail of smaller wallets/gateways — the regulatory concern is tail-firm failure contaminating customer float. Drivers/"why now": (1) the governing law is the 2011 National Payment System Act — pre-dates the modern fintech stack and is silent on resolution/takeover, so the sector has outgrown its framework; (2) interconnectedness — mobile money, banks, fintechs and gateways now settle into each other, so one PSP's default propagates (systemic-risk framing is explicit in the Bill). This is a de-PR'd regulatory proposal, not an enacted law: published for public participation 21 Sep 2026, consultation closed 9 Oct 2026 (afropolitain/lawyard).

**Competitive landscape.** This is regulation, not a competitive contest — the relevant "KPIs" are the Bill's thresholds. New minimum capital (per kenyanwallstreet/techspace, as of 2026-09): e-money issuers KES250M, e-wallet providers & merchant acquirers KES50M, remittance providers KES30M, payment-initiation / account-information service providers KES5M each. New CBK tools: inspect premises with or without notice; direct removal of any officer/employee who caused a breach or a deterioration in stability; appoint a statutory manager (up to 12 months, extendable up to a further 12 by the High Court) to run and orchestrate the sale of a failing firm; appoint a CBK director to the board (protected from removal without CBK approval); suspend/revoke licences for undercapitalisation, un-approved amalgamation or licence transfer. Positioning of the protagonist (CBK): catching up to peer regimes — the article itself cites Ireland, Thailand and the UK (unannounced access/data seizure) and Rwanda (central bank can disqualify/remove a payment firm's CEO/director on failure, bankruptcy or dishonesty conviction) as templates. Recent regional moves: Nigeria's CBN revoked 46 microfinance licences effective 1 Jul 2026 under BOFIA and published a fintech roadmap (Feb 2026); Kenya's CBK expanded licensed digital-credit providers to 227 after approving 32 in Apr 2026 (per ecofinagency/techpoint). The African direction of travel is uniformly toward tighter, resolution-capable oversight.

**Risk flags.**
1. Regulatory/compliance-cost concentration: a KES250M e-money-issuer capital floor is a hard barrier that favours incumbents (M-Pesa, Airtel Money) and can force exit/consolidation of smaller wallets and gateways — net-reducing competition in a market already top-heavy (second-order: less tail competition is the opposite of the stated "protect customers" intent).
2. Governance/independence risk: unannounced raids, discretionary removal of executives ("in the opinion of the Central Bank"), and a CBK-appointed board director shielded from removal concentrate broad, subjectively-triggered power in the regulator — scope for arbitrary or politically-tinged intervention; investor-perception risk for VC-backed Kenyan fintechs.
3. Execution/bill risk: this is a draft in consultation (closed 9 Oct 2026), not law — scope, thresholds and takeover triggers can still shift materially before (and if) it is enacted; nothing is live yet.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** Direction, not re-rating: Kenya is building a bank-style resolution regime for payments, mirroring Nigeria/Rwanda — expect the licensed-PSP count to shrink via capital-driven consolidation, not grow (analysis). Falsifiable thesis: if enacted roughly as drafted, within ~12–24 months at least one sub-scale Kenyan wallet/gateway exits or is absorbed citing the KES250M/KES50M capital floors (analysis).
