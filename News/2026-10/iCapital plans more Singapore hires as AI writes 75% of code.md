Fintech platform iCapital plans more Singapore hires even as AI takes on 75% of coding work
The firm plans to hire more product and engineering talent in the Republic

 Lawrence Calcano, CEO of iCapital, says AI now writes 75% of the firm’s code, spanning code generation, testing and quality assurance. PHOTO: ICAPITAL 
[SINGAPORE] Global fintech platform iCapital plans to hire more product and engineering talent in Singapore, even as it steps up its use of artificial intelligence to improve efficiency and expand operations.
AI now writes 75 per cent of the firm’s code, spanning code generation, testing and quality assurance, CEO Lawrence Calcano told The Business Times .
“I think it is a significant opportunity for us to be more efficient and deliver more capabilities and user friendliness to clients,” he said.
The AI push comes as the alternatives sector undergoes a structural shift, with iCapital’s 2026 Asia Advisor Survey finding that investors are moving beyond basic access to private markets to building more sophisticated portfolios supported by analytical tools.
Calcano said that in the past, the focus was on creating access to private markets. Now, iCapital is seeking to grow a larger group of advisers and therefore more clients into alternative investments.
“We are at a really interesting point in the cycle of adoption, and I think a lot of the newer advisers in the asset class today are dealing with (the next wave of) clients,” he said.

These clients are seeking more analytics and education so that they can make informed decisions, moving beyond the reporting capabilities that were a priority of large institutions that were early adopters of alternative investments, he added.
Technology is key
Technology plays a big role in delivering these capabilities at scale, Calcano pointed out.
Since its inception, iCapital has spent over US$1 billion on technology to address pain points in education, access, administration and reporting, and liquidity solutions.

The firm’s survey found that risk analytics has become the top technology priority in Asia, with compliance cited as the fastest-growing challenge in the region.
As a result, the questions asked of a fund manager of alternatives increasingly focus on exposure, liquidity and regulatory fit, rather than the availability of a specific solution.
Calcano noted that the firm has to be flexible to client needs, as some require a full range of services while others need a bespoke solution. “You need to be involved in both, and have big technology around that and nimble technology.”
Alternatives now a “core holding”
Alternatives, Calcano said, are becoming more like a “core holding”.
“Even though people had more pessimistic views of the global economy, they still had planned to sustain or increase their allocation (to) alternatives,” he said.
“People should be incorporating these as part of their core overall portfolio, even though they might be worried,” he added, highlighting that investors now understand the fact that these assets can help both drive potentially incremental returns and manage risk.
The survey found that more than a quarter of advisers now plan to increase allocations to alternatives over the next 12 months, even as confidence in the economic outlook has weakened.
But within alternative investments, investor preferences are also shifting.
Calcano noted growing interest in real assets among Asian investors, who recognise the role funds play in the growth of this business.
Meanwhile, private credit, the leading area of interest in 2025 and the defining feature of last year’s Asia findings, fell by 23 percentage points.
Calcano said that investors have been concerned about private credit due to the economic climate and the disruption of technology companies due to AI.
“Sentiment, emotion still drives behaviour, and I think (we) saw some of that. What I think people are looking at very carefully right now are the actual credit metrics and credit statistics to try to figure out if there are real significant risks above expectations.
“The question is: Are there underperforming credits in excess of what a portfolio manager or an asset manager would have expected out of the portfolio? I don’t think I have seen that yet,” he said, adding that this has helped ease some concerns.
Singapore a key hub
Singapore remains an important hub for iCapital’s international operations, said Brooks Entwistle, the firm’s newly appointed head of international.
“It has a little bit of everything… all of our functional expertise, our sales team, and everything we bring,” he said.
Beyond its current alternative offerings, the firm is also looking at additional products such as annuities and insurance, and will look to establish capabilities in Singapore or Zurich to support clients in these areas when the time comes.
“And so Singapore is by no means an outpost,” Entwistle noted. “It is actually a big, important centre, which I think shows the commitment.”

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** Alternatives/private-markets wealthtech sits inside a market Preqin forecasts to reach ~$32tn global alternatives AUM by 2030, up from $16.8tn at end-2023 (per Preqin "Private Markets in 2030," via theasset.com, 2025) — a ~CAGR in the high-single digits, with the private-wealth channel (advisers, HNW, family offices) the fastest-growing source of new money. The structural "why now": institutions are already allocated, so platform growth has to come from the retail/adviser channel, which is operationally hard to serve (education, subscription docs, reporting, liquidity). That is exactly the pain iCapital monetises — note it says the firm has spent >$1bn on technology since inception on "education, access, administration, reporting and liquidity." Barriers are high: capital (to build infra + acquire), fund-manager relationships (feeder/structuring), and distribution into wirehouses/banks — favouring a few scaled aggregators. Note the sentiment wrinkle iCapital itself flags: private credit interest fell 23pp YoY in its 2026 Asia survey amid AI-driven disruption of tech-company credits — a reminder the TAM is cyclical, not a straight line.

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs: platform/alternative AUM, number of advisers & wealth firms on-platform, transaction volume growth, and (increasingly) share of AUM in proprietary vs third-party product. Key players: **iCapital** (the scale leader), **CAIS** (its closest US adviser-channel rival), **Moonfare** (direct-to-HNW/PE, Europe-led), **Allfunds** (fund-distribution rail, now being bought), **Yieldstreet** (retail alts). Basis of competition = distribution reach + breadth of fund menu + tech/reporting depth, not price. Recent moves: Deutsche Börse agreed to acquire Allfunds for €5.3bn ([[Deutsche Börse inks €5.3bn Allfunds acquisition agreement]], Jan 2026) — strategic buyers now paying up for distribution rails; CAIS raised $170m Series D at >$2bn (Jul 2026, fintech.global); Moonfare passed €4bn AUM with ~20% of H1-2026 inflows into proprietary product (Bloomberg, Jul 2026). iCapital's own recent trajectory: [[iCapital raises $820M at $7.5B valuation]] (Jul 2025), [[iCapital to acquire Passthrough investor onboarding platform]] (Feb 2026), [[iCapital appoints Brooks Entwistle as Head of International]] (Jun 2026), and [[iCapital taps Anthropic's Claude for AI adviser tools]] (May 2026) — the AI-coding story is consistent with that stack build-out. Position: **clear category leader** — ~$257bn alternative platform assets / ~$945bn–$1.2tn serviced assets reported (via Yahoo Finance/Wikipedia, 2025–26) dwarfs CAIS (advisers overseeing ~$8.5tn end-client assets but far smaller on-platform alts) and Moonfare (€4bn). Moat (analysis): scale + fund-manager network effects + adviser switching costs.

**Risk flags.**
1. **The "AI writes 75% of code" claim is largely unverifiable and likely over-stated.** Peer self-reports (e.g. Google's 75%) describe AI-*suggested, human-accepted* lines, not autonomous output; independent measurement (GitClear, ~25% of commits) sits far lower, and studies show adoption rarely converts to measured delivery velocity (the "AI productivity paradox," faros.ai 2026). Treat as a PR/recruiting signal, not an opex-reduction fact — note iCapital is *still hiring* engineers, which contradicts a pure efficiency narrative.
2. **Cyclicality / private-credit sentiment.** iCapital's own 2026 Asia survey shows private-credit interest down 23pp YoY on AI-disruption fears about underlying borrowers; alts inflows — the firm's revenue driver — are sensitive to a credit-cycle turn and to any visible NAV/mark stress in feeder funds.
3. **APAC execution & regulatory fit.** Singapore/Zurich build-out into annuities/insurance adds regulated-product complexity; the survey names compliance the fastest-growing regional challenge — margin and timeline risk on new-product expansion into unfamiliar regulatory regimes.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** (analysis) This is an expansion/positioning story, not a re-rating: a leader doubling down on APAC distribution while using an "AI-efficiency" narrative to justify continued (not reduced) hiring — consolidation of the adviser-channel alts stack continues (Allfunds taken out; CAIS re-capitalised). Falsifiable thesis: if AI genuinely replaces 75% of dev work, headcount/opex should flatten even as capabilities grow — watch iCapital's hiring pace and any disclosed cost-to-serve. It breaks if the firm keeps adding engineers at historical rates (as this very note implies), confirming the 75% figure is marketing. Trigger to watch: private-credit mark stress across platform feeder funds, or an Apac regulatory setback on the annuities/insurance push.
