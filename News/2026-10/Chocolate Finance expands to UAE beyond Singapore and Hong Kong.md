# Chocolate Finance expands to UAE beyond Singapore and Hong Kong

 Chocolate Finance has entered the UAE market, extending its investment services beyond Singapore and Hong Kong.
UAE residents and international non-residents can now open accounts and invest in UAE dirhams (AED), US dollars (USD) or both.
Customers can earn returns of 4.4 percent per annum on their first AED 100,000 or US$25,000 respectively, with returns of up to 4.4 percent on amounts exceeding those thresholds.
Chocolate Finance’s top-up programme applies to investments within the stated thresholds, subject to its terms and conditions.
The offering has no minimum investment requirements or lock-in periods.
Founder Walter de Oude explained that the UAE offering operates under a different investment structure from its Singapore and Hong Kong businesses.
The UAE business uses the company’s proprietary Chocolate Global Income Fund instead of investing through third-party funds.
According to de Oude, this structure lowers operating costs and allows the company to offer slightly higher returns to customers.
Chocolate Finance said both the company and its fund are licensed and regulated by the UAE’s Capital Market Authority.
The company cautioned that invested capital is at risk and past performance does not indicate future returns.

## Контекст

<!-- enrichment:context -->
**[0] What happened.** Chocolate Finance, the Singapore cash-management app founded in 2022 by Walter de Oude (also founder of insurer Singlife), launched in the UAE (reported 2026-10-21 via [fintechnews.sg](https://fintechnews.sg/139205/wealthtech/chocolate-finance-uae/)) — its third market after Singapore and Hong Kong. UAE residents and international non-residents can open accounts and invest in AED, USD, or both, earning up to 4.4% p.a. (headline rate applies to the first AED 100,000 / US$25,000, with "up to 4.4%" above those thresholds, subject to a top-up programme). No minimums, no lock-ins.

**[1] Why it's different from SG/HK.** Unlike the Singapore and Hong Kong operations — which route customer cash through third-party fixed-income funds — the UAE offering runs on Chocolate's own proprietary **Chocolate Global Income Fund**. De Oude says this in-house structure lowers operating costs and lets the firm offer "slightly higher returns." This is a vertical-integration step: Chocolate is becoming its own fund manager abroad, not just a distribution front-end. Both the company and the fund are stated to be "licensed and regulated by the UAE's Capital Market Authority." (Caveat: the UAE's federal securities regulator is the **Securities and Commodities Authority (SCA)**; DIFC is regulated by the DFSA and ADGM by the FSRA. "Capital Market Authority" is the name of Saudi Arabia's regulator — the source's phrasing may be imprecise about which UAE licensing body actually applies. Unverified.)

**[2] Prior arc / background.** Chocolate scaled fast in Singapore to ~100k users and ~S$1bn AUM by early 2025, but in **March 2025** suffered a classic confidence run: after it quietly pared back a "2 miles per dollar" rewards scheme (and suspended AXS debit-card payments) customers pulled **~S$500M in about two weeks — ~40% of AUM** — forcing it to suspend instant withdrawals (now a 3-day standard process; instant withdrawals "not part of the recipe"). It recovered to ~S$900M AUM and in **July 2025 raised US$15M** (Nikko Asset Management, Peak XV, Prosus, Saison Capital, de Oude) to fund regional expansion (prior round: US$19M in 2022). It later scaled to ~S$1.1bn AUM targeting profitability by 2027. **Hong Kong launched Q1 2026** (see [[Chocolate Finance launches in Hong Kong after Singapore debut]], [[Chocolate Finance raises $15M and gets Hong Kong approval]]). De Oude has said expansion would be "slightly less aggressive" on promotions post-March.

**[3] Market context.** The UAE is a crowded digital-wealth / cash-management arena: **StashAway** (DIFC/DFSA), **Sarwa** (ADGM/FSRA; Sarwa Save ~3.7-4%), **Baraka** (ADGM + DFSA), plus bank money-market funds. UAE money-market instruments yield ~4-5% in 2026, so Chocolate's 4.4% is competitive but not an outlier; its edge is the no-minimum/no-lock-in UX and self-managed fund economics. DIFC had ~592 wealth/asset-management firms in H1 2026; Abu Dhabi (ADGM) AUM grew 57% YoY in Q1 2026 — a deliberate target market for yield-seeking expat cash.

**[4] So what.** First Middle East beachhead and first deployment of Chocolate's own fund vehicle — a strategic shift from distributor to manufacturer. Targeting non-residents (not just UAE residents) signals an offshore-savings play for a global expat base, a bigger TAM than domestic retail. Watch: which UAE body actually licenses it, whether proprietary-fund economics truly beat third-party funds net of risk, and whether the March-2025 liquidity lesson shapes a more conservative launch.

## Челлендж / ред-тим

<!-- enrichment:challenge -->
**Red-team (skeptical; never invent):**

1. **Is the regulator claim accurate?** OPEN/SUSPECT. Source says "UAE's Capital Market Authority." The UAE's actual federal securities regulator is the **Securities and Commodities Authority (SCA)**; free zones use DFSA (DIFC) or FSRA (ADGM). "Capital Market Authority" is Saudi Arabia's regulator. Likely a source imprecision — the specific UAE licensing body is unverified.

2. **Is 4.4% a real floor or a promo hook?** PARTLY OPEN. Headline 4.4% applies to the first AED 100k / US$25k and is tied to a "top-up programme subject to terms." Rate above the threshold is "up to 4.4%" — i.e. variable, not guaranteed. Capital explicitly "at risk."

3. **Is this a genuinely new event vs. the Jan 2026 HK launch?** ANSWERED — yes. HK (Q1 2026) and the March-2025 run are distinct; UAE is a net-new market. Fresh.

4. **Does a proprietary fund add risk vs. third-party funds?** OPEN. Self-manufacturing the Chocolate Global Income Fund removes third-party diversification/oversight and concentrates credit, duration and liquidity risk in-house — the exact axis that broke in March 2025.

5. **Could UAE suffer a March-2025-style run?** OPEN. Same model (cash parked in a bond fund marketed as near-cash) + non-resident money that can flee fast = latent run risk if yields wobble or sentiment turns.

6. **What's the AUM/user traction in UAE?** UNKNOWN. No figures disclosed; launch-stage.

7. **"Non-residents" — regulatory/tax exposure?** OPEN. Soliciting international non-residents into a UAE fund raises cross-border marketing/KYC/tax-reporting questions not addressed in the source.

8. **Is 4.4% actually competitive?** ANSWERED — marginally. UAE MMFs yield ~4-5%; Sarwa ~3.7-4%. Edge is UX (no min / no lock-in) and cost, not raw yield.

9. **Is the "lower cost → higher return" claim verifiable?** OPEN. De Oude's assertion; no expense-ratio or fund-fact disclosure cited.

10. **Single-source risk?** PARTLY. Core facts rest on fintechnews.sg (which reads as a company-announcement rewrite). Background corroborated by CNA/finews.asia. No independent UAE outlet confirmation yet.

11. **Does "both company and fund licensed" mean retail-authorised?** OPEN. Licensing ≠ public-offer authorisation; the distribution permission scope is unstated.

12. **Why UAE now?** ANSWERED (inferred). Post-$15M expansion mandate; UAE is a large expat yield-seeking pool with booming DIFC/ADGM wealth sector.

13. **Does this change the Singapore/HK proposition?** NO evidence it does; SG/HK still on third-party funds.

14. **Reputational carryover from March 2025?** OPEN. The run is well-documented; a UAE launch on an unproven in-house fund invites "did they learn the lesson?" scrutiny.

**Importance: 3/5** — Meaningful strategic milestone (3rd market, first proprietary fund vehicle, offshore/non-resident play) for a notable Singapore wealthtech with a cautionary run history. But it's an early-stage launch, zero disclosed traction, single-source, with a possibly-garbled regulator claim — not a market-moving event on its own.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** UAE/GCC wealthtech is in a land-grab phase driven by (a) a high-rate environment (CBUAE overnight deposit rate 3.90% as of 17 Sep 2026, per StashAway via investingintheweb) that makes cash-management yield products easy to market, and (b) zero-tax-residency-fuelled millionaire migration into the UAE (per GlobeNewswire/Research&Markets, MENA Wealth Management Report 2026, 19 Mar 2026). Market size is small but fast: UAE WealthTech solutions ~US$25.62m (2023) → US$89.41m (2031 est.), CAGR ~16.9% (per marketsandata, as of 2024-2031 forecast); treat the absolute figure as a narrow "solutions" slice, not the full managed-assets pool — the AUM pool is much larger. Structure: fragmented, low switching costs, product-led competition on headline yield. Entry barrier is regulatory (ADGM/DFSA/CMA licensing) more than capital. Why now: rates near a peak + resident/HNWI influx = a window to grab cash balances before rate cuts compress the 4.4% headline.

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs: AUM/client assets, net yield after fees, cost-to-serve, customer count, lock-in/liquidity terms. Key players and basis of competition (all compete on net yield + liquidity + "beats the bank"): Sarwa (first UAE-founded fintech to cross US$1bn client assets, May 2026; Sarwa Save ~3.7–4% gross, nets ~3.2–3.5% after 0.5% fee, per stashaway.ae); StashAway (regional, MENA + Asia; StashAway Simple ~3.7% net / ~4.3% gross fund yield less 0.63% fees, as of 31 Jul 2026); Baraka (DFSA-licensed, more trading/ETF than cash). Recent moves: Sarwa US$1bn milestone (May 2026). Protagonist's position: new entrant, not incumbent — Chocolate arrives with a Singapore track record (~S$1.1bn AUM, 100k+ customers in its first full commercial year, per fintechnews.sg) but zero UAE base. Differentiator = its proprietary Chocolate Global Income Fund (vertical integration vs third-party funds in SG/HK), which it says lowers cost and lifts yield. Moat (analysis): thin — the only durable edge is cost-structure from owning the fund; headline 4.4% is not a moat since peers' net yields already cluster ~3.2–3.7% and the gap narrows after Chocolate's own fees (undisclosed for UAE).

**Comps & multiples.** No valuation/round attaches to this UAE-launch news, so trading multiples are not computable — distribution not computed, qualitative comparison only. Internal comps (same company, history): [[Chocolate Finance raises $15M and gets Hong Kong approval]] (Jul 2025, Series A+ at ~US$702m AUM; investors incl. Peak XV, Prosus, Nikko AM, Saison) and [[Chocolate Finance launches in Hong Kong after Singapore debut]] (Jan 2026). Unit-economics reference (de-PR): for the 16-month period to 30 Apr 2025, Chocolate posted ~S$5.3m revenue against a ~S$6.55m net loss (per fintechnews.sg/dealstreetasia) — i.e. loss-making, targeting profitability by 2027. External peer valuations: Sarwa/StashAway last-round post-money not verifiable here → [UNSOURCED]. Yield "multiple" sanity: 4.4% headline vs peer net ~3.2–3.7% looks rich on the headline but is pre-Chocolate-fee and capped at the first AED 100k / US$25k — effective marginal yield above the cap is "up to 4.4%", so the real spread vs peers is likely in-line, not superior (analysis).

**Risk flags.**
1. Trust/liquidity overhang (why): Chocolate suspended instant withdrawals in Mar 2025 (processing stretched to 3–10 working days) after a bank-run-style surge — entering a new market where it has no brand equity means any repeat liquidity event has no reservoir of UAE customer goodwill to absorb it.
2. Proprietary-fund concentration (why): the UAE book runs entirely on the in-house Chocolate Global Income Fund, not third-party funds — self-managed credit/duration risk plus the conflict of being both distributor and fund manager; "capital at risk" is explicitly flagged, and the 4.4% is not a guaranteed deposit rate.
3. Yield-compression / rate-cut exposure (why): the whole pitch leans on a 3.90% policy rate; when CBUAE follows a Fed easing cycle, the 4.4% headline is hard to hold, and a loss-making entrant may have to choose between margin and a marketing number.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** This is a new-entrant move into a crowded-but-growing GCC cash-yield segment, not a re-rating (analysis). Falsifiable thesis: Chocolate's vertically-integrated fund lets it under-price Sarwa/StashAway on net yield at scale; it's wrong if UAE net yield (after Chocolate's undisclosed fee) lands inside the peer 3.2–3.7% band — watch for the first disclosed UAE net-of-fee figure and UAE AUM traction 2–3 quarters out as the trigger. Second thing to watch: whether instant-withdrawal is offered in UAE from day one (it was the exact feature that broke in SG).
