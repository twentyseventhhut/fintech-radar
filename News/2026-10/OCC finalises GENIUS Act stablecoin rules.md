 OCC Bulletin 2026-50 
 | 
 October 21, 2026 
 
GENIUS Act Regulations: Final Rule
Share This Page:

To
Chief Executive Officers of All National Banks, Federal Savings Associations, and Federal Branches and Agencies; Department and Division Heads; All Examining Personnel; and Other Interested Parties
Summary
The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) is issuing a final rule to implement the Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins (GENIUS) Act regarding the issuance of payment stablecoins and certain related activities by entities subject to the OCC’s jurisdiction.
The final rule will be effective on January 18, 2027.
Note for Community Banks
The final rule will apply to national banks and their subsidiaries, federal savings associations and their subsidiaries, federal branches and their subsidiaries, foreign payment stablecoin issuers, nonbank entities that seek to be or are approved as federal qualified payment stablecoin issuers, and state qualified payment stablecoin issuers for whom the OCC has regulatory or enforcement authority pursuant to the GENIUS Act.
Highlights
The final rule addresses all of the regulations the OCC is required to promulgate under the GENIUS Act other than those related to the Bank Secrecy Act, anti-money laundering, and Office of Foreign Assets Control sanctions, which will be addressed in a separate rulemaking in coordination with the Department of the Treasury.
The final rule incorporates or addresses the comments the OCC received following the notice of proposed rulemaking. 1 
The majority of the GENIUS Act OCC rules are addressed in a new 12 CFR 15, which includes standards and requirements related to the following:

 
 Activities
Reserve assets
Redemption
Risk management
Audits, reports, and supervision
State qualified payment stablecoin issuers transitioning to the federal regulatory framework
Custody
Applications and registrations for permitted payment stablecoin issuers subject to OCC jurisdiction
Examination and supervision of foreign issuers
Revocation or recission of approval of a permitted payment stablecoin issuer in certain instances
Capital and operational backstop
The final rule also revises the capital adequacy standards in 12 CFR 3, prompt corrective action regulations in 12 CFR 6, assessment of fees in 12 CFR 8, and rules of practice and procedure in 12 CFR 19.
Background
The GENIUS Act was enacted on July 18, 2025. It establishes a regulatory framework for payment stablecoin activities. The GENIUS Act generally prohibits any person other than a permitted payment stablecoin issuer from issuing a payment stablecoin in the United States. The act further prohibits digital asset service providers from offering or selling a payment stablecoin to a person in the United States unless the issuer is a permitted payment stablecoin issuer or the issuer is a foreign payment stablecoin issuer that meets certain requirements. The act also sets forth various regulatory and licensing requirements for permitted payment stablecoin issuers and foreign payment stablecoin issuers.
The OCC will have regulatory or enforcement authority over certain permitted payment stablecoin issuers, including subsidiaries of national banks or federal savings associations, federal qualified payment stablecoin issuers, and state qualified payment stablecoin issuers subject to the OCC’s regulatory or enforcement authority under sections 4 or 7 of the GENIUS Act (12 USC 5903 and 5906). In addition, the OCC will have regulatory authority over foreign payment stablecoin issuers. The final rule generally sets forth the regulations that will apply to permitted payment stablecoin issuers and foreign payment stablecoin issuers under the OCC’s jurisdiction as well as certain custody activities conducted by OCC-supervised entities.
The GENIUS Act’s effective date is January 18, 2027. The OCC published a notice of proposed rulemaking to implement GENIUS Act regulations on February 25, 2026. That notice of proposed rulemaking appeared in the Federal Register on March 2, 2026. 2 The OCC received comments from various groups and individuals in response to the proposal.

Senior Deputy Comptroller and Chief Counsel

# Context-enrichment: OCC finalises GENIUS Act stablecoin rules
_Analytical notes (not a post). Importance: 4/5._

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
On 2026-10-21 the OCC issued **Bulletin 2026-50 — "GENIUS Act Regulations: Final Rule"** (news release NR-OCC-2026-92; Federal Register at 91 FR 67000, 2026-10-23). This is the first final rulemaking implementing the GENIUS Act (enacted 2025-07-18) and it is substantive, not procedural. The note's own primary source (occ.gov bulletin) confirms: a **new 12 CFR Part 15 "Payment Stablecoins"** plus amendments to capital (12 CFR 3), prompt corrective action (12 CFR 6), fee assessment (12 CFR 8) and rules of practice (12 CFR 19). **Effective 2027-01-18**, the same day the statute takes effect — i.e. no transition runway; issuers must be compliant on day one.

Concrete numbers the press-release-style summary in the note omits (from external reporting of the rule text):
- **Reserves:** 1:1 backing in approved assets only (Treasuries, demand deposits); minimum **daily liquidity requirement cut to 5%**; **redemptions within 2 business days**.
- **Capital/operational backstop:** ongoing **$5M capital floor**, plus an operational backstop covering **~12 months of total expenses**.
- **Disclosure:** monthly public reserve composition on the issuer's website; **weekly confidential reports on Form PS-01 from 2026-01-25** (note: likely means 2027-01-25, shortly after the effective date — flag as a possible reporting typo in secondary sources).
- Companion: **Bulletin 2026-51** on GENIUS Act waivers and application-submission guidance.

**Why structured this way / what it reveals:** the OCC deliberately carved out BSA/AML/OFAC (sanctions) to a later joint rulemaking with Treasury. That means this rule is the "prudential core" (reserves, redemption, capital, supervision), while the harder financial-crime perimeter is deferred — so "finalised" is partial: a second rulemaking still looms. The anti-PR read: this is real, binding supervision ("supervise issuers like banks"), not a light-touch framework.

## [1] Competitors / peers
Not a company event — a regulator setting the rails that reshape competitive dynamics. The real "competition" is between regulatory tracks and issuer business models:
- **Federal charter track (OCC):** Ramp applied for an OCC charter for crypto custody ([[Ramp applies for OCC charter to offer crypto custody]], 2026-10-19); Agora got conditional OCC trust-bank approval for AUSD ([[Agora secures conditional OCC approval for trust bank]], 2026-10-07); Kraken's Payward and Zerohash earlier sought OCC national trust charters. This final rule is the destination those applicants were racing toward — it defines what being "permitted" actually requires.
- **Issuer economics:** Circle (USDC, largest US issuer) and Coinbase are structurally advantaged — Circle earns reserve yield and shares it with Coinbase, which pays users a "loyalty reward" (~4.1%). The rule's **no-safe-harbor stance on rewards** puts PayPal's 4% PYUSD, Coinbase One's 3.75% USDC rewards, and Kraken+'s up to 4.5% USDG returns under case-by-case scrutiny.
**Why the landscape is this way / second-order:** the GENIUS Act already banned issuers from paying yield "solely for holding." The novelty here is the OCC **declining to grandfather pre-existing rewards programs** — predating the Act buys no protection. This advantages Circle's indirect (reserve-yield → distributor → "reward") model's optics over issuers who pay directly, but every affiliate/white-label arrangement now needs individual OCC blessing. Margin in the stack shifts toward whoever controls the reserve-yield relationship and can structure "bona fide service" payments rather than naked yield.

## [2] Company history / fit (regulator fit)
The OCC under the current administration has pursued a pro-innovation digital-asset posture (conditional trust approvals for Agora, charter pipeline for Kraken/Ramp/Zerohash). The NPRM published 2026-02-25 (FR 2026-03-02, 91 FR 10202, OCC Bulletin 2026-3); the final rule arrives ~8 months later, well ahead of the statutory deadline. Treasury Secretary Bessent framed the law as a modern "National Currency Act" (Lincoln-era) — signalling the OCC sees stablecoin oversight as core to national banking policy, not a crypto side-quest. **Why:** the OCC is positioning itself as the primary federal stablecoin supervisor, pre-empting a fragmented state-by-state regime, consistent with the GENIUS Act giving it authority over federal qualified issuers, foreign issuers, and certain state issuers.

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
**Genuinely new (vs prior corpus):** earlier notes cover the Act's *passage* and *effects* — [[Stablecoin payments surge 70% after GENIUS Act]] (2025-10), [[IMF GENIUS Act cut payment firms' value by $300 billion]] (2026-06), [[Connecting the Dots Stablecoin competition heats up post-GENIUS]]. This is the first time the *binding implementing regulation* is finalised with hard numbers. That is a distinct, material event — the move from "law exists" to "here is exactly what compliance requires."
**Traction/reality check:** the rule is finalised and dated, not "proposed." Market read it as de-risking: Circle (CRCL) +~6%, Coinbase (COIN) +~5%, Strategy +~8% on the day; PayPal, Robinhood, Gemini also up. Circle's Dante Disparte and Coinbase's Faryar Shirzad publicly welcomed it. **Who in the stack captures margin:** clarity favours scaled, compliant issuers (Circle) and reserve-yield distributors; it raises the bar ($5M capital, 12-month opex backstop, weekly PS-01 reporting) against small/new entrants — consolidation pressure.

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
- **Next shoe:** a separate OCC–Treasury rulemaking on BSA/AML/OFAC — the compliance-cost wildcard still outstanding.
- **Rewards fight:** no safe harbor means PayPal/Coinbase/Kraken rewards programs face individual OCC review before/after 2027-01-18; expect lobbying and possible litigation on where "bona fide service" ends and "yield" begins.
- **Bank-industry pushback:** ABA CEO Rob Nichols argued the rule "preserves the status quo for crypto platforms" against Congress's intent to protect local lending — the deposit-flight / disintermediation complaint. **Counterintuitive second-order:** by legitimising stablecoins as bank-like, the rule could accelerate deposit migration from community banks even as it tightens issuer prudential standards — i.e. safer issuers, but a bigger competitive threat to small-bank funding. **The central question shifts** from "will stablecoins be allowed?" to "who absorbs the rewards/yield ambiguity, and does clarity entrench incumbents (Circle/Coinbase) while squeezing both small banks and small issuers?"

## Челлендж / ред-тим

<!-- enrichment:challenge -->
**Top challenge / red-team questions.** Importance: 4/5.

1. **Is this a new event or a re-run?** New. Prior corpus covers the GENIUS Act's *passage* (2025-07-18) and *effects* ([[Stablecoin payments surge 70% after GENIUS Act]], [[IMF GENIUS Act cut payment firms' value by $300 billion]]). This is the first *final implementing rule* (Bulletin 2026-50, 2026-10-21) with binding numbers — a distinct event. **Fresh.**

2. **"Finalised" — fully?** No. It explicitly excludes BSA/AML/OFAC rules, deferred to a separate OCC–Treasury rulemaking. So a second, arguably harder, rule is still pending. Secondary coverage calling it "the GENIUS Act rules finalised" overstates completeness.

3. **Effective date reality:** 2027-01-18 — same day as the statute. No phase-in. Confirmed by the note's own primary source. Open: are there any delayed-compliance provisions for specific sub-requirements? (not established).

4. **Reserve/liquidity numbers — sourced?** 1:1 backing, 5% minimum daily liquidity, 2-business-day redemption, $5M capital floor, 12-month opex backstop all come from *secondary* reporting of the rule text, not the note's bulletin extract. Treat as high-confidence but formally secondary until cross-checked against 91 FR 67000.

5. **The "Form PS-01 weekly reports from 2026-01-25" date** — almost certainly 2027-01-25 (a week after the effective date); a likely secondary-source typo. Flagged, not asserted as 2026.

6. **Rewards/yield — what actually changed vs the statute?** The Act already banned yield "solely for holding." The rule's novelty: OCC removed a proposed rebuttable presumption AND declined to grandfather pre-existing programs. So PayPal/Coinbase/Kraken rewards are judged case-by-case, same as new ones. This is the real teeth.

7. **Does "no safe harbor" = rewards banned?** No. "Bona fide service" payments generally aren't prohibited; naked hold-based yield is. The line is deliberately fact-specific → litigation/lobbying risk. Open: how OCC will adjudicate Circle→Coinbase "loyalty reward" (4.1%) structures.

8. **Who benefits, who's squeezed?** Clarity favours scaled compliant issuers (Circle/Coinbase); $5M floor + 12-month opex backstop + weekly reporting raises the barrier for small/new issuers → consolidation. Circle's indirect reserve-yield-share model is optically cleaner than direct payers.

9. **Market reaction — signal or noise?** CRCL ~+6%, COIN ~+5%, MSTR ~+8% on the day; PayPal/Robinhood/Gemini up. A genuine de-risking signal, but a one-day stock move is sentiment, not a durable valuation verdict.

10. **Industry split:** crypto issuers welcomed it (Disparte/Circle, Shirzad/Coinbase); ABA (Rob Nichols) opposed, saying it "preserves the status quo for crypto platforms" vs protecting local lending. The deposit-disintermediation fight is unresolved.

11. **Second-order on banks:** legitimising bank-like stablecoins could accelerate deposit flight from community banks even while tightening issuer prudential rules — safer issuers, bigger funding threat to small banks. (analysis)

12. **Charter pipeline link:** does this rule change the calculus for OCC charter applicants (Ramp, Agora, Kraken/Payward, Zerohash)? It defines what "permitted issuer" requires, so it raises the compliance bar those applicants must meet. Open: whether conditional approvals (Agora) auto-convert under the new 12 CFR 15.

13. **Foreign issuers:** rule asserts OCC authority over foreign payment stablecoin issuers — a notable extraterritorial reach. Open: enforcement mechanics and reciprocity.

14. **What's genuinely NOT disclosed:** exact text of the capital/backstop calculation, interaction with state qualified-issuer transition, and the custody provisions' scope — all in 12 CFR 15 but only summarised in the bulletin.

15. **Importance rationale:** First binding federal implementation of landmark stablecoin law, hard prudential standards, immediate market reaction, direct bearing on multiple corpus charter stories, and a live rewards/yield fight. High weight but not a 5 because (a) it's an expected regulatory milestone, not a surprise, and (b) the AML half is still pending. **Importance: 4/5. Verdict: fresh** (no prior note reports this specific 2026-10-21 final rule).

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** US payment-stablecoin supply sits at ~$308bn (Aug 2026, off the ~$322bn May 2026 peak), up ~12x from ~$27bn end-2020 (per spark.money / bitcoinfoundation.org tracker, as of Oct 2026). The market is highly concentrated: Tether's USDT ~$184bn (~58-59% share) and Circle's USDC ~$72-74bn, so the top two are >80% of supply (CoinLaw / Fool, Oct 2026). Structurally the chain is issuer (mint/redeem + reserve float) → distribution (exchanges, wallets, card rails) → settlement; the economics live at the issuer, which earns the reserve yield on Treasuries. The GENIUS Act (enacted 2025-07-18) created a federal licensing regime, and this OCC final rule (Bulletin 2026-50, 2026-10-21; Federal Register 2026-10-23; effective 2027-01-18) is the "why now": it converts a statute into supervised standards — reserves limited to Treasuries/demand deposits, redemptions within two business days, no discretionary redemption limits, a $5m minimum capital floor plus a 12-month operating-expense backstop, and bank-style exam/supervision via new 12 CFR 15 (per OCC bulletin; leaprate.com; forbes.com, Oct 2026). Entry barrier just rose from "none" to a federal charter/approval plus capital — a regulatory moat that favours incumbents and chartered banks.

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs: outstanding supply (float), reserve yield captured, redemption-SLA reliability, and distribution reach. The $10bn-outstanding threshold is the dividing line — above it an issuer falls under full federal supervision (OCC/FDIC/Fed); only Tether (~$184bn) and Circle (~$72bn) clearly clear it today (per cryptotimes.io, Oct 2026). Named/adjacent players building for the chartered path appear in-base: [[Agora secures conditional OCC approval for trust bank]] (AUSD moving from Bermuda to a US trust bank), [[Anchorage Digital and Securitize bring stablecoin issuance to banks]] (federally chartered Anchorage Digital Bank as the mint/redeem rail for USDtb/USA₮, with Paxos Trust named as the comparable chartered issuer for USDG/PYUSD/USDL). Basis of competition is shifting from pure distribution to regulatory legitimacy + reserve quality. Position: US-domiciled, already-regulated issuers (Circle, Paxos, trust-chartered entrants) are structurally advantaged; Tether — larger but offshore — must clear the "foreign payment stablecoin issuer" requirements to keep US distribution, which is the binding question the rule leaves partly to a later Treasury-coordinated AML/OFAC rulemaking (analysis).

**Comps & multiples.** Only one clean public comp exists; this is a regulatory event, not a deal, so most multiples = no data. Circle (CRCL): market cap ~$21.8bn on TTM revenue+reserve income ~$2.9bn (to 2026-06-30) → P/S = $21.8bn / $2.9bn = 7.5x (macrotrends / stocktitan, Oct 2026). On a revenue multiple this is mid-range for a regulated-financial/payments name and not obviously rich given the regime now de-risks its charter path (analysis). Tether is private (no listed equity; ~$184bn float is supply, not market cap — do not conflate). Internal round comp: [[Binance invests $100M in stablecoin issuer Circle]] ($100m at $80.84/share, two-year lockup) — a strategic stake, not a valuation mark. EV/EBITDA, forward multiples → [UNSOURCED].

**Risk flags.**
- Yield-ban loophole (regulatory): the rule keeps the issuer-level ban on paying yield but drops the tool that would have reached rewards routed through exchanges, so Coinbase can keep paying ~4.1% on USDC via a Circle revenue-share outside the rule's language (cointelegraph.com, Oct 2026). Second-order: deposit-competition fears — the ABA is pushing back, and a future fix could compress the distribution economics that currently drive USDC adoption.
- Incomplete rulebook: BSA/AML and OFAC sanctions are carved out for a separate Treasury-coordinated rulemaking. The foreign-issuer (read: Tether) treatment and the hardest compliance costs are therefore not yet settled — clarity is partial, not final.
- Winners/losers concentration: the charter + capital floor + supervision stack favours large and bank-affiliated issuers and squeezes sub-scale or offshore entrants; a regime that entrenches the top two could reduce competitive pressure on fees/yield passthrough.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** This is a re-rating/legitimisation catalyst for US-chartered issuers more than a growth shock: regulatory clarity lowers the discount on a federally supervised float business, favouring Circle/Paxos/trust-charter entrants over offshore supply (analysis). Falsifiable thesis: if the follow-on Treasury/AML rule and the "rewards" gap are tightened, the exchange-distributed yield that fuels USDC growth gets curtailed — watch the next rulemaking and whether Tether files for foreign-issuer approval before the 2027-01-18 effective date; either would test the thesis. (analysis)
