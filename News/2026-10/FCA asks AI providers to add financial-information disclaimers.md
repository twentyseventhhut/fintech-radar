### fca.org.uk
<https://www.fca.org.uk/news/speeches/building-future-finance-age-ai>
*1762 слов · direct*

Jessica Rusu, FCA chief data, information and intelligence officer at Bloomberg's UK Policy Series.

 
 On this page 
 
 
 The Mills Review 
 AI adoption: good and poor practice 
 From frontier AI to agentic finance 
 Becoming an agentic regulator 
 Conclusion 
 Speaker: Jessica Rusu, FCA chief data, information and intelligence officer
 Event: Bloomberg UK Policy Series
 Delivered: 19 October 2026
 Note: This is a drafted speech and may differ from the delivered version
 Reading time: 8 minutes
Highlights
We want the UK to be at the forefront of the safe and responsible adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) in financial services, supporting innovation, productivity and economic growth.
As AI evolves towards increasingly autonomous systems, firms should put consumers first, maintain clear accountability and plan for when things go wrong.
We are sharing examples of good and poor AI practice to help firms innovate responsibly, with a focus on putting consumers first, ensuring clear accountability and planning for failure.
The FCA is becoming a smarter regulator, using AI in its own work and taking forward the Mills Review through engagement, testing and action.
It’s great to be here today with you – humans.
The AI news cycle has become almost impossible to keep up with.
Like many of you, I’m reading a lot – how the race for faster AI chips is driving investment in data centres, creating both growth and risk across equity and debt markets.
Global spend on AI is expected to reach over $2.6 trillion this year.
That same acceleration is playing out across science and technology.
Last week, I visited a Quantum lab to see how AI is advancing cryptographic research.
And I’m fascinated by the way AI builders are turning to neuroscience to analyse the behaviour of agents, following the Hugging Face security incident.
After 25 years working at the intersection of data, technology and innovation, I have never found this field more exciting.
Adoption is not coming. It is here.
ChatGPT has reached over a billion active users.
Across financial services, firms are moving from experimentation to deployment. Our joint AI survey with the Bank of England, published next week, shows 86% of firms now use AI.
AI will redefine financial services. Done well, AI can increase productivity, improve competition, services and drive economic growth.
And the FCA wants the UK to be at the forefront of the safe adoption of AI in financial services.
The Mills Review
That is why we commissioned the Mills Review , published in July, focused on how AI could reshape retail financial services by 2030.
Some developments we anticipated are already emerging, including the transition towards agentic finance, such as the launch of Meta’s Muse.
Our response to the review is not to consult, study and report progress. Our focus is testing, assurance, monitoring and surveillance: directly with industry to understand how AI systems are being deployed.
Whilst the Review focused on retail, the AI shift does not stop at the retail boundary. Across wholesale markets too, AI is accelerating how firms analyse, trade, monitor and make decisions.
We are embedding 6 of the 7 recommendations into live work immediately. Our innovation services such as the AI Lab give us direct insight into what firms are building from agent-led payments and fraud detection to consumer support.
On frontier AI security, we are working directly with banks to understand how frontier models are changing cyber defence, identifying and linking vulnerabilities and assisting with triaging, patching and recovering at greater speed. Those insights are feeding directly into our supervisory and policy response.
Firms are not bystanders in that process. The leaders will be those that deploy AI effectively, innovatively and safely.
But increasingly, AI is also changing what happens before a consumer ever reaches a regulated firm. Consumers are turning to general-purpose large language models (LLMs), like ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude, for financial information and advice, while regulated firms are beginning to integrate their services into AI platforms.
The good news – this can significantly widen access to financial tools and information. But it can also blur the boundary between regulated and unregulated activity, and the protections consumers receive.
Four in five less-experienced investors aged 18-40 have used AI to help with investing, but only 40% of consumers correctly recognise that there is no recourse if something goes wrong after acting on advice from a general-purpose AI tool. Our answer is not simply to expand our remit, but to make the existing boundary clearer.
Through InvestSmart and the FCA Firm Checker , we already help consumers understand the protections they have. But warnings may be more effective at the point where AI-generated financial information is delivered.
So we are calling on AI providers to help consumers understand the limits of financial information and advice generated by their models through clear, proportionate and consistent disclaimers.
And rather than create a new public-interest financial capability tool, as the Mills Review suggested, we will build on existing work, including IOSCO’s first TechSprint, powered by our AI Lab to help investors spot scams, verify information and challenge unreliable AI outputs.
AI adoption: good and poor practice
Protecting consumers starts with how firms deploy AI.
Today, we are publishing good and poor practice drawn from our observations from firm testing this year, to give practical guidance and examples.
Three things stand out: consumer centricity, accountability and governance, and operational resilience.
Put the consumer first
The Consumer Duty provides an outcomes-based regulatory framework. We’ve seen firms using AI for inclusive product design, clear consumer choice, and a path to speak to a person. Good practice: one firm developed a live list of vulnerability signals that its chatbot can use to flag consumers who may need additional support to human advisers.
But we have seen poor practice too: firms developing AI tools without a clearly defined need, excluding vulnerable consumers by default, overstating capabilities, or deploying automated customer journeys without sufficient testing.
AI should improve consumer outcomes, not simply automate existing processes.
Know who is accountable
The second principle is governance, where too many firms are still under-investing.
Firms need clear accountability, risk-based controls, human oversight, escalation triggers, and senior managers personally accountable for AI systems.
We have seen good examples. One firm established an independent ethics panel to interrogate proposed AI use cases before deployment – including ownership, bias, and controls. But we have also seen firms retrofit governance after technology has already been deployed or fail to adapt monitoring as systems change.
That is the wrong way around.
Operational resilience
Third: stress-test AI systems. Plan for failure. Maintain fallback options. Understand supplier concentrations and dependencies, including reliance on a single model. A system that fails at scale disrupts the services consumers rely on. Make sure you have proper cyber insurance in place.
Overall, our good and poor practice finds that both firms and consumers benefit from well-deployed systems. Even AI deployed purely for internal efficiency passes value to consumers – through better-run firms, more competitive pricing, and faster service.
Our good and poor practice, published today, is not intended to be static guidance – we will update it as the technology evolves.
From frontier AI to agentic finance
And the frontier is moving quickly – shifting from technology that helps us understand and decide, to technology that can increasingly act on our behalf.
Agents can search across products, manage payments and investments, and transact directly with other agents, creating a new layer of autonomous technology between institutions and their customers. With its strengths in fintech and financial services, the UK can lead this next wave.
But greater autonomy also changes the risk.
Poorly governed AI can create consumer harm, cyber vulnerabilities and operational failures, potentially at much greater speed and scale.
That is why firms need to invest now in cyber and operational resilience. Our rules already require firms to report major incidents, and that must extend to significant AI failures and near-misses. As the AI Minister has recognised, near-miss reporting for agentic AI is becoming a national security priority. It is a regulatory priority too.
It also means thinking differently about assurance. A system that is safe at deployment may behave differently at scale, under stress or as the models beneath it change. Agentic systems therefore require continuous, conditional assurance.
The FCA will work with industry, fellow regulators and frontier labs to develop a trusted framework for agentic finance, including clear approaches to agent identity, delegated authority, accountability and execution.
Our aim is to create the conditions in which agentic finance can thrive, with innovation and consumer protection built together from the start.
Becoming an agentic regulator
And the FCA itself is changing. Part of our work to become a smarter regulator means developing our future as an agentic regulator, using the same technologies reshaping our markets to make ourselves sharper, faster, and more effective.
Across the FCA we are deploying AI to scale our operations and focus on the greatest harms, upskilling staff and investing in technology. AI can gather evidence, surface intelligence from disparate sources, and flag early warning signs so we can act at pace.
For example, we are building AI tools for the oversight of 60,000 anti-money laundering (AML) firms coming into the FCA’s remit in 2028. We are deploying automation and building AI agentic tools – like our market first responder agent – which will supervise ~9,000 small wholesale markets transaction reporting firms, so supervisors can prioritise more complex supervisory activities.
Dynamic data collection becomes part of the future of supervision. Building on our launch in August of the FCA’s Handbook API, giving access to firms to digest our machine-readable rules via their AI tools, we are also working on machine-readable data collection APIs.
And leveraging our AI Lab and Sandbox environment becomes ever more important for system-wide simulations and method of supervision.
Conclusion
I began by saying it was good to be here among humans. While that was light-hearted, there is a serious point behind the work we must do together.
AI is moving faster than any regulatory framework was designed. That means regulators will increasingly need to make judgements without precedent, while ensuring that trust remains at the heart of financial services.
We remain focused on our strategy , becoming a smarter regulator, using technology to become faster and more effective, enabling markets that work well – for consumers, for growth, and for the long term.
Building on our world-leading approach to innovation and proportionate regulation, we believe developing the best approach to deploying AI safely is what will differentiate the UK market.
The future of finance is being built now. Let's build it together.

# Context-enrichment: FCA asks AI providers to add financial-information disclaimers
_Analytical notes (not a post). Importance: 3/5._

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
On **19 Oct 2026**, FCA chief data, information and intelligence officer **Jessica Rusu** told Bloomberg's UK Policy Series that the FCA is "calling on AI providers to help consumers understand the limits of financial information and advice generated by their models through clear, proportionate and consistent disclaimers." Named providers: **general-purpose LLMs — ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude**.

De-PR'd, this is **not a rule, not a consultation, not an enforcement action** — it is a public *request* ("calling on"). Rusu explicitly framed the FCA's stance as "**not simply to expand our remit, but to make the existing boundary clearer**." Reporting confirms the voluntary nature: the FCA set **no new AI rules**, relying instead on existing frameworks (Consumer Duty, Senior Managers & Certification Regime). So the substance is a *soft-power nudge to third parties the FCA does not regulate*, delivered alongside guidance it does control.

What it was bundled with (same speech/day): (a) publication of **"good and poor practice" in AI** drawn from firm testing — real guidance for *regulated* firms on consumer-centricity, accountability/governance, operational resilience; (b) a restated response to the **Mills Review** (July 2026) — "testing, assurance, monitoring" over "consult and report"; (c) a forthcoming **FCA/Bank of England AI survey** showing 86% of firms use AI; (d) plans for an **agentic-finance framework** (agent identity, delegated authority) and the FCA's own "agentic regulator" build-out.

The load-bearing data points the FCA leans on: **four in five less-experienced investors aged 18-40 have used AI to help with investing**, but **only ~40% of consumers recognise there is no recourse** if something goes wrong after acting on general-purpose AI advice. That ~40% recourse-awareness gap is the entire justification for the disclaimer ask.

**+ Why framed this way and what it reveals:** The FCA has no jurisdiction over OpenAI/Google/Anthropic as *AI providers* (they are not authorised firms giving regulated advice). It cannot mandate disclaimers, so it reaches for the only lever it has over non-regulated parties — public moral suasion + the implicit threat that if AI output crosses into regulated "advice," the perimeter bites. The disclaimer ask is therefore a way to *police the regulatory perimeter without expanding it* — pushing the compliance burden onto platforms voluntarily rather than taking on liability or a new remit itself.

## [1] Competitors / peers (regulatory landscape)
Peers here are other regulators facing the same "LLMs give de-facto financial advice" boundary problem:
- **UK / FCA prior art:** Mills Review (Jul 2026) on AI reshaping retail finance by 2030 [[FCA publishes review on how AI could reshape retail finance]]; AI Live Testing cohorts [[FCA names eight new participants for AI Live Testing]]; AI Sandbox with Nvidia; Firm Checker and InvestSmart consumer tools [[FCA launches Firm Checker tool to fight financial scams]]; **finfluencer enforcement** (74 actions in 2025) [[FCA ramps finfluencer enforcement to 74 actions in 2025]] — the disclaimer ask is the *AI analogue* of the finfluencer crackdown: unregulated "advice" reaching retail at scale.
- **Cross-border:** FCA cites **IOSCO's first TechSprint** as the vehicle it will build on (rather than a new UK capability tool). Internationally, the US (SEC/FINRA) and EU (via the AI Act's transparency obligations + MiFID advice rules) are circling the same issue; the EU AI Act already imposes some transparency/labelling duties, so the FCA's voluntary ask is *lighter-touch* than the EU's statutory route.

**+ Why the lay of the land is this way:** No major jurisdiction has cleanly solved "an LLM told a retail user what to buy." The FCA is positioning the UK as "proportionate / innovation-friendly" (voluntary ask) vs the EU's rules-based transparency mandate — a deliberate competitive differentiation ("safe adoption is what will differentiate the UK market"). Second-order: a voluntary ask is cheap and fast but has **no enforcement teeth against the AI providers**, so its effectiveness depends entirely on whether OpenAI/Google/Anthropic choose to comply.

## [2] Company history / fit
The FCA has run a consistent 2025-26 arc: AI Sandbox/Supercharged Sandbox (2025), live AI testing cohorts (Dec 2025, Apr 2026), Mills Review (Jul 2026), crypto/stablecoin regime build-out, and consumer-protection tooling (Firm Checker, InvestSmart). Rusu's speech fits as the next step: moving the AI agenda **beyond regulated firms to the pre-firm consumer touchpoint**.

**+ Why the FCA acts this way:** Structural pressure — its statutory objectives (consumer protection, market integrity, competition/growth) now collide with the fact that consumers increasingly get financial guidance *before they ever reach a regulated firm*, from entities outside the perimeter. The FCA can either (a) expand its remit (slow, politically costly, resource-heavy — note it is already absorbing 60,000 AML firms by 2028) or (b) shift the burden outward via disclaimers. It chose (b) because growth-mandate politics (post-2023 the government leans on the FCA to be pro-growth) make remit expansion unattractive.

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
**Novelty is modest.** The *idea* that general-purpose AI blurs the advice boundary is not new (Mills Review flagged it in July; finfluencer parallels are years old). The disclaimer ask itself is new *as a public FCA position*, but it is an *ask*, not a mechanism: **no traction yet** — no provider has publicly committed to FCA-specified disclaimers, no agreed wording, no timeline, no compliance metric. This is "announced," firmly not "live/adopted."

**+ Why the value-add is thin (for now):** The FCA captures no new power and bears no new cost; the burden sits with AI providers who have no regulatory obligation to act. Disclaimers also have known limits — consumers routinely ignore boilerplate warnings (the FCA's own 40%-awareness figure despite existing InvestSmart/Firm Checker tools proves disclaimers-at-distance underperform, which is *why* Rusu argues for warnings "at the point where AI-generated financial information is delivered"). The genuine value-add, if realised, would be point-of-delivery friction inside the chat UI — but that is exactly what the FCA cannot compel. So the item's real weight is **signalling / perimeter-policing**, not an operative control.

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
Watch for: (a) whether OpenAI/Google/Anthropic respond with any UK-specific disclaimer commitment (none announced as of 22 Oct 2026); (b) the **FCA/BoE joint AI survey** (due the week after the speech) for the 86%-adoption detail and sector risk read; (c) the **agentic-finance framework** work (agent identity, delegated authority, near-miss reporting as a "national security priority") — this is where real rulemaking, and real teeth, are more likely to land than on disclaimers; (d) IOSCO TechSprint output. Industry/adviser sentiment (per trade press) is **supportive** — human advisers welcome anything that re-draws the line between them and free AI "advice."

**+ Why the market goes this way / second-order:** The counterintuitive effect is that a *voluntary, no-new-rules* posture may entrench the status quo: platforms add a thin disclaimer, the FCA declares the boundary "clearer," and the substantive risk (retail acting on unaccountable AI output with no recourse) persists. The more consequential thread is **agentic finance** — once AI *transacts* (not just advises), the perimeter question becomes unavoidable and voluntary asks won't suffice; that is where the FCA's framework work, and any future hard rules, will concentrate. The disclaimer ask is best read as the low-stakes opening move of a multi-year perimeter fight.

**Red-team / challenge questions**

1. Is this a rule or an ask? — **Open/answered: it is a voluntary request.** Rusu "calls on" AI providers; the FCA explicitly set no new AI rules and relies on Consumer Duty + SM&CR. No legal obligation on OpenAI/Google/Anthropic.
2. Does the FCA even have jurisdiction over general-purpose LLM providers? — No, not as AI providers. They are outside the authorised-firm perimeter unless their output constitutes regulated advice. That is the whole reason the FCA must "ask" rather than mandate.
3. Has any named provider (ChatGPT/Gemini/Claude) committed to FCA-style disclaimers? — **No public commitment as of 22 Oct 2026.** Announced, not adopted.
4. What is the agreed disclaimer wording / timeline / compliance metric? — None. No standard, no deadline. This is signalling, not an operative control.
5. Is the "four in five 18-40 investors use AI" stat sourced and robust? — FCA-cited; methodology/sample not in the speech. Treat as FCA figure, not independently verified.
6. Is the ~40%-know-there's-no-recourse figure the real driver? — Yes, it is the justification. But it also implicitly concedes that existing tools (InvestSmart, Firm Checker) under-deliver — which weakens the case that another disclaimer will work.
7. Do disclaimers actually change consumer behaviour? — Weak evidence; boilerplate warnings are routinely ignored. Rusu's own "at the point of delivery" framing admits distant warnings fail — but point-of-delivery is exactly what the FCA can't compel.
8. Is this genuinely new vs the Mills Review (Jul 2026) and finfluencer crackdown? — The boundary problem is old; the disclaimer *ask* is new as a public FCA position. Related but distinct item → fresh, not a re-run.
9. What's the enforcement path if providers ignore the ask? — Only the existing perimeter: if AI output = regulated advice, the firm/activity could be caught. Against the AI providers themselves, effectively none.
10. Is this UK-specific or does it track EU AI Act transparency duties? — FCA route is lighter (voluntary) vs EU's statutory transparency/labelling. Deliberate "proportionate/growth" differentiation.
11. Who's silent on what? — No mention of who bears liability when AI advice harms a consumer, no economics of implementing disclaimers, no stance on whether AI advice should ever be brought into the perimeter.
12. Is the real story the disclaimer, or the agentic-finance framework bundled alongside? — Arguably the latter: near-miss reporting as "national security priority" + agent-identity/delegated-authority framework is where hard rules (and teeth) are more likely to land.
13. Does this change the FCA's remit or resourcing? — No. Rusu explicitly avoids remit expansion; the FCA is already stretched (60,000 AML firms by 2028).
14. Any conflict between FCA primary text and trade-press summaries? — No material conflict; trade press ("no new rules", Consumer Duty/SM&CR reliance) is consistent with the speech.
15. Second-order risk? — A thin voluntary disclaimer lets the FCA declare the boundary "clearer" while the substantive no-recourse risk persists; status-quo entrenchment until agentic finance forces a harder perimeter decision.

Importance: 3/5 — A genuine, dated, named-provider FCA intervention on a real and growing consumer-protection gap (AI-as-adviser), from a top-tier regulator, which makes it more than noise. But it is a *voluntary ask with no teeth, no committed providers, no mechanism and no adoption* — signalling/perimeter-policing rather than an operative rule. It sits one notch above the finfluencer note (2/5) because it names the frontier labs and defines a boundary strategy, but below the Mills Review (4/5) which set the actual agenda. Not a 4 because nothing is binding or live.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** The subvertical is LLM-delivered personal-finance/investment guidance — general-purpose chatbots (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) acting as de-facto financial advisers outside the regulated perimeter. Adoption is the story, not revenue: the FCA cites 86% of financial-services firms now using AI (joint FCA–Bank of England survey, published w/c 20 Oct 2026, via FCA speech 19 Oct 2026), and four-in-five less-experienced investors aged 18-40 have used AI to help invest, while only 40% understand there is no recourse if AI advice goes wrong (FCA, 19 Oct 2026). Consumer-side demand is corroborated externally: ~49% of global consumers used AI for savings/investment decisions in the past six months (EY, Apr 2026); 200M+ people came to ChatGPT monthly for budgeting/investing before OpenAI launched a dedicated personal-finance mode (OpenAI, May 2026); ~43% of Gen Z/Millennial investors have tried chatbots for guidance vs 15% of boomers (Vanguard/MIT, via TheStreet/CNBC Jul 2026). Structure: value is being captured UPSTREAM of regulated firms — the LLM layer now sits between the consumer and the advice/product, which is precisely the "blurred boundary between regulated and unregulated activity" the FCA flags. Entry barrier to the guidance step is near-zero (no licence, no suitability test), which is the regulatory problem. Why now: (1) frontier-model ubiquity (ChatGPT >1bn active users per FCA); (2) the advice-guidance gap — robo-advice never scaled (only ~1.5% of UK adults used it by 2022, FCA), so general-purpose LLMs are filling the vacuum faster than regulated "targeted support" (near-final rules only 11 Dec 2025). Second-order effect: the FCA's own quoted quality data shows ~37% of chatbot users got incorrect/misleading info and 44% couldn't judge accuracy (Vanguard/MIT) — a consumer-harm pipeline forming before any regulated firm is in the loop.

**Competitive landscape.** This is a regulator-vs-platform dynamic, not a corporate comp. The sector "KPIs" that matter are adoption share, recourse-awareness gap, and misinformation rate (all above). Key actors: (a) regulator — FCA, choosing a light-touch "make the existing boundary clearer" posture (disclaimers + InvestSmart/Firm Checker + IOSCO TechSprint) rather than extending its remit; (b) AI providers — OpenAI, Google (Gemini), Anthropic (Claude), named directly, who are being ASKED (not mandated) to add "clear, proportionate and consistent" disclaimers; (c) regulated advice/robo incumbents, whose moat (suitability, FOS recourse, Consumer Duty) is being disintermediated. Basis of competition: trust/recourse vs convenience/free-at-point-of-use. Recent peer-regulator moves: ESMA says AI does not move a firm outside MiFID II and requires chatbot users to know they're talking to a machine (ESMA guidance, 2024 → TRV risk analysis Feb 2026); MAS Guidelines on Digital Advisory Services mandate algorithm governance and human-escalation paths, and MAS issued new AI risk rules this month [[MAS mandates new AI risk rules for financial firms]]; US SEC/FINRA apply existing adviser/supervision rules (FINRA 2026 Oversight Report added a GenAI section) with no AI-specific federal rule yet. FCA's position: ahead on soft engagement (AI Lab, Live Testing [[FCA names eight new participants for AI Live Testing]], Handbook API, agentic-finance framework) but deliberately NOT regulating the general-purpose LLM itself — its moat is convening power, not jurisdiction over OpenAI/Google/Anthropic, which sit outside the UK perimeter (analysis).

**Comps & multiples.** No corporate valuation/round/deal in this item — no EV/Revenue, P/E or price-per-user to compute; "no data / not applicable." Comparison is regulatory-precedent, not financial. Internal comps (same FCA/AI-guidance thread): [[FCA ramps finfluencer enforcement to 74 actions in 2025]] (adjacent unregulated-promotion crackdown — the enforcement stick vs the disclaimer carrot); [[FCA names eight new participants for AI Live Testing]] and [[Singapore and UK launch AI in finance partnership]] (FCA's build-with-industry model); [[MAS mandates new AI risk rules for financial firms]] (harder-edged cross-jurisdiction contrast — binding rules vs FCA's ask). Multiples distribution not computed — no comparable financial figures exist for this news.

**Risk flags.**
1. Enforceability gap (regulation): the FCA is *asking*, not mandating — the three named providers are outside its perimeter, so compliance is voluntary. Why it matters: if OpenAI/Google/Anthropic decline or ship inconsistent disclaimers, the FCA has no direct lever short of a remit expansion it has explicitly ruled out, leaving the 60% recourse-unaware consumer cohort exposed.
2. Disintermediation of regulated advice (margin/structure): the value and first touchpoint is migrating to an unregulated LLM layer upstream of FCA-authorised firms. Why: regulated robo/advice economics (acquisition, suitability cost) are undercut by free LLMs, so the advice gap the FCA's "targeted support" regime was meant to close may instead be captured by unregulated rails.
3. Divergent cross-jurisdiction standards (regulation): FCA (voluntary disclaimers) vs ESMA (MiFID II applies, mandatory chatbot disclosure) vs MAS (binding digital-advisory + AI-risk rules). Why: global providers face a patchwork; a UK-only soft ask may be ignored if stricter EU/SG obligations set the compliance baseline elsewhere, or conversely the UK looks under-protective — reputational/consumer-outcome risk under Consumer Duty if harm materialises.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** This is a soft-law trial balloon, not a rule — the FCA is testing whether "point-of-delivery" disclaimers beat remit expansion (analysis). Falsifiable thesis: within ~6-12 months either (a) the named providers ship visible, consistent UK financial disclaimers (validating the voluntary model, likely copied by other regulators), or (b) they don't, and a consumer-harm event forces the FCA toward the finfluencer-style enforcement route or statutory change. Trigger to watch: the joint FCA–BoE AI survey + good/poor-practice publication (w/c 20 Oct 2026), the IOSCO TechSprint output, and any first provider to add a UK-specific disclaimer — that first mover sets the de-facto standard (analysis). What breaks the thesis: a provider framing itself as "information not advice" to sidestep the ask entirely, keeping the boundary as blurred as before.
