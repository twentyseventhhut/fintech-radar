# Context-enrichment: US appeals court strikes down Fed debit interchange cap
_Analytical notes (not a post). Importance: 4/5._

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
On 26 Oct 2026 the U.S. Court of Appeals for the **Eighth Circuit**, ruling unanimously in **_Corner Post, Inc. v. Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System_**, affirmed a district-court judgment vacating the Fed's 2011 debit-interchange cap under **Regulation II**. The headline figure is the **21¢-per-transaction base cap** (plus 0.05% of value for fraud loss and 1¢ for fraud-prevention) applied to issuers with ≥$10bn in assets under the **Durbin Amendment** (Dodd-Frank §1075).

The court's precise holding is narrower than "fees are too high": it is a **methodology** ruling. The Durbin Amendment requires fees "reasonable and proportional to the cost incurred by the issuer **with respect to the transaction**." The Eighth Circuit held the Fed may count the **incremental/additional cost of processing a particular transaction** but **not fixed costs** that banks bear regardless of whether any given transaction occurs. The Fed is said to have improperly folded in non-incremental expenses, inflating the cap.

**Why structured this way / what it reveals.** This is an **administrative-law / statutory-construction** win, not a rate-setting one. The court did **not** set a replacement number — it remanded for the Fed to rewrite the framework on a transaction-specific cost basis. Nothing changes today: the 21¢+0.05%+1¢ cap remains in force until the mandate issues and the Fed re-rules. The "strikes down" framing overstates immediacy; the operative word is **remand**. The case is also a textbook child of _Corner Post_ standing doctrine — a young North Dakota truck-stop plaintiff (incorporated 2017) used its recent formation to reopen a 15-year-old rule past the usual 6-year APA clock, which is the real structural reason this 2011 rule is litigable in 2026 at all.

## [1] Competitors / peers (parallel regulatory fronts)
This sits inside a broad, multi-front war over card economics:
- **District-court stage of the SAME case** — a North Dakota court first struck Regulation II in Aug 2025 ([[North Dakota court strikes down Fed debit swipe-fee cap]]); the Oct 2026 ruling is the appellate affirmation, not a separate event.
- **Credit (not debit) interchange** — Visa/Mastercard reached a revised swipe-fee settlement with merchants ([[Visa and Mastercard reach revised interchange settlement]]); the **Credit Card Competition Act / routing mandates** push remains legislative ([[Lawmakers push credit card rate caps and routing mandates]]).
- **State-level** — OCC preempted Illinois's interchange-on-taxes-and-tips ban for national banks ([[OCC preempts Illinois interchange-fee ban on taxes and tips]]).
- **International analogues** — the UK High Court upheld the PSR's cross-border interchange cap against Visa/Mastercard/Revolut ([[Two European fintech deals raise €13.4 million in a week]]); the RBA is cutting Australian interchange caps and banning surcharging ([[Australian merchant card fees reach $8 billion annually]]); a UK tribunal found Visa/Mastercard fees breach competition law ([[UK tribunal rules Visa and Mastercard fees breach competition law]]).

**Why the lay of the land is this way.** Globally, regulators and courts are converging on **cost-based, transaction-specific** interchange — the US (via litigation), the EU/UK and Australia (via regulation). The US is the outlier that regulated debit but not credit; the Durbin cap has always been the soft underbelly because it is the one place Congress wrote an explicit cost test the Fed can be measured against. Visa/Mastercard set the *rates banks follow* but are not parties bound to lower them — they are the plumbing, insulated from the direct hit.

## [2] Company history / fit (the Fed and the 2011 rule)
Regulation II has been contested since inception. In **NACS v. Board of Governors (2014)**, the D.C. Circuit *upheld* the Fed's reading after a district court had struck it as too generous to banks — i.e., prior appellate precedent went the Fed's way. The Eighth Circuit now cuts the other direction on the fixed-vs-incremental question, creating the kind of **inter-circuit tension / methodology dispute** that invites Supreme Court review. The Fed's posture through the appeal was defensive (it filed a reply brief; faced "tough questions" at argument), consistent with an agency defending a 15-year-old compromise it has no appetite to reopen.

**Why the Fed acts this way.** Rewriting the cap downward is politically and operationally costly: lower it and ~$10bn+ banks lose debit revenue and warn of pricier checking accounts; the Fed has every incentive to appeal (en banc or cert) to preserve optionality rather than immediately re-rule.

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
**Genuinely new vs the Aug-2025 note:** (a) it is now **binding appellate law** in the Eighth Circuit, not a single district judgment; (b) the court articulated the **fixed-vs-incremental cost standard** as the operative legal test; (c) the remedy is a **mandate to rewrite**, raising the odds of a materially lower future cap. That is a real "talks → signed" style escalation, so **fresh**.

**But traction on the thing that matters — merchant/consumer savings — is zero today.** No new rate exists; the old cap still binds. The deeper question is **who captures any future saving**. Durbin's 2011 debit cap is widely studied to have shifted rents to large retailers with **little consumer pass-through** and a documented rise in bank account fees / loss of free checking for lower-balance consumers. A lower cap plausibly repeats that: merchant margin up, bank fee income down, consumer benefit uncertain. Small issuers (<$10bn) were **exempt all along**, so this never touched community banks/credit unions directly — hence the credit-union trade groups' muted framing.

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
Procedural path: the Fed can seek **rehearing en banc** at the Eighth Circuit or file for **certiorari** at the Supreme Court; otherwise the mandate issues and the Fed must open a **new rulemaking** to reset the cap on a transaction-specific basis — a multi-year process. Retail groups (RILA, FMI) applauded; banking/credit-union groups warned of revenue loss and consumer-cost knock-on. Analyst/commentary skepticism ("may not ring up savings") centres on pass-through.

**Why the second-order effect may invert the headline.** A merchant "win" that lowers the cap could (i) be clawed back by Visa/Mastercard raising *network/scheme fees* (uncapped) to defend issuer economics, and (ii) push banks to re-price deposit accounts — so the incidence may land on consumers while Visa/Mastercard revenue stays intact. The real question is not "did merchants win" but **whether a cost-based rewrite survives SCOTUS and whether any saving escapes the networks' uncapped fee layer**.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** US debit interchange is a ~$30bn+ fee pool set under the Durbin Amendment (Dodd-Frank, 2010) and the Fed's Regulation II (effective 2011): a 21c base + 0.05% ad-valorem + 1c fraud-prevention cap on "regulated" issuers (banks ≥$10bn assets); smaller issuers are exempt. In the Fed's 2024 data the average covered-transaction fee was $0.23 (~0.47% of a $48.95 ticket) vs $0.51 on exempt transactions — i.e. regulated issuers earn roughly half of what exempt ones do (per Fed Reg II report, via Digital Transactions / ABA, 2024-25). On 2026-10-26 the Eighth Circuit affirmed vacatur of the cap, holding the Fed exceeded its authority by baking in fixed/non-incremental issuer costs; "incremental cost" was read as marginal cost per the Durbin text, and the Fed is ordered to rewrite the framework (per Nat'l Law Review / ABA Banking Journal, 2026-10-26). Structure: a consolidated 4-party card model (issuer → network Visa/Mastercard → acquirer → merchant) with the Fed as price-setter on the issuer leg; the barrier here is regulatory/legal, not capital. "Why now": this affirms the Aug-2025 North Dakota district ruling [[North Dakota court strikes down Fed debit swipe-fee cap]], escalating a 15-year fight to the appellate level and forcing the Fed back to a rulemaking it had already proposed (2023: cut base 21c→14.4c) but never finalized (per Federal Register / consumerfinancemonitor, 2023).

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs: interchange per transaction (c), effective rate (% of ticket), covered vs exempt mix, and issuer interchange revenue as a share of non-interest income. Key stakeholders split cleanly by incentive: merchants/retailers (RILA, FMI) and their acquirers want the cap lowered (merchants estimate ~$9bn/yr saved under the current cap, ~70% passed to consumers per RILA/ConsumerAffairs, 2026-10); large regulated banks + credit-union/community-bank groups (ABA, America's Credit Unions, ICBA, Electronic Payments Coalition) want it preserved or raised, warning of pricier checking accounts (joint statement, 2026-10). Basis of competition is purely political/legal — no product moat. Note the ambiguity the headline flattens: vacatur does NOT itself set a new price. Because the court faults the Fed for over-counting costs (marginal-cost reading), a compliant rewrite points toward a LOWER cap than 21c, not higher — merchant-favorable — even though "cap struck down" sounds issuer-favorable. Timing: the mandate issues no earlier than 2026-12-17, so nothing changes at the terminal yet.

**Comps & multiples.** No single issuer/valuation here (regulatory), so trading multiples = not applicable / no data. Internal precedent in-base, showing this is one node in a global interchange-litigation wave:
- [[North Dakota court strikes down Fed debit swipe-fee cap]] (2025-08) — the district ruling this appeal affirms (same Reg II, same Corner Post plaintiff lineage).
- [[OCC preempts Illinois interchange-fee ban on taxes and tips]] (2026-05) — parallel US state-vs-federal interchange fight (IFPA), national banks carved out.
- [[Visa and Mastercard reach interchange fee settlement]] (2025-11) — $38bn credit-interchange class settlement; shows the scale of merchant-side economic stakes on the credit side.
- [[UK tribunal rules Visa and Mastercard fees breach competition law]] (2025-07) and [[Visa and Mastercard face new UK class action over card fees]] (2025-07) — same merchant-vs-network theme offshore.
Arithmetic the ruling turns on: current cap ≈ 21c + 0.05%×$48.95 ≈ 21c + 2.4c ≈ 23c effective; the Fed's shelved 2023 proposal (14.4c base + 4bps + 1.3c fraud) ≈ 14.4c + 2.0c + 1.3c ≈ 17.7c — a ~5c (~23%) cut per covered swipe, the rough magnitude a court-compliant rewrite could crystallize.

**Risk flags.**
1. Revenue hit to large issuers — a marginal-cost-only rewrite likely drops the per-swipe cap (toward the ~14.4c zone), compressing interchange income at ≥$10bn banks; second-order: pressure to reprice checking/DDA (lost free-banking cross-subsidy) and chase it back via fees elsewhere.
2. Durbin-exempt neobanks/BaaS are indirectly exposed — fintech debit programs (Chime et al.) monetize via the EXEMPT higher (~$0.51) interchange on sub-$10bn partner-bank BINs; a Fed rewrite that re-anchors the whole debit-pricing debate (or feeds Credit Card Competition Act momentum) threatens the exempt-rate durability that underpins neobank unit economics — a disintermediation risk to the core fintech funding model.
3. Prolonged uncertainty / timing — mandate not before 2026-12-17, then a multi-year Fed rulemaking + likely further appeal (possibly SCOTUS); networks, issuers and acquirers must price under an unsettled cap, raising operational/compliance risk without a resolution.
4. Mislabel risk in the headline itself — "struck down" reads pro-issuer but the legal logic (over-counted costs) is pro-merchant; anyone trading/positioning on the headline direction alone is exposed to being wrong on the economics.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** This is a re-rating of regulatory tail-risk for US card economics, not a one-day event: it reopens a lower cap that had been dormant since 2023 and strengthens the merchant lobby's broader "swipe-fee" push (incl. the credit-side CCCA) (analysis). Falsifiable thesis: the Fed's eventual rewrite lands BELOW the current 23c effective rate, modestly denting big-bank debit interchange — watch for (trigger) the mandate issuing ~2026-12-17 and the Fed re-opening the Reg II docket; the thesis breaks if the Fed instead justifies a cap at/above 21c on a defensible cost basis, or SCOTUS stays/reverses.
