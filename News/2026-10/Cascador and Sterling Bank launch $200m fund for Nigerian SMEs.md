# Cascador and Sterling Bank launch $200m fund for Nigerian SMEs

## Первоисточники

### afrotechhorizon.com
<https://www.afrotechhorizon.com/blog/cascador-sterling-200m-sme-credit-backstop-fund>
*705 слов · direct*

Nigerian small businesses that have outgrown grants but can't meet a bank's collateral demands may soon find bigger loans easier to get. Venture builder Cascador and Sterling Bank announced a $200 million SME credit backstop fund on 14 October. It will cover part of the losses that lenders take on approved loans to these businesses.
The partners say the reserve can support up to $1 billion in outstanding credit. First loans will be in naira, worth about $250,000 each, and the ceiling rises to $1 million per business once the fund is fully capitalised, according to BusinessDay and Techeconomy .
How the SME credit backstop fund works
The fund doesn't lend money itself. Venture funds, studios and other ecosystem partners nominate businesses they already know. The fund checks each one against its own eligibility, guarantee and portfolio-risk rules, using AI-assisted underwriting. If a business passes, the lender gets a defined level of protection against losses on that loan.
The bank still does the lending. It assesses the borrower, approves and pays out the loan, and handles repayments and collections. What changes is how much risk the bank carries, and so how much collateral it needs to ask for. Repayment periods are also meant to follow the borrower's cash flow, rather than the short tenors common in Nigerian SME lending.
The fund runs through two companies. Riverwell Platform Holding Limited is registered in the UK, and Riverwell Platform Services Nigeria handles partner coordination, borrower checks, data and portfolio monitoring. A third, separate company holds the reserve capital. Sterling Bank group executive Obinna Ukachukwu will sit on the Riverwell board.
Built on a two-year pilot
Cascador and Sterling have tested this before. For about two years they ran a smaller catalytic fund in which Cascador nominated and guaranteed borrowers and Sterling lent to them. The new vehicle turns that into a standalone platform that other backers can join. The partners want philanthropic funders, impact investors and development finance institutions to add capital to the guarantee reserve, which would let it cover more loans.
Cascador says the 80 ventures it has backed since 2019 have raised more than $150 million. In 2025 they served over 1.7 million customers and directly employed close to 4,000 people.
Collateral is the problem it targets
Cascador released a white paper, The Multiplier Model , alongside the fund. It notes that 22% of businesses in Sub-Saharan Africa hold a bank loan or line of credit, compared with a global average of 36%. Banks usually want property or other hard assets as security. Many young companies with steady revenue simply don't have them.
That leaves founders choosing between raising more equity, and giving up more of the company, or staying small. Equity has been an unreliable option this year. Nigerian startups raised just $11.3 million in September after a $364 million August that was mostly one deal, as we explained in our breakdown of September's 97% fall . Across the continent, trackers disagree on the totals but agree that money is going to fewer, larger rounds (see our look at the 2026 funding numbers ).
"Equity must work with debt and long-term securities to provide builders with the capital they need to build the future," said Iyinoluwa Aboyeji, founding partner at Future Africa, in comments reported by BusinessDay. Cascador CEO Trish Thomas said Nigeria is "rich in entrepreneurial talent and innovation."
What founders should know
You can't apply to the fund directly. A business has to be nominated by a participating ecosystem partner, already have commercial traction and be ready to carry commercial debt. If you're interested, start by asking your investors or studio whether they are part of the nominating network.
Every loan still needs the bank's approval, and the amount of credit that actually reaches SMEs depends on how much more reserve capital the partners raise. Sterling has a stated lending focus on health, education, agriculture, renewable energy and transport. The fund itself hasn't published sector limits.
If it works, investors get a way to support portfolio companies without another equity round, and banks get cover to lend to businesses they would normally turn down. The first thing to watch is how quickly the initial $250,000 loans move from nomination to payout.

# Context-enrichment: Cascador and Sterling Bank launch $200m fund for Nigerian SMEs
_Analytical notes (not a post). Importance: 3/5._

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
On 14 October 2026, Nigerian entrepreneur-support org (ESO) **Cascador** and **Sterling Bank** launched a **$200m SME Credit Backstop Fund** — a **partial loss-sharing guarantee reserve**, NOT a lending vehicle. The fund does not disburse money; it absorbs a "defined level" of lender losses on approved loans, so the bank carries less risk and can demand less collateral. The partners claim the reserve can "support up to $1bn in outstanding credit" (i.e. ~5x leverage on the guarantee), initial loans in naira at ~$250k/business, ceiling rising to $1m "once fully capitalised."
- **De-PR'd read 1 — the headline is soft.** "$200m fund" is a *guarantee-capacity ceiling*, not $200m of committed capital deployed. The "up to $1bn in credit" and "$1m loans" are explicitly conditional on **raising more capital** from "philanthropic funders, impact investors and DFIs." So today's real, funded number is unstated — the PR anchors to the aspirational ceiling, not committed cash. **Why structured this way:** guarantee funds lever a small reserve into large notional credit; announcing the *notional* ($1bn) and *aspiration* ($200m) maximises headline weight while hiding how thin the actual first-loss capital is. Watch for the committed-capital figure, not the ceiling.
- **De-PR'd read 2 — borrowers can't apply.** Access is **nomination-only** via participating VC funds/studios who "already know" the business; the fund then runs AI-assisted eligibility/portfolio-risk checks; Sterling still underwrites, approves, disburses and collects. **Why it matters:** this is a *portfolio-protection product for the existing VC ecosystem's debt-ready alumni*, not broad SME financial inclusion. It narrows adverse selection (nominators have skin in the game) but means the "40m MSMEs / $236bn gap" framing (see [3]) is marketing — the addressable set is a few hundred vetted, revenue-generating ventures.
- **Structure:** runs through **Riverwell Platform Holding Ltd (UK)** + **Riverwell Platform Services Nigeria** (ops/monitoring); a **third separate company holds the reserve capital**. Sterling group exec **Obinna Ukachukwu** sits on the Riverwell board. **Why UK holdco:** likely to make the vehicle credible/legible to international DFIs and impact investors writing hard-currency cheques, and to ring-fence the reserve — consistent with the stated goal of attracting external guarantee capital.

## [1] Competitors / peers
- **The direct model peer is the DFI/sovereign guarantee scheme, and it just got dwarfed.** The **World Bank's Dec-2025 $500m FINCLUDE** programme for Nigeria is designed to issue up to **$800m in guarantees** and mobilise ~$1.89bn private capital — an order of magnitude above Cascador/Sterling and state-backed. Nigeria also runs long-standing public guarantee/intervention schemes. So Cascador/Sterling is a **private, VC-ecosystem-scoped** complement, not a system-mover.
- **Peer interventions this cycle:** [[Bank of Thailand launches measures to revive SME lending]] (central-bank push to un-stick SME credit) and [[India approves ₹20,000 crore microfinance credit guarantee scheme]] (state credit-guarantee at far larger scale) show the *guarantee/backstop* mechanic is the global tool-of-choice for SME credit gaps — Nigeria's version is private and small by comparison.
- **The thing it's really competing with is equity.** The explicit pitch (Aboyeji/Future Africa, Cascador CEO Trish Thomas) is "debt instead of another dilutive round." That matters because Nigerian equity cratered — Sept-2026 startup funding was just $11.3m. **Why the land lies this way:** with equity scarce and the MPR ~27% (double-digit real rates), *de-risked* bank debt is one of the few scalable capital channels left; a guarantee is the cheapest way to bend bank risk appetite without the bank funding the first loss itself.

## [2] Company history / fit
- **Cascador** (ESO, founded 2019; CEO Trish Thomas): runs a 12-week ScaleUp program + a **Catalytic Fund** (~$5m/yr, debt+equity for alumni). Claims **80 ventures backed, >$150m raised** by them, 1.7m customers served and ~4,000 employed in 2025 (self-reported; note the note says 80/$150m while other Cascador material says 70/$125m — figures are in flux, treat as approximate).
- **Sterling Bank** runs the **HEART strategy** (Health, Education, Agriculture, Renewable energy, Transport); HEART financing grew 39% to N331.7bn in 2025. Stated SME focus sits in those verticals, but **the fund has published no sector limits** — a gap.
- **Fit / why now:** this is the **scale-up of a ~2-year pilot** in which Cascador *nominated and guaranteed* borrowers and Sterling lent (reported earlier as a ~$2m catalytic arrangement that fed a June-2026 pitch day disbursing >$5m). **Why structured this way:** the pilot proved the nominate-guarantee-lend loop works at tiny scale; turning it into a standalone, open platform (Riverwell) is the logical move to (a) attract third-party guarantee capital and (b) let *other lenders/backers* join beyond Sterling. It fits Sterling's development-finance brand and Cascador's need to give alumni non-dilutive growth capital.

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
- **Novelty: moderate, real but incremental.** First-loss / partial credit guarantees for SME lending are a decades-old DFI instrument; what's *new here* is (a) **private** reserve capital, (b) a **VC-nomination funnel** with skin-in-the-game to cut adverse selection, (c) **AI-assisted underwriting**, and (d) **cash-flow-aligned tenors** vs Nigeria's typically short SME tenors. None of these is individually novel; the combination packaged as an open platform is the value-add.
- **Traction: thin and announcement-stage.** The only proof points are the 2-year pilot and ~$5m disbursed via the predecessor — real but tiny. The $200m/$1bn/$1m numbers are all forward-looking and capital-raise-dependent. **The genuine KPI to watch (per the source itself): how fast the first $250k loans move from nomination to payout.** Until then this is "announced, lightly piloted," not "live at scale."
- **Why the value-add may or may not be real — who captures the margin:** the guarantee bends Sterling's risk appetite, but **Sterling still owns underwriting, pricing and collections** — so the bank captures the lending spread (in a ~27% MPR environment, that spread is fat) while the *reserve* (funded partly by philanthropic/DFI money) eats the first-loss tail. That's efficient for borrowers but means **concessional/impact capital is effectively subsidising a commercial bank's credit risk**. The model only "multiplies" if external funders keep topping up the reserve; if they don't, capacity stays near the committed (undisclosed) floor, not $1bn. Default experience in the early cohorts is the real test — a guarantee fund is only as good as its loss curve, and that data doesn't exist yet. Contrast the downside: [[Nigerian digital lender Lidya shuts down]] shows Nigerian SME-credit models can fail on economics/collections even with good intentions.

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
- **Backdrop is brutal for the problem, favourable for the mechanism.** Only ~4% of Nigeria's ~40m MSMEs have a formal bank loan; the MSME funding gap is cited at ~$236bn; MPR ~27%; equity funding collapsed (Sept-2026 $11.3m). Cascador's *Multiplier Model* white paper notes 22% of Sub-Saharan African firms hold a bank loan/line vs 36% globally. So demand for de-risked debt is enormous — but the *supply* here is capped by how much guarantee capital gets raised.
- **Risks / what to watch:** (1) **capital-raise execution** — the whole $1bn story is contingent on DFIs/impact investors funding the reserve; (2) **loss curve** of early cohorts (the number that decides whether guarantors re-up); (3) **FX** — loans start in naira but the ceiling is quoted in USD, and naira volatility can erode the reserve's hard-currency value; (4) **no published sector limits** despite Sterling's HEART focus — governance ambiguity; (5) **concentration** — nomination-only keeps it narrow, which is good for quality but means limited inclusion impact. **Second-order:** if it works, it becomes a template other Nigerian ESOs/banks copy and a channel for DFIs to deploy guarantees efficiently; if early losses spike or capital-raise stalls, it stays a boutique alumni-debt facility — exactly what the predecessor pilot already was.

### Red-team / challenge questions

1. **How much capital is actually committed today?** The "$200m fund" is a guarantee *ceiling* and "$1bn credit" is notional; the source never states committed first-loss capital. **Open** — likely far below $200m, as $1m loans and full capacity are explicitly conditional on future DFI/impact raises.

2. **Is "$200m fund" even the right framing?** It's a loss-sharing reserve, not deployed capital or a loan book. Calling it a "$200m fund for SMEs" overstates money reaching businesses. **Answered:** PR-inflated; the honest number is undisclosed committed reserve.

3. **Who eats the first loss, who keeps the spread?** Reserve (partly philanthropic/DFI) absorbs the loss tail; Sterling keeps the lending spread (fat at ~27% MPR). **Answered:** concessional capital subsidises a commercial bank's credit risk — the key equity question.

4. **What's the guarantee coverage ratio per loan?** Source says only "a defined level of protection." 20%? 50%? 80%? This single parameter determines both leverage and how much behavior it actually changes. **Open.**

5. **Where's the loss-curve evidence?** A guarantee fund lives or dies on defaults. Only prior data is the ~2-yr, ~$2m pilot / >$5m disbursed. No published default/recovery data. **Open — this is the decisive unknown.**

6. **Is this genuinely new or a re-packaged pilot?** It scales a ~2-year Cascador/Sterling catalytic-fund pilot (ThisDay May-2025) into a standalone Riverwell platform with open third-party participation. **Answered: FRESH** — new vehicle, new terms (nomination-open, $200m/$1bn ceiling, AI underwriting), not a reprint. No prior note covers it.

7. **How does it compare to the World Bank's $500m FINCLUDE (Dec-2025)?** FINCLUDE issues up to $800m guarantees + ~$1.89bn mobilised — an order of magnitude larger and sovereign-backed. **Answered:** Cascador/Sterling is a small private complement, not a system-mover.

8. **Why a UK holdco (Riverwell Platform Holding Ltd)?** **Analysis:** to make the reserve legible/credible to international DFIs and impact investors and ring-fence capital — consistent with the external-capital goal. **Open on tax/regulatory specifics.**

9. **Who is the real addressable market?** Not 40m MSMEs — only VC/studio-nominated, revenue-generating, debt-ready alumni. **Answered:** hundreds of ventures, not mass inclusion; the gap-stats are context, not TAM.

10. **FX risk?** Loans start in naira; ceiling quoted in USD. Naira depreciation erodes the reserve's hard-currency value and the real size of future $1m loans. **Open — unaddressed in PR.**

11. **No published sector limits despite Sterling's HEART (Health/Edu/Agri/Renewables/Transport) focus — will the fund actually be sector-agnostic or quietly HEART-tilted?** **Open** — governance ambiguity.

12. **Does cutting collateral but keeping Sterling's pricing really help at a ~27% MPR?** Removing collateral lowers one barrier, but double-digit real rates may still make the debt unaffordable for many SMEs. **Open — affordability, not just access, is the bind.**

13. **What stops this staying boutique?** Entire $1bn story depends on continuous external reserve top-ups; if DFIs/impact investors don't fund it, it stays near the pilot's scale. **Answered: capital-raise execution is the gating risk.**

14. **Downside precedent?** [[Nigerian digital lender Lidya shuts down]] shows Nigerian SME-credit models fail on unit economics/collections even when well-intentioned. **Relevant cautionary comp.**

15. **What is the one KPI that proves it real?** Per the source: speed of the first $250k loans from nomination to payout, and the subsequent default rate. **Answered — watch payout velocity + loss curve.**

Importance: 3/5 — A substantively new, well-structured mechanism addressing a real and acute Nigerian SME credit gap, built on a proven (if tiny) pilot, with notable partners. But it is announcement/early-pilot stage: the headline numbers are aspirational and capital-raise-dependent, committed capital is undisclosed, there is no loss-curve data, and the addressable market is a narrow VC-alumni funnel rather than broad inclusion. Real but incremental; weight above a routine launch, well below a system-changing DFI programme.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** Nigeria's MSME credit gap is the structural "why now": only ~4% of an estimated ~40m MSMEs hold a formal bank loan, against a financing gap put at ~$236bn (per Stears, via leadership.ng, 2026). Collateral is the binding constraint — the note's own white-paper citation (22% of Sub-Saharan African firms hold a bank loan/line of credit vs a 36% global average) matches this. Structure: Nigerian SME lending is collateral-heavy and short-tenor; the value this fund adds sits in the *risk-transfer* layer (a loss-backstop), not in origination. Barriers to entry are capital (reserve) + regulatory + the nomination network — not technology. Demand-side driver reinforcing the thesis: equity funding has collapsed (Nigerian startups raised $11.3m in Sept 2026 after a one-deal-inflated $364m Aug), pushing founders toward debt, exactly the gap a guarantee fund targets. This is a bank-led instance of a well-established development-finance tool — the partial credit guarantee (PCG), used across Africa by the African Guarantee Fund, Afreximbank and DFI-backed vehicles (per ILO/AfDB/World Bank). State-side precedent landed weeks earlier: the World Bank's FINCLUDE ($500m, Dec 2025) routes guarantees through Development Bank of Nigeria's Impact Credit Guarantee Limited (which had guaranteed >₦574bn / ~97,000 MSMEs by Mar 2026).

**Competitive landscape.** KPIs a guarantee fund runs on: leverage ratio (credit enabled ÷ reserve), guarantee coverage % per loan, portfolio default/claims rate, and nomination-to-payout conversion. Players: public/DFI schemes (DBN/ICGL via FINCLUDE, African Guarantee Fund) vs this private, bank-coupled vehicle; direct SME debt lenders (FairMoney, bank SME desks) compete on the same end-borrower but carry full credit risk. Basis of competition here is risk-sharing terms + distribution (the nominating network), not price. Sterling is a mid-tier Nigerian bank — Sterling Financial Holdings reported FY2025 total assets of ₦3.91tn, loans/advances ₦1.41tn, PAT ₦76.3bn, NPL ratio 4.7% (per africanfinancials/BusinessDay). Position (analysis): niche/first-mover among *private bank-led* SME guarantee platforms in Nigeria, built on a ~2-year pilot (Cascador nominates/guarantees, Sterling lends); moat is the proprietary nomination network + pilot track record (switching costs/intangibles), but it is thin and replicable by any bank-DFI pair.

**Comps & multiples.** No valuation/round attaches to this news, so trading multiples are n/a. The load-bearing arithmetic is the leverage claim: $200m reserve supports "up to $1bn" outstanding credit = 5.0x ($1,000m / $200m). That is *above* the ~4x leverage typical of PCG instruments (per IISD/AfDB) — aggressive, and contingent on raising more reserve capital + low realised default. Initial loans ~$250,000 each → the first $200m-backed tranche implies order-of-magnitude ~hundreds-to-low-thousands of loans before the $1m ceiling applies (not disclosed; `[UNSOURCED]`). Internal comps (`grep` fallback; semsearch 402): [[Zempler Bank partners with iwoca to improve access to funding for SMEs]] (bank + lender SME-access partnership), [[GrowXCD Finance raises Rs 200 crore for SME lending]] and [[Klar launches Klar Empresarial for Mexican SMEs]] (EM SME-credit expansion), [[Paystack acquires Ladder Microfinance Bank in Nigeria]] and [[FairMoney enters acquisition talks with Shara]] (Nigeria SME/consumer-credit consolidation). FINCLUDE/ICGL is the closest live comp but is not in-base. Distribution not computed — different instruments, no comparable valuation figures.

**Risk flags.**
1. **Leverage/claims risk** — the 5.0x reserve-to-credit ratio only holds if realised SME default stays low; a credit-cycle downturn in a high-inflation naira economy could exhaust the reserve faster than modelled, and the $1bn capacity depends on *future* DFI/impact capital that hasn't been committed (de-PR: "up to" and "once fully capitalised" are conditional).
2. **Concentration + conflicted nomination** — borrowers are nominated only by ecosystem partners (VC funds/studios) who have an equity interest in the companies they refer; this channels guarantees toward a narrow, VC-backed cohort and risks adverse selection (guaranteeing the loans investors themselves won't fund with equity).
3. **Economics/liability undisclosed** — who is silent on what: guarantee pricing/fees, exact coverage %, first-loss waterfall across the three Riverwell entities, and who eats residual losses beyond the reserve are all unstated. Sterling still underwrites, approves and collects, so execution risk stays with the bank.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** (analysis) If it works, this is a template for *privately-financed, bank-coupled* SME guarantees in Nigeria — a lighter, faster alternative to sovereign/World Bank schemes (FINCLUDE) — and could pull mid-tier banks into SME lending they otherwise avoid on collateral grounds. Falsifiable thesis: the fund is additive only if it crowds in fresh DFI/impact capital toward the $1bn ceiling and converts nominations to payouts quickly. Watch (per the note): how fast the first $250k loans move from nomination to disbursement, and any disclosed guarantee-reserve top-up — absence of either within ~2–3 quarters would mark it as a pilot rebrand, not a scaling platform.
