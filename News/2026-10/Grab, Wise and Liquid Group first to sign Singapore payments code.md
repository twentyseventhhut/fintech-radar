 Grab , Wise and Liquid Group are the first to sign up to Singapore’s new payments industry code of conduct.
The Singapore Fintech Association (SFA) introduced the voluntary code in August 2026 to improve transparency and consumer protection across the payments sector.
It sets standards for payment service providers, including disclosing the full cost of transactions before customers proceed.
This covers transaction fees, exchange rates and any mark-ups.
The code also addresses fair advertising, documented fraud-prevention frameworks, data privacy and security, and regular stress-testing of critical payment systems.
Providers Must Assess Their Own Compliance
The code applies to regulated fiat payment services under Singapore’s Payment Services Act 2019, including services offered by licensed and exempt payment providers. Digital payment token services are excluded.
Participation is voluntary, with providers required to assess their own policies, processes and systems against the code’s standards before publicly declaring their adherence.
These declarations remain valid for one year, after which providers must complete another self-assessment to maintain their status.
The code complements existing Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) regulations rather than replacing them.
Grab, which helped develop the code ahead of its August launch, said it is now working alongside Wise and Liquid Group to put the standards into practice.
The company hopes more payment service providers will follow suit.
Shrawan (SK) Saraogi, Asia Pacific Head of Banking and Expansion, Singapore CEO at Wise, said,
 “Because the code is voluntary, its success depends on whether payment service providers choose to adopt it. The code encourages payment service providers to assess themselves against its principles and publicly declare their adherence. Over time, if more providers do so, consumers can increasingly expect the same standard of pricing transparency across the industry—not just from a handful of companies, but as the norm for payments in Singapore.”

## Контекст

<!-- enrichment:context -->
**[0] What happened.** On 2026-10-28 fintechnews.sg reported that Grab, Wise and Liquid Group became the first three payment service providers (PSPs) to publicly declare adherence to Singapore's new **Payments Industry Code of Conduct**. The Code is a *voluntary*, self-assessment-based standard — it is NOT a MAS regulation. It was published by the **Singapore FinTech Association (SFA)**, an industry body, on 2026-08-03, and these three firms are the first signatories ~3 months after launch. (Source: https://fintechnews.sg/139753/payments/grab-wise-liquid-group-singapore-payments-code/ ; launch: https://fintechnews.sg/135262/payments/singapore-payments-code/)

**[1] What the Code actually is / who administers it.** The SFA (industry association, not the regulator) administers the Code. It applies to **regulated fiat payment services under the Payment Services Act 2019** (both licensed and exempt providers); digital payment token (crypto) services are **excluded**. Core commitments: (a) disclose the *full* cost of a transaction up front — principal + fees + exchange rate + any FX mark-up + final amount — before the customer proceeds; (b) a ban on "drip pricing" (adding unavoidable charges mid-flow); (c) no "free"/"zero fee" marketing if an FX mark-up is embedded unless prominently disclosed; (d) documented fraud-prevention frameworks (real-time monitoring, incident response, scam education), with card-liability standards "broadly aligned with banks"; (e) data privacy/security and regular stress-testing of critical systems. Adherence is by **self-assessment + public declaration, valid one year**, then reassessment. The Code **complements, not replaces, MAS rules** — where MAS is prescriptive, firms must comply regardless of Code status. (Sources: https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/08/singapore-sfa-publishes-payments-industry-code-of-conduct ; official text: https://singaporefintech.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/SFA-Payments-CoC.pdf ; https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322758/20260803/singapore-payments-code-bans-drip-pricing-forces-fx-markup-disclosure-before-transfer.htm )

**[2] Why these three, why "first."** Grab says it **helped develop** the Code ahead of the August launch — so its signing is unsurprising/self-interested. Wise's whole brand is transparent-FX pricing ("we show the mark-up"), so a transparency code is natural low-cost marketing for it; Wise's APAC/Singapore chief Shrawan (SK) Saraogi explicitly framed success as depending on *more* PSPs adopting it so transparency becomes "the norm… not just a handful of companies." Liquid Group is a Singapore cross-border QR/remittance infrastructure player. All three gain a reputational/marketing edge from being first-movers on a transparency pledge; none incur a binding regulatory obligation.

**[3] Regulatory / market context (internal).** Singapore's payments regime is heavily licence-driven under the PS Act 2019 — recent notes: [[MAS grants Banking Circle's BC Payments a major payment license]], [[Hong Kong's Reap secures MAS Major Payment Institution license]], [[2C2P secures Major Payment Institution licence in Singapore]], [[MAS licenses Coda Payments for cross-border money transfers]]. Cross-border FX transparency is a live regional theme: [[Cambodia and Singapore complete cross-border QR linkage]] explicitly markets "transparent FX rates before confirming payment." This Code layers a *soft-law* transparency pledge on top of that hard-licensing backdrop, mirroring Wise's long-running global campaign against hidden FX mark-ups.

**[4] So what / significance.** Modest-but-real signal: Singapore is pushing payment-cost transparency via industry self-regulation rather than a new statute, and the first adopters are a super-app (Grab), a global transparency-first remittance player (Wise) and a local cross-border infra firm (Liquid Group). The test is adoption breadth — a voluntary code with 3 signatories is a PR milestone, not an industry standard, until banks and the large incumbent PSPs sign. Watch whether MAS later hardens parts of it into rules and whether non-adherence becomes a competitive disadvantage.

## Челлендж / ред-тим

<!-- enrichment:challenge -->
**Red-team questions**

1. Is this binding? No — it is a *voluntary* SFA industry code, self-assessment-based, not a MAS regulation. The headline "sign Singapore payments code" can be misread as regulatory; it is soft law.
2. Who enforces it? The SFA (an industry association), not the regulator. There is no statutory penalty for non-adherence or for a false self-declaration — what is the actual accountability mechanism? Unclear beyond reputational.
3. Is "first to sign" impressive or trivial? Only 3 signatories ~3 months after an August launch could equally read as *slow/low uptake*. Is 3 a strong start or a weak one?
4. Conflict of interest: Grab "helped develop" the Code — so Grab signing its own co-authored code is near-circular. How independent is this endorsement?
5. Self-serving for Wise: Wise's entire marketing is FX-transparency. A transparency code costs Wise ~nothing and hands it a marketing cudgel against bank/incumbent FX mark-ups. Is the signing news or advertising?
6. Scope gap: crypto / digital payment token services are *excluded*. Does that leave the fastest-growing cost-opacity area (stablecoin remittance) uncovered?
7. Does "disclose full cost before transaction" change anything Wise/Grab don't already do? If these three were already transparent, the Code adds no behavioural change for them — only a pledge.
8. One-year self-reassessment with public declaration: who audits the self-assessment? Risk of greenwashing/"transparency-washing" with no third-party verification.
9. Will the entities that actually *rely* on hidden FX mark-ups (banks, legacy remittance firms) ever sign? If not, the Code reaches only firms that were already transparent.
10. Card-liability standards "broadly aligned with banks" — is that a real commitment or aspirational language? What is the enforceable floor?
11. Jurisdiction limit: applies to PS Act 2019 fiat services in Singapore only. How much of these firms' actual cross-border volume is in scope vs out-of-SG?
12. MAS relationship: Code "complements, not replaces." Could MAS simply have mandated disclosure directly? Does a voluntary code risk *delaying* harder regulation?
13. Is there any funding, revenue, user-count or market-share figure here? No financials — this is a governance/PR story, not an earnings/market event.
14. Headline-vs-body check: body and source agree (SFA voluntary code, 3 signatories, Aug 2026 launch, PS Act 2019 scope, DPT excluded, Grab co-developed). No cross-wiring.
15. Duplicate check: no prior vault note covers the SFA Payments Code of Conduct or its signing; closest are MAS *licensing* notes, which are a different (hard-law) topic. Not a duplicate.

**Importance: 2/5** — Real but minor regulatory-governance signal. A voluntary, self-assessed industry transparency pledge with only three (partly self-interested) first signatories and no enforcement teeth. Matters as a directional indicator that Singapore favours soft-law FX/fee transparency, and as free positioning for Wise/Grab, but it is not a market-moving or binding event and no financials are attached. Would warrant 3/5 only if banks/large incumbents adopt it or MAS hardens it into rules.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector context — Singapore payments & cross-border transparency.** The news sits inside a maturing MAS/SFA regulatory stack. Singapore's cross-border and payments sector is governed by the Payment Services Act 2019, under which the regulator listed roughly 218 major payment institutions authorised for cross-border money transfer by mid-2026; around 70% of licensed payment firms offer money-transfer services and ~44% support cross-border remittances. The SFA's voluntary Payments Industry Code of Conduct — launched August 2026 — layers best-practice standards (pricing transparency, fair advertising, fraud prevention, card dispute liability, data protection, operational resilience) on top of, not in place of, MAS rules. It covers regulated fiat payment services (MPI/SPI/money-changing/exempt providers) but explicitly excludes digital payment token services. Adherence is self-assessed and lapses after one year unless renewed. Singapore's digital remittances & cross-border payments market is estimated at ~US$1.4bn in 2025, growing ~11.5% CAGR toward ~US$2.7bn by 2031, so transparency rules bite on a fast-growing, crowded pool.

**Why this matters competitively.** The code's headline requirement — disclosing the full all-in cost of a transfer (fees, FX, mark-ups) up front — is squarely the pricing model Wise has campaigned on globally for years, so Wise signing first is low-cost signalling that codifies an existing competitive advantage against banks and remittance incumbents that rely on opaque FX spreads. Grab, which co-developed the code, uses it to burnish its licensed-provider credentials across its GrabPay/GrabFin and digibank footprint in Southeast Asia. Because adherence is voluntary, the near-term risk is that only transparency-native players opt in while spread-dependent incumbents abstain, limiting the "industry norm" effect Wise's APAC head flagged. Watch for the next cohort of signatories (the roster of ~218 MPIs — including names like Ripple Markets APAC, BC Payments/Banking Circle, Reap, TransferMate, ShopBack already active under MAS) as the real adoption signal.

**IR grounding — Wise (own-filed scale, PRIMARY).** Wise's disclosures show it is a genuine cross-border scale player entering this: personal cross-border volumes of £106.4bn in FY2025 (+22% YoY), with Wise customers sending/converting £68.4bn in H1 FY25 (+19% YoY); Wise Business customers alone sent or spent ~US$4.1bn on average every month in FY2025, with business cross-border volumes up 26% vs FY2024 (Wise Group plc UK Prospectus, 13 Apr 2026, drive: https://drive.google.com/file/d/1uar9Bf3bH1cM17zgP3ClDdYW8SkBxXKC/view). Crucially for a transparency code, Wise is already pushing take rates DOWN as strategy: cross-border take rate fell 10bps YoY to 52bps in H1 FY26 even as cross-border volume grew 24% (outpacing 5% cross-border revenue growth), an explicit "strategic investment into reduced prices" (Wise plc H1 FY26 Results, 6 Nov 2025, drive: https://drive.google.com/file/d/1GtVDA-t7FyRZnqt9LmpWSmmV93F9naDH/view). Business take rates averaged 0.44% FY2025 (UK Prospectus, link above). This confirms the code aligns with, rather than threatens, Wise's economics.

**IR grounding — Grab (own-filed scale, PRIMARY).** Grab's Financial Services segment is its fastest grower but still sub-scale vs ride/delivery: FS revenue grew 43% YoY (38% cc) to US$107m in Q1 2026, driven by lending across GrabFin and its digibanks; gross loan portfolio accelerated 130% YoY to US$1,438m (from US$625m a year prior) and total loans disbursed hit an all-time-high US$1.1bn, with segment adjusted EBITDA improving to -US$17m from -US$30m YoY (Grab Q1 2026 earnings press release / prepared remarks, 5 May 2026; GMV operating-metric definition per Grab 6-K filings, drive: https://drive.google.com/file/d/1YlnIBm_wKrG5BEBqwgaSToZfNGLvei4q/view). Grab's FS arm is payments-plus-lending and still EBITDA-negative, so the transparency code is more a regulatory-positioning play (it co-authored it) than a cost event.

**Risk flags.**
1. Voluntary-adoption risk: no enforcement teeth — if spread-dependent incumbents don't sign, the "industry norm" fails to materialise and the three signatories carry the transparency burden alone while competitors keep opaque pricing.
2. Scope-gap risk: the code excludes digital payment token (crypto/stablecoin) services, leaving a fast-growing, fee-opaque corner of Singapore payments uncovered even as stablecoin rails expand regionally.
3. Margin/greenwashing risk: upfront all-in-cost disclosure invites direct price comparison; Wise is already cutting take rates (52bps H1 FY26), and broad mandatory-style transparency could compress take rates sector-wide, pressuring providers whose FS units are still loss-making (e.g. Grab FS at -US$17m adj. EBITDA).
4. Annual self-assessment / credibility risk: adherence lapses after one year and is self-declared with no third-party audit, so "Code Adherent" status could become a marketing label detached from actual pricing behaviour, eroding its signalling value if lapses or non-renewals go unnoticed.

## Earnings Review

<!-- enrichment:earnings_review -->
**Verdict (headline read).** Not an earnings print — a regulatory/code-of-conduct story (first three signatories to the Singapore FinTech Association's voluntary payments-transparency code). Scale check on the business the code touches — fee/FX/markup disclosure on regulated fiat payments and remittances: both signatories are IN-LINE-to-strong on their latest reported numbers. Grab Financial Services revenue $107m (+43% YoY) in Q1 2026; Wise cross-border volume $243.5bn (+31% YoY) with underlying income $2,502.8m (+19% YoY) in FY2026 (ended 31 Mar 2026). The pricing-transparency mandate bites Wise's model (take-rate disclosure is already its marketing pitch) far more than Grab's (fintech is still a small, loss-narrowing slice of a mobility/delivery group).

**Key figures (with growth).**
- Grab (Q1 2026, reported 5 May 2026): Group revenue $955m (+24% YoY); On-Demand GMV $6.1bn (+24% YoY); Group adjusted EBITDA $154m (+46% YoY from $106m); profit for the period $120m (up from $10m prior-year). Financial Services (the segment the SG code touches): revenue $107m (+43% YoY; +38% constant-currency); segment adjusted EBITDA –$17m (improved $13m YoY from –$30m); total loans disbursed $1.1bn (+67% YoY, all-time high); gross loan portfolio $1,438m (+130% YoY); GXS/GX Bank customer deposits $1,630m (roughly flat QoQ).
- Wise (FY2026, ended 31 Mar 2026, reported 25 Jun 2026; now reporting in USD): cross-border volume $243.5bn (+31% YoY); underlying income $2,502.8m (+19% YoY); cross-border take rate 0.52% (–6bps YoY — deliberate price cuts); active customers 18.9m (+21% YoY); customer holdings/balances $39.0bn (+40% YoY); underlying income before tax $660.4m at a 26% margin (above the 20–25% guided range).

**By segment / driver.** Grab: payments/lending is the fastest-growing but smallest leg — FinServ +43% vs Group +24%, yet $107m is ~11% of group revenue and still EBITDA-negative. The transparency code lands mainly on GrabPay/wallet and remittance-adjacent flows, not the mobility/delivery core. Wise: the entire P&L is cross-border payments/remittance — the code's "disclose full cost incl. fees, FX and markups" requirement is effectively Wise's existing go-to-market (upfront pricing, mid-market rate), so signing codifies a position it already markets; the 6bps take-rate cut shows it is actively competing on transparent price.

**vs expectations / prior period.** No consensus frame — this is a regulatory item, not a scheduled print; beat/miss N/A. Momentum vs prior periods: Grab FinServ revenue accelerated ($107m Q1-26 vs the trajectory off FY2024's smaller base), loan book +130% YoY is the standout. Wise volume growth held ~+24–31% across recent quarters (Q1 FY26 +24% YoY to £41.2bn; H1 FY26 +24%), with the structural take-rate glide-down continuing (52bps). `[[Grab-Reports-Third-Quarter-2025-Results]]` · `[[WISEplcH1FY26Results]]`.

**Guidance / forward.** Grab FY2026 guidance (per release/press): revenue $4.04bn–$4.10bn, adjusted EBITDA $700m–$720m (figures from the Q1-2026 earnings release cycle; the EDGAR press-release body did not restate the full-year numbers). Wise FY2027: net revenue growth "around the middle" of its 15–20% medium-term range; income-before-tax margin "around the top" of the 20–25% range; medium-term targets reiterated (15–20% income CAGR; 20–25% margin until additional interest payouts commence). Neither company's guidance is affected by the voluntary SG code — it is a conduct/disclosure standard, not a fee cap.

**Thesis-flags.**
1. Regulatory tailwind, not cost. The code is voluntary and disclosure-only (no price cap), complementing MAS rules — so it adds compliance/stress-testing overhead but no direct revenue hit. Second-order: it raises the industry transparency bar, which structurally favours Wise's already-transparent pricing vs opaque-markup competitors → mild competitive moat, not a margin risk.
2. Wise's take-rate glide-down is strategic, not distress. 52bps (–6bps YoY) with volume +31% and PBT margin 26% shows price cuts funding volume/customer growth — the code hard-wires the transparency Wise already weaponises. Flag: if the take rate keeps falling faster than volume scales, income growth (+19%) could decelerate toward the lower end of the 15–20% band.
3. Grab fintech still sub-scale and loss-making. FinServ EBITDA –$17m despite +43% revenue and a +130% loan book; the code touches a small part of a group now solidly profitable at the top ($120m quarterly profit). Flag: lending growth that fast (loans disbursed +67%, book +130%) raises credit-quality/reserve questions the press release is quiet on — watch asset quality, not the SG code.
