# Bank of America emails customers warning over AI agent access

> [!info] 2026-10-22 · 2 упоминаний · 2 источника(ов) с текстом
> Каналы: TechCrunch — Fintech

## Агрегированный текст (из дайджестов)

[TechCrunch — Fintech] This latest warning comes as banks are still exploring how AI agents fit into the financial world but are reportedly grappling with unauthorized agents accessing their customers’ money. As we discussed onstage at TechCrunch Disrupt, banks, payment providers, and processors are still trying to figure out how to allow customers access to their bank accounts using AI tools, such as for agentic payments, which allow people to set agents to autonomously buy things at the best price or the right time.

[TechCrunch — Fintech] A spokesperson for Bank of America confirmed the authenticity of the bank’s email that was posted to social media. But heed the bank’s warning, for if things go wrong, it’s on you. Account agreements at most banks hold customers responsible for anyone they give their log-in to. “If you do share your credentials,” its email reads, “keep in mind that you’re responsible for activity resulting from that access.”

Major U.S. banks are detecting unauthorized third-party AI agents operating inside customer accounts, with some agents attempting to take actions that appear to be independent of user instructions, sources tell Axios.

**Why it matters:**AI agents are becoming a nightmare for America's banks, making their way into banking systems faster than financial institutions can respond.

*   The difficulty in distinguishing between a human and an unauthorized bot is forcing banks to block the technology and rapidly rewrite their terms of service.

**Driving the news:** Banks including Morgan Stanley, Bank of America, JPMorgan, Wells Fargo and others are racing to contain agent risks. Some are aware of unauthorized access, people familiar with the issue said.

*   These are generally situations when an agent or AI assistant accesses a human's account with the user's permission and login credentials. Even if permitted by the user, this goes against the guidelines of most banks requiring that humans log in to their own accounts.
*   This presents significant security risks: Banking executives fear the steps agents with login access might take, especially if a user has granted purchase authority or the ability to shift funds.

*   In other instances, some banks have tracked activity when agents — programmed to fulfill requests or complete tasks on a user's behalf — contact call centers or appear to take actions independent of user instructions, the people said.

**What they're saying:**Bank executives described the situation as horrifying, with one official saying the industry has grave concerns about agents gaining access to customer accounts and taking steps that could impact user finances.

**State of play:** The concerns are at the root of the decision by several banks to restrict third-party AI agent access, the people said.

*   Morgan Stanley has begun blocking third-party agents from accessing its platforms, sources familiar with the decision said.
*   JPMorgan, Bank of America and Citi are also restricting agents.
*   A Chase spokesperson said customers should not give their usernames and passwords to anyone. A Morgan Stanley spokesperson said the bank hasn't observed instances of "rogue AI agents accessing our platforms."

**Zoom out:**Most banks have authorized or are moving in the direction of allowing read-only access, which should allow agents to see customer financial information without taking action.

*   Banks already grant such access to third-party apps like Quicken for budgeting and other activities.

**Reality check:**Several industry and bank executives told Axios they recognize the need to find a middle-ground with AI agents.

*   Customers will want to use these agents going forward, which banks recognize.
*   But for now, the risks of being too permissive are too high.

**Threat level:**Many banks are broadly not able to parse whether agent account activity is user-directed, sources said.

*   One bank executive told Axios they struggle to differentiate between agents, as well as whether an agent has been authorized by a user or has gone rogue and gained access to a customer account.

**What we're watching:**On one hand, banks are best positioned to respond to the threats and opportunities of AI given their cash on hand and experience as critical infrastructure.

*   But money isn't necessarily enough to solve this problem, one source said, because of how quickly the technology is developing and revealing new weaknesses in real time.
*   Banks are investing in preventing worst-case scenarios, but they are also preparing in case they do happen, including with "day after" scenario planning that would allow them to quickly secure or restore customer access, sources said.

**The bottom line:**The rapid pace of AI advancement is becoming a security nightmare for America's financial institutions.

## Контекст

<!-- enrichment:context -->
**[0] What happened.** On ~2026-10-22 Bank of America emailed retail customers a notice ("Protecting your finances in the AI World") warning them NOT to share their banking username/password with any third party — explicitly "including an AI-enabled tool or agent that offers to act on your behalf." BofA confirmed the email's authenticity after a screenshot circulated on social media. The key liability line: "If you do share your credentials, keep in mind that you're responsible for activity resulting from that access." The warning lands amid an Axios scoop (2026-10-22) reporting that major U.S. banks — Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citi, Wells Fargo, Charles Schwab — are detecting *unauthorized* third-party AI agents operating inside customer accounts, sometimes taking actions that appear independent of user instructions (e.g., agents calling bank call centers on their own). Several banks are restricting or outright blocking third-party agent access; most are moving toward permitting only *read-only* access. Sources: <https://www.axios.com/2026/10/22/ai-agents-banks-unauthorized-accounts-access>, <https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/27/bank-of-america-kindly-asks-that-you-dont-share-your-banking-password-with-your-ai-assistant/>.

**[1] Why it matters.** This is the first high-profile instance of a top-tier U.S. bank directly telling consumers to keep AI agents *out* of their accounts — a defensive posture that cuts against the agentic-commerce narrative that AP2/Visa-TAP-style protocols were meant to enable ([[Google launches Agent Payments Protocol for AI commerce]], [[Fintech Wrap Up: Visa Trusted Agent Protocol reference]]). The friction is structural: consumer-facing AI assistants (OpenAI Operator-style browsing agents) access accounts via credential-sharing / screen-scraping, the exact insecure method banks have fought for years and that the CFPB's §1033 open-banking rule was meant to retire in favor of bank-run APIs. Banks cannot reliably distinguish a human from a user-authorized agent from a "rogue" agent, so they fall back on the oldest tool they have — the account agreement's "you shared credentials, you own the liability" clause (mirrored almost verbatim in the U.S. Bank Digital Services Agreement cited in-note).

**[2] Key numbers / mechanics.** No disclosed figures (no breach count, no loss total). Mechanics that matter: (a) credential-sharing + screen-scraping = full read/write access, including transfers; (b) read-only aggregation (à la Quicken/Plaid) = the "middle ground" most banks say they'll allow; (c) liability shift: under standard TOU, any activity via your credentials is "presumed authorized by you" — Reg E consumer protections for unauthorized EFTs may not apply once you've voluntarily handed over login. This is the load-bearing legal point of the BofA email.

**[3] Context / regulatory backdrop.** The CFPB's §1033 open-banking rule (finalized Oct 2024) aimed to push third-party access off credential-based scraping and onto bank-run APIs with explicit consumer authorization — but it has been in litigation and under reconsideration by the agency's post-2025 leadership, leaving the API-vs-scraping question unsettled right as agentic access explodes. Industry had been building agent-authorization rails precisely to avoid this mess: Google's AP2 and Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol ([[Fintech Wrap Up: Google Agent Payments Protocol (AP2) reference]], [[Fintech Wrap Up: Visa Trusted Agent Protocol reference]]) define how an agent proves delegated authority to *transact*, and first-party-fraud risk in agentic payments was already flagged a year earlier ([[Connecting the Dots: ACI on first-party fraud in agentic AI payments]]). Banks' own appetite for agentic AI is real but internally focused ([[Fintech Wrap Up: how retail banks can put AI agents to work]], [[Fintech Wrap Up: seven agentic AI use cases in banking]]) — the Axios reporting is about *external, consumer-controlled* agents, a different and riskier vector.

**[4] What to watch.** (1) Whether BofA/JPMorgan/Morgan Stanley formally update TOS to ban agent write-access vs. only warn; (2) whether the industry converges on a sanctioned agent-authorization standard (AP2 / Visa TAP / an API-gated OAuth flow) so agents get *scoped* access without raw credentials; (3) CFPB §1033 resolution — if scraping is blessed or killed, it reshapes who bears liability; (4) response from OpenAI/Anthropic/agent vendors on honoring bank restrictions; (5) first litigated loss where a customer disputes liability for a rogue-agent transfer — that case will test the "presumed authorized" clause.

**Red-team questions**

1. Is the BofA email a *new* policy or just a restatement of long-standing credential-sharing TOU language? (Evidence leans restatement-with-AI-framing; the liability clause already exists in bank account agreements — cf. the U.S. Bank TOU quoted in-note.)
2. The note's headline frames this as BofA-specific, but Axios reports a *multi-bank* pattern (Morgan Stanley blocking, JPMorgan/Citi restricting). Is the story really "BofA warns" or "U.S. banks collectively pull back on agents"? Risk of under-scoping.
3. Did BofA actually *block* agent write-access, or merely *warn*? Reporting says it's limiting to read-only — verify the operational action vs. the PR email. The note conflates the two slightly.
4. Are the "unauthorized agents taking independent actions" claims first-hand/documented, or anonymous-sourced via Axios? ("People familiar," "sources said" — single-sourced, no logs or counts disclosed.)
5. Morgan Stanley's spokesperson said it has NOT observed "rogue AI agents" — does this contradict the "horrifying" executive quotes? Possible over-dramatization by some sources.
6. Is there any confirmed financial loss, breach, or enforcement action — or is this entirely anticipatory risk? (No loss figures exist; importance should be weighted as a *signal/posture* story, not a realized-harm story.)
7. Does credential-sharing to an agent actually void Reg E consumer protections for unauthorized EFTs? The legal "presumed authorized" claim is bank-TOU language, not settled law — could a customer still win a dispute?
8. How much of this is OpenAI-Operator/browser-agent specific vs. API-based aggregation (Plaid, which already uses bank APIs)? The scraping-vs-API distinction is the crux and the note should not blur it.
9. Does this kill or merely delay agentic commerce? AP2/Visa TAP exist precisely to avoid raw-credential sharing — is the real story "banks reject *insecure* agent access" rather than "banks reject agents"?
10. Is the CFPB §1033 angle load-bearing or speculative? The rule's status is genuinely unsettled (litigation, post-2025 reconsideration) — flag as context, not as a driver of the email.
11. Timing: the email circulated ~Oct 22 but TechCrunch covered it Oct 27 — is there a single verifiable email date, or did it spread virally with uncertain provenance? (BofA confirmed authenticity; exact send-date to individual customers unclear.)
12. Counter-view: is this bank protectionism / rent-seeking (keeping fintech/agent competitors out) dressed as security, echoing years of bank-vs-screen-scraper fights?
13. Does "read-only access is fine" hold up? A read-only agent still exfiltrates full financial data to a third party whose security the bank doesn't control (the U.S. Bank TOU explicitly disclaims this).
14. Reproducibility of the "agents calling call centers" claim — is this a handful of anecdotes or a measured pattern? Material to severity.

**Importance: 4/5** — A marquee U.S. bank publicly telling consumers to lock AI agents out of their accounts is a genuine inflection point for agentic commerce: it exposes that the delegated-authority protocols (AP2, Visa TAP) are not yet trusted enough to displace credential-sharing, and it hands banks a liability shield rooted in decades-old TOU language. Deducting one point because there's no disclosed loss, breach, or regulatory action — it's a posture/signal story, multi-sourced-but-anonymous, and partly a restatement of existing account terms rather than a brand-new rule.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** This sits at the collision of three fast-moving layers: agentic commerce/payments, bank account access, and open-banking data rights. Agentic-commerce transaction value is still tiny today — Juniper Research pegged it at ~$8bn in 2026, forecasting ~$1.5tn by 2030; McKinsey projects $3–5tn of agentic transaction volume by 2030 (per Juniper/McKinsey via Forbes/Stellagent, 2026). The structure is barbell: card networks and processors are building sanctioned agent rails (Mastercard enabled all US cardholders for agent-initiated payments, rollout completed Nov 2025; Visa's Intelligent Commerce had 100+ partners / 30+ building, per Forbes 2026-03-19), while an unsanctioned grey market of credential-sharing agents (sparked by Meta's "Muse" AI assistant, launched ~Sept 2026, per American Banker) logs into accounts directly. "Why now": consumer AI assistants that can hold and replay credentials have outpaced banks' ability to tell a user-directed agent from a rogue one — banks are rewriting ToS in real time to contain it (Axios 2026-10-22).

**Competitive landscape.** The relevant "KPIs" here are access-control primitives, not payment metrics: read-only vs. transactional scope, credential-sharing vs. tokenized/API access, and agent-identity attribution. Key actors split into (a) incumbents gating access — JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citi, Wells Fargo restricting agents; Morgan Stanley and Charles Schwab outright blocking third-party agents (incl. Meta's Muse) (American Banker / cryptobriefing, 2026-10); (b) sanctioned-rail builders — Visa, Mastercard, Stripe (top-3 on Juniper's 2026 leaderboard); and (c) agentic-banking infra vendors already shipping scoped, audited agent access as the "right way" to do this: [[Prometeo launches Agentic Banking solution]] (scoped permissions, approval thresholds, per-action audit log, identity attribution), [[Backbase launches AI-native Banking OS for agentic banking]], and [[Fiserv launches agentOS agentic AI operating system for banks]]. BofA's position: defensive/incumbent — it is taking the *softer* line of the big banks (read-only permissions allowed, action blocked) rather than Morgan Stanley's hard block (TechCrunch 2026-10-27). Its moat is regulatory + liability framing, not technology: the account agreement already makes the customer liable for anyone they hand login to.

**Comps & multiples.** No valuation/round/deal is attached to this story — it is a risk/commentary note on an incumbent's ToS email, so trading multiples are **not applicable** (BofA market cap exists but is not meaningfully comparable to anything in-story). Internal comps are structural precedents rather than valuation peers: [[JPMorgan Chase is moving to restrict Checkbook's access to its customer data,]] (2026-01 — same playbook, banks choking off third-party data access and shifting liability/terms); agentic-access infra comps [[Prometeo launches Agentic Banking solution]], [[Backbase launches AI-native Banking OS for agentic banking]], [[Fiserv launches agentOS agentic AI operating system for banks]]; sector framing [[Fintech Wrap Up BCG on retail banks putting agentic AI to work]]. Distribution not computed — no comparable valuation figures; qualitative comparison only.

**Risk flags.**
- **Liability dumped on the consumer (regulatory/reputational).** The email's core move — "you're responsible for activity resulting from that access" — pushes loss allocation onto customers for credential-shared agent actions. If an agent moves funds wrongly, the customer, not the bank, eats it. Second-order: this is exactly the liability gap US open-banking (CFPB §1033) still hasn't resolved (rule finalized 2024-10-22, enjoined/being rewritten; revised NPRM at OIRA 2026-08-06, per BPI/American Banker) — leaves BofA exposed to regulatory and reputational backlash if consumer-protection expectations (Reg E unauthorized-transfer norms) clash with the "you agreed to it" defense.
- **Screen-scraping chokepoint collides with open-banking policy (disintermediation/legal).** §1033's final rule prohibits credential-based screen scraping and mandates free API access across ~175m accounts. Banks blocking agents can be read two ways: legitimate security, or incumbents using "security" to gate competitors off customer data — the same tension in the Checkbook/JPMorgan fight. If the rewritten rule mandates sanctioned API access for agents, blunt blocking becomes non-compliant.
- **Detection gap / execution risk.** Banks admit they often cannot distinguish a user-authorized agent from a rogue one, or a human from a bot (Axios 2026-10-22). A warning email does nothing about agents that already hold valid credentials — it is governance theater unless paired with technical agent-identity controls the incumbents don't yet have.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** This is the opening shot of a land-grab over *who authorizes agent access to the account* — banks want to own sanctioned, tokenized, revenue-bearing agent rails (read-only now, transactional later) and are using liability + ToS to kill the credential-sharing grey market in the meantime (analysis). Falsifiable thesis: within ~12 months the big US banks converge on tokenized, scoped agent-access APIs (à la Prometeo/Backbase) rather than permanent blocking — trigger/catalyst = the revised CFPB §1033 rule and the first sanctioned agent-payment integration by a top-4 bank. What would break it: a high-profile loss where a customer is held liable for a rogue-agent transfer triggers Reg E / consumer-protection enforcement, forcing banks to absorb liability and slowing agent access entirely (analysis).
