## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
On 20 Oct 2026 the UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) published, **for consultation** (not a binding order), proposals that would require Apple to open the iPhone's NFC (near-field / tap-to-pay) hardware to rival wallets "on fairer commercial terms". Key de-PR'd points:
- **It is a proposal + consultation, not a ruling.** Consultation closes **17 Nov 2026**; a final decision follows. Nothing is live, mandated or decided. (The sibling note's "legal battle" framing refers to Apple *likely* challenging any final requirement in court — FT reporting — not an actual filed case.)
- **The real fight is the FEE, not "access".** Apple already opened NFC in the UK in 2024 under a *paid* commercial framework. The CMA's own words: Apple **can still charge** for access, but fees must be "substantially lower" and it "stopped short of proposing a fixed price". So this attacks Apple's fee economics, not access existing at all.
- **New technical ask:** Apple must support **both major international NFC standards**, not just its proprietary single approach — and justify charges against "principles covering costs, value and fairness".
- **The damning evidence cited:** Apple introduced a third-party NFC access path in the UK in 2024, but the CMA says **no rival wallet-type product has launched** through it — i.e. the 2024 "opening" was commercially unworkable. Contactless is now **39% of all UK payments** (vs 3% a decade ago), so the chokepoint matters more than ever.
- **Why structured this way:** this is the first substantive conduct requirement flowing from Apple's **Strategic Market Status (SMS)** designation under the DMCCA. Non-compliance with a final SMS requirement carries fines up to **10% of global turnover** — which is why Apple's rational play is to litigate scope/fee, not ignore it. The PR frame ("fairer terms, lower fees") hides that the CMA deliberately left the fee *unpriced* — the loophole Apple will fight through.

## [1] Competitors / peers
- **Beneficiaries / rival wallets:** Revolut (David Tirado, CCO: "a real opportunity for UK fintechs to compete on an equal footing with Big Tech"), PayPal, Monzo, Klarna, traditional UK banks; industry bodies Innovate Finance (Adam Jackson) and UK Finance publicly welcomed it.
- **Android/Google:** the structural counterfactual — on Android NFC tap-to-pay is open and Apple's fees are not charged; the comparator the £1.5bn consumer claim and the CMA both lean on.
- **Regulatory peers (prior art, Apple on the back foot globally):** EU (DMA 2024 commitments — NFC opened **free** + default-wallet choice; PayPal/Klarna wallets launched there) is ahead; Australia (CBA/NAB lobbying for free access, Feb 2026, [[Apple battles Australian banks over digital payments access]]); Brazil (CADE pushing contactless Pix on iPhone, [[Apple nears CADE deal to allow contactless Pix on iPhone in Brazil]], [[Apple opens iOS to new competitors in Brazil]]).
- **Why the lay of the land is this way:** Apple's NFC/Secure-Element control is a single global chokepoint; once the EU forced it open for free, every other regulator inherited a ready template and Apple's "security" defence weakened. Second-order: the marginal cost of each new jurisdiction opening falls, so the dominoes accelerate — the UK is a domino, not a new direction.

## [2] Company history / fit
Apple's UK payments conduct has been under sustained pressure since the CMA/PSR began scrutinising digital wallets in 2024–25. In **Jan 2026** a **£1.5bn opt-out class action** (James Daley / Fairer Finance) was filed at the Competition Appeal Tribunal alleging Apple Pay was the only iPhone contactless option for ~a decade and that bank fees (not charged on Android) were passed to ~50m consumers ([[Apple Pay GBP1.5bn class action launched in UK over fees]]). The current CMA proposal is the *public-enforcement* bookend to that *private* claim. Globally Apple is simultaneously conceding/fighting the same NFC point in the EU, Australia and Brazil while expanding Apple Pay into new markets (India, Oct 2026, [[Apple launches Apple Pay in India with Axis Bank]]).
- **Why Apple acts this way:** Apple Pay is a high-margin Services line that monetises the NFC monopoly via per-transaction fees + default-wallet lock-in. Opening NFC *for free* removes a Services sliver and erodes stickiness — so Apple litigates to preserve a non-zero "fair" fee and delay, even where already conceded elsewhere. Apple's response here ("we do not believe the CMA should require an approach with lower privacy and security protections") is the same security-defence it has used — and largely spent — in the EU.

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
Low novelty on two axes: (a) **as a capability**, UK NFC access has existed since 2024 (just paywalled/unworkable); (b) **as a news event**, it is a duplicate of [[Apple faces UK legal battle over iPhone tap-to-pay rules]] (same 20 Oct proposal). The only genuinely *new* substance vs the sibling note is detail colour — the explicit "both NFC standards" requirement, the 39%-of-payments stat, the 17 Nov consultation deadline, and that no rival wallet launched via the 2024 route. **Traction = zero**: proposal + consultation, no binding order, no live rival iPhone default wallet in the UK.
- **Who captures the margin:** today Apple (fee + default position). If the CMA forces an EU-style near-zero fee, margin shifts to issuers/rival wallets; but the CMA's refusal to set a price leaves Apple room to defend a "fair" (non-zero) fee. What breaks the thesis: a multi-year court fight preserving the status quo, or a settlement with a positive fee.

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
Sequence: consultation (closes 17 Nov 2026) → final conduct requirement (2027?) → likely Apple court challenge → appeal. 10%-global-turnover fine anchors stakes but is a tail. Financially immaterial to Apple near-term (UK tap-to-pay fees are a rounding error vs ~$30.8bn quarterly Services); the read-through is **precedent/moat erosion**, not P&L. Fintech sentiment uniformly positive (Revolut, Innovate Finance, UK Finance); market reaction muted.
- **Why the market goes this way:** regulators globally are converging on "open the rails"; each jurisdiction that falls lowers the bar for the next. Counterintuitive second-order: the direct revenue loss is trivial, so Apple fights a "small" fee hard precisely because the *unpriced* fee is where it can preserve the moat — the CMA's decision NOT to fix a price is the real battleground, and may let Apple keep enough of a fee to deter rivals even post-order.

**Sector & drivers.** Mobile/contactless payments. Contactless is now ~39% of all UK payments vs ~3% a decade ago (per CMA, via wkzo/Euronext, 2026-10-20) — the rails Apple gates have become mainstream, not a niche. Global digital-wallet market ~$56.8bn (2025) → ~$68bn (2026) (per sqmagazine, as of 2026); ~803m people use Apple Pay worldwide, the #1 wallet (per chargebacks911/capitaloneshopping, 2026). Structure: on-device NFC is a **chokepoint** — Apple controls the hardware secure element (SE) and, until recently, blocked host-card-emulation (HCE), so no rival could run a tap-to-pay wallet on iPhone regardless of bank/scheme relationships. Barrier = platform control, not capital. Why now: this is a conduct remedy under the UK's new DMCC Act "Strategic Market Status" regime (CMA designated Apple's mobile ecosystem SMS in 2025) — the first live test of whether the UK can replicate the EU's DMA outcome. The CMA opened a consultation on 2026-10-20 that closes **2026-11-17**; no final decision yet (per dig.watch / CMA, 2026-10-20).

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs: wallet default-share, tap volume/share, NFC access fee (bps or per-tx), number of competing wallets launched. Players: Apple Pay (incumbent gatekeeper, ~49% of US mobile-wallet users / ~54% of in-store taps 2024 — per capitaloneshopping/nchstats), Google Pay/Samsung Wallet (Android, already open), and the challengers the remedy would unlock — Revolut (publicly backed the proposal, CCO David Tirado), Curve, Klarna (launched a UK debit card + wallet 2025-10 — see in-base note), banks via UK Finance. Basis of competition: distribution + default-wallet placement, not price. Recent moves: Apple opened a third-party NFC arrangement in the UK in 2024, but the CMA notes **zero** rival wallet products launched under it — i.e. the 2024 self-remedy failed, which is why the CMA is now prescribing HCE/SE access on FRAND terms. **Latest reported quarter (Q3 FY2026).** Total revenue $109.4bn, +16% YoY; diluted EPS $2.02, +29% YoY (incl. a $0.11 tariff-refund benefit); company gross margin 50.1% (~2pp tariff-refund tailwind). One line of device context: iPhone $54.3bn (+22%), Mac $10.4bn (+29%) — not the focus here.

**Services segment (where Apple Pay / wallet revenue sits).** Services set a June-quarter record of $30.7bn, +12% YoY *despite significant FX headwinds*, with records in every category and **all-time records in cloud and payment services**; paid subscriptions passed 1.5bn. Services carries a far higher margin than hardware — in the prior Q4 FY2025 print Services gross margin was ~75% vs a blended company margin ~50%, and Services overtook iPhone as the single largest contributor to gross profit. So Services is the profit-and-moat engine, and Apple Pay/wallet "payment services" is explicitly one of its fastest-growing, record-setting lines.

**Why the CMA remedy is immaterial to the P&L but strategically live.** Apple does not disclose Apple Pay or NFC-access fee revenue separately; it is a sub-line of the "payment services" slice of a $30.7bn Services quarter, and the CMA's remedy is UK-only. Even if UK NFC-access charges fall "substantially" (the CMA set no fixed price), the direct revenue hit is a rounding error against $109.4bn quarterly revenue — immaterial (analysis). The strategic exposure is different: forcing open NFC on fairer terms, supporting both NFC standards, and letting rival wallets (à la PayPal/Klarna in the EU) sit on the iPhone tap-to-pay chip erodes the default-wallet lock-in that helps keep "payment services" at all-time-record growth. The risk to the thesis is not this quarter's dollars but the medium-term defensibility of the highest-margin, fastest-compounding part of the business (why it matters → second-order: EU precedent + UK remedy = a widening regulatory template chipping at Services' structural margin story).
