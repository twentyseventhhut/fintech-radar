South Africa’s Reserve Bank (SARB) has formally appointed Wise Payments South Africa (Pty) Limited as a Category Two Authorised Dealer in Foreign Exchange with Limited Authority (ADLA).
The licence gives Wise a place among the dealers SARB authorises to handle foreign exchange, and it follows a year in which the company has steadily built its presence in the country and region.
The appointment appeared as Notice 4183 in the Government Gazette on October 2 and in Exchange Control Circular No. 20/2026 dated October 5, ten months after the British fintech first announced conditional approval.
The Category Two tier carries defined limits and is the most realistic path to a remittance licence in South Africa. The SARB says this category covers bureau de change services, certain transactions under the single discretionary allowance — which permits each applicant to transfer up to R2 million (about $120,000) annually — and money remittance services offered in partnership with external money transfer operators.

Wise announced the conditional approval on December 1, 2025. Nadia Costanzo, its director of banking and expansion for Latin America, the Middle East and Africa, said at the time: “We look forward to actively engaging with SARB as it continues to modernise and develop its regulatory framework to fuel financial innovation .”

 What has Wise done since December 
In April, Wise Platform, the company’s infrastructure arm, announced that Capitec, the country’s largest bank by customer base, would use its tech to power international payments for individuals and businesses directly from their Capitec accounts.
That deal gave Wise a large domestic distribution channel before its own appointment was final.
Days after the gazette notice, BusinessTech reported that Wise was hiring a country manager in South Africa, although Wise declined to comment on the role.
The job posting centres on owning and expanding Wise’s regulatory presence in the country, which suggests the company plans to run its South African operations through a local leader.
The regulatory setting has been moving in Wise’s favour, too. Media reports note that SARB has been reforming the National Payment System to let firms outside the banking sector access payments infrastructure directly. Wise’s appointment arrives in the middle of that reform and gives it a concrete example.

## Контекст

<!-- enrichment:context -->
### [0] What happened
On 2 Oct 2026 the South African Reserve Bank (SARB) formally appointed **Wise Payments South Africa (Pty) Ltd** as a **Category Two Authorised Dealer in Foreign Exchange with Limited Authority (ADLA)** — published as Notice 4183 in the Government Gazette (2 Oct) and in Exchange Control Circular No. 20/2026 (dated 5 Oct). This converts the *conditional* approval Wise announced on 1 Dec 2025 into a live regulated footing inside SA's exchange-control system. Category Two covers bureau-de-change services, certain single-discretionary-allowance transactions (up to R2m / ~$120k per applicant annually), and money remittance offered in partnership with external money-transfer operators. Wise has not yet set a launch date for its own SA consumer service.

### [1] Why it matters
This is Wise's **first local operating licence on the African continent** and — with the Nigerian IMTO conditional approval (Mar 2026) — gives it regulated standing in **two of Africa's largest remittance markets**. South Africa is the primary SADC send-market (the country→rest-of-SADC remittance corridor is a multi-billion-rand flow serving Zimbabwean, Mozambican and Malawian diaspora workers). A direct licence lets Wise operate inside exchange control rather than purely through bank partners, strengthening the economics of its local-pooling / own-ledger netting model. It also lands mid-way through SARB's National Payment System modernisation (the Payments Ecosystem Modernisation programme and the exemption push to let non-banks access the NPS directly for e-money and remittances) — Wise's appointment is an early concrete example of that opening.

### [2] Key numbers / facts
- Licence tier: **Category Two ADLA** (independent MTOs sit in Category Three).
- Single discretionary allowance cap: **R2 million (~$120k)** per applicant per year.
- Timeline: conditional approval **1 Dec 2025** → gazette **2 Oct 2026** → circular **5 Oct 2026** = **~10 months**.
- Distribution already in place: **Capitec** (SA's largest bank by customers, ~25m) adopted **Wise Platform** for international payments in **April 2026** — see [[dLocal integrates Capitec Pay for A2A payments in South Africa]] / [[Capitec hits 25 million clients as profits surge 26%]] for Capitec's scale.
- SA remittance market: est. ~US$1.97bn, ~5.2% CAGR; **~62% of flows are digital** (2025). Incumbents: Mukuru (17m+ customers), Mama Money, Western Union, MoneyGram, WorldRemit.
- Post-gazette, Wise was reported hiring a **SA country manager** to own regulatory presence.

## Челлендж / ред-тим

<!-- enrichment:challenge -->
### Red-team questions
1. Is this genuinely new, or just the Dec-2025 conditional approval recycled? (Answer: new — formal gazette appointment 2 Oct 2026 converts conditional → live; distinct event.)
2. Does Category Two actually let Wise run its full consumer product, or only a constrained slice? The R2m/yr single-discretionary-allowance cap and the mandatory partnership with external MTOs suggest the licence is narrower than a full remittance authorisation.
3. No launch date has been announced — is a gazetted licence being over-read as a live market entry? The product's real reach "will show how far Category Two reaches in practice."
4. How dependent is Wise on the Capitec distribution deal (Apr 2026) vs. its own direct-to-consumer service? If Capitec is the main channel, the licence is more regulatory hygiene than a standalone growth driver.
5. What exactly is Wise's economic edge over entrenched incumbents Mukuru (17m+ customers, now a transactional bank) and Mama Money on SA→SADC corridors, where the diaspora base and cash-out networks matter more than FX transparency?
6. Does Category Two's dependence on "external money transfer operators" mean Wise must pay/partner with the very Category Three MTOs it competes with? Margin and control implications?
7. Is SA→SADC (mostly lower-income migrant corridors, cash-heavy) actually a good fit for Wise's digital, bank-account-centric model?
8. Exchange-control reporting/oversight burden: does operating inside SARB's regime add compliance cost that erodes Wise's low-fee positioning?
9. Regulatory contingency: the NPS/non-bank-access reforms are still in draft/consultation — could the rules change the value of a Category Two licence before Wise even launches?
10. Is the "two of Africa's largest remittance markets" framing (SA + Nigeria) overstated when the Nigerian licence is itself still *conditional*?
11. FX/sovereign risk: rand volatility and SA capital-control tightening could directly hit a remittance-FX business.
12. Competitive response: incumbents and banks (Capitec itself) could in-house cross-border; is Wise's partner today a competitor tomorrow?
13. Materiality to the group: how much revenue can a capped-allowance SA remittance tier realistically add to a ~$2.5bn-revenue global business?

Importance: 3/5 — First African operating licence for a major global fintech and a concrete proof-point of SARB's NPS liberalisation, strengthened by the Capitec channel and the parallel Nigeria push. But it is an incremental, partnership-constrained Category-Two tier with no launch date, limited near-term group materiality, and entrenched local incumbents — strategically notable, not market-moving.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** Cross-border remittance into/within Africa is large but structurally expensive. Sub-Saharan Africa receives ~$100bn/yr through formal channels and carries the world's highest remittance cost — ~7.9–8.78% average vs the World Bank's <3% target, with intra-African corridors far worse (SA→Mozambique ~14.2%, SA→Malawi ~13.8%, SA→Zimbabwe up to ~12.7%) (per World Bank Remittance Prices Worldwide / The Open Letter, as of Q4 2025). South Africa's own remittance market is comparatively small — ~$1.25bn in 2025, ~5.2% CAGR to ~$1.97bn by 2034 (per Ken Research / The Report Cubes). "Why now": SARB is reforming the National Payment System to let non-banks access payments rails directly, and Wise's Category Two ADLA appointment (Gazette Notice 4183, 2 Oct 2026) is a concrete beneficiary — the regulatory tailwind, not a single deal, is the driver. The structure is fragmented (incumbents + digital challengers + corridor specialists + mobile operators) and gated by heavy exchange-control / AML regulation — a regulatory moat that favours licensed, reporting-capable entrants over informal flows.

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs: cross-border volume, take rate (bps), active customers, cost-to-send %. Players in SA: incumbents Western Union, MoneyGram, Xoom (PayPal); digital WorldRemit, Wise; African-corridor specialists Mukuru, Mama Money, Hello Paisa, NALA. Basis of competition: price (cost-to-send on high-fee corridors), delivery reach (M-Pesa/mobile payout), and distribution. Recent moves: Capitec (SA's largest bank by customers) partnered Mama Money (Aug 2025) to cut migrant-corridor fees targeting Kenya/Lesotho/Malawi/Mozambique/Uganda/Zambia/Zimbabwe; separately Capitec adopted **Wise Platform** (Apr 2026) to power its international payments — so Wise sits on both sides (infra supplier + now its own licensed ADLA). Wise's conditional approval dates to Dec 2025; formal appointment Oct 2026; it is also hiring a SA country manager (Oct 2026). Position: **catching up / niche-regulated** — Wise holds standing in two of Africa's largest remittance markets (SA + Nigeria IMTO, see [[Wise secures its own Nigerian remittance (IMTO) licence]]), but Category Two is partnership-constrained (money remittance only via external transfer operators; independent MTOs are Category Three) and SA single-discretionary-allowance caps transfers at ~R2m (~$120k)/yr. Moat: scale + its local-pooling/netting ledger (cost advantage), not local-corridor depth where Mukuru/Mama Money lead `(analysis)`.

**Comps & multiples.** Wise is public (dual-listed; primary US listing WSE, see [[Wise moves primary listing to United States]]). Reported FY26 (yr to 31 Mar 2026): cross-border volume £181.7bn (+25–31% by source), take rate ~51–52bps (cut from ~62bps), active customers 18.9m (+21%), net revenue $2.5bn (+19%), underlying income £1,609.2m (+18%), PBT $660.4m / ~26% margin (Wise FY26 Results, owners.wise.com; drive: https://wise.com/imaginary-v2/images/7225a78f5d177b9bba2c8152e664ee7e-Wiseplc-FY2025.pdf for FY25 baseline). H1 FY26 (to 30 Sep 2025): CB volume £84.9bn (+24%), 13.4m active (+18%), take rate 0.52% (drive: https://wise.com/imaginary-v2/images/04bfdcbc39ddf7b9cbd06aea398e014b-WISEplcH1FY26Results.pdf).
- Market cap ~$11.95–14.55bn (Oct 2026, stockanalysis / stocktitan); P/E ~24–25x.
- P/S = ~$12.5bn / $2.5bn ≈ **5.0x** (using mid-cap and FY26 net revenue). For a ~19% revenue-grower with 26% PBT margin this is **in-line-to-rich but growth-justified** — not an outlier vs the ~0.5–20x revenue-multiple range given profitability.
- Peers: Western Union is a low-multiple declining incumbent; Mukuru / Mama Money are private (no verifiable last-round valuations surfaced) → **no data** on their multiples. Distribution not computed (fewer than 3 comparable public figures); comparison is qualitative.

**Risk flags.**
1. **Corridor economics vs mandate conflict.** SARB-type regulators want cost-to-send down (3–5% targets), but the licence confines Wise to partner-mediated remittance and capped allowances — hard to undercut Mukuru/Mama Money's M-Pesa-delivery pricing on the exact high-fee corridors (SA→Zimbabwe/Malawi) that matter, so the TAM Wise can actually address is the narrower, smaller SA market (~$1.25bn), not the $100bn SSA flow.
2. **Regulation / compliance overhang.** A regulated ADLA base brings SARB reporting + oversight, and Wise already faces a Belgian criminal AML probe (see [[Wise under Belgian criminal investigation over suspicious transactions]]) — heightened scrutiny risk on exchange-control compliance in a new market.
3. **Take-rate decel + channel conflict.** Group take rate is being deliberately cut (~62→51bps), so SA must add volume not margin; and Wise supplies Capitec (via Wise Platform) while Capitec also backs rival Mama Money — Wise risks competing with its own distribution partner.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** `(analysis)` Not a re-rating event — immaterial to a ~$12bn-cap group near-term; it's a **licence-stacking land-grab**: Wise is assembling African regulatory standing (SA + Nigeria) ahead of SARB's NPS reform to be positioned as a licensed non-bank rail rather than to win the retail-corridor price war. Falsifiable thesis: if Wise launches a live SA consumer product and still routes remittance through third-party MTOs (Category Two constraint), its cost advantage won't reach the migrant corridors, and specialists keep the high-fee SA→SADC flows — watch for (a) a launch date + pricing and (b) any move to Category Three / direct NPS access as the real catalyst.

## Earnings Review

<!-- enrichment:earnings_review -->
**Financial context (this is a licence story, not an earnings release).** Wise's latest reported results are FY2026 (year ended 31 March 2026), published 25 June 2026 as the first set under the reorganised, US-listed Wise Group plc (reporting now in USD). Headline: a **beat** vs its own medium-term guide — revenue at the top of the 15–20% range and profit margin above the 20–25% range.

**Latest reported figures (FY26, ended 31 March 2026) — vs prior year:**
- Net revenue (underlying income): **$2,502.8m, +19% YoY** — top end of the 15–20% medium-term target range. (RNS-basis underlying income ≈ £1,609.2m, +18%.)
- Cross-border volume: **$243.5bn, +31% YoY** (≈ £181.7bn, +25% in GBP).
- Cross-border take rate: **0.52%, down 6bps from 0.58% in FY25** — the deliberate price-cut / "Mission Zero" dynamic; volume growth (+31%) more than offset the lower take rate.
- Income (profit) before tax: **$660.4m, 26% margin** — above the guided 20–25% range.
- Active customers: **18.9m, +21% YoY** (Q4 split: ~10.7m personal, ~572k business).
- Customer holdings/balances: **$39.0bn, +40% YoY**; card spend $43.6bn, +37% YoY.

**Guidance (FY27, issued with FY26 results):**
- Net revenue growth: **around the middle of the 15–20% medium-term range** (a step down from FY26's top-end +19%).
- Income-before-tax margin: **around the top of the 20–25% range** (assuming stable rates).
- Medium-term targets reiterated: net revenue CAGR **15–20%** (FY24 base); PBT margin **15–20%** long run, currently guided 20–25% until additional customer interest payouts begin.

**Beat / miss / in-line:** FY26 was a **beat** — revenue top-of-range and margin above guide, with volume +31% and customers +21%. FY27 guidance is more conservative (growth mid-range), a modest deceleration signal rather than a warning.

**Read-through to the South Africa licence (thesis flags):**
- The SARB Category Two ADLA appointment is a **regulatory/expansion milestone, not a near-term P&L event** — no revenue is reported from it and Wise has not announced a launch date. South Africa is immaterial to the ~$243.5bn cross-border volume base today.
- It is **consistent with the growth thesis the FY26 numbers support**: +31% cross-border volume and +21% customers are driven by precisely this kind of market-by-market licensing and rail access (now two of Africa's largest remittance markets — South Africa plus conditional Nigerian IMTO approval). The Capitec distribution deal (Apr 2026, via Wise Platform) adds a domestic channel.
- **Take-rate watch:** the Category Two tier is built around partnerships with external money transfer operators and strict SARB exchange-control reporting. New-corridor expansion under a lower-margin, partnership-dependent structure fits the broader 6bps take-rate compression already visible in FY26 — a flag for whether African volume comes at a dilutive take rate.
- **Timing risk:** licence formalised ~10 months after Dec 2025 conditional approval; product launch still undated. Benefit to volume/revenue is a FY27+ story at the earliest, beyond the FY27 guidance window.
