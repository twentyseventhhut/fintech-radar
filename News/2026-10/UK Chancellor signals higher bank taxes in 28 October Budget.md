## Контекст

<!-- enrichment:context -->
### [0] What happened (the signal)
Ahead of the UK Autumn Budget on **28 October 2026**, reports (first in *The Guardian*, echoed by *City AM*) indicate Chancellor **John Healey** is poised to **raise taxes on UK banks**. This is a **pre-Budget signal / press report**, NOT an enacted measure. The core option under discussion is reversing Jeremy Hunt's 2023 cut to the **bank surcharge** — restoring it from the current **3% to 8%** on bank profits above the £100M allowance. The move could raise **>£2bn/year** (estimates ~£2.0–2.25bn; TUC cited ~£2.25bn). Note: this is Healey's **first Budget** as Chancellor; Rachel Reeves was removed from the role on 20 July 2026, so the "Rachel Reeves" framing is outdated — the signal is Healey's.

### [1] Current UK bank taxation (the baseline)
UK banks currently pay: standard **25% corporation tax** + a **3% sector-specific surcharge** on profits above a ~£100M allowance + a separate **bank levy** on balance sheets. Industry argues total UK bank taxation is ~**46% of profits**, vs ~42% Amsterdam, 39% Frankfurt, 29% Dublin, 28% New York. The surcharge was **8% until 2023**, cut to 3% under Sunak/Hunt partly to offset the corporation-tax rise to 25%. So the proposal is effectively a **partial reversal** back toward the old 8% level, not a new tax — framing it as a "windfall tax" is imprecise, though headlines (e.g. a mooted ~£19bn windfall levy) conflate several distinct ideas.

### [2] Market / bank reaction
On the report (16 Oct), London bank shares sold off: **NatWest -~2%** (~660p), **Lloyds -~2.5%** (~103p), **Barclays -~0.4%**. Lloyds and NatWest — almost wholly UK-exposed — are seen bearing the brunt; Barclays and HSBC are more diversified internationally. Analysts flagged the surcharge hike could shave **~6% off lenders' earnings**. Industry bodies (UK Finance / trade associations) are lobbying hard: a draft letter to Healey warns a higher burden "risks driving business out of Britain without raising revenue." Sector is highly profitable — the big four reported ~**£29.2bn pre-tax profit in H1 2026** alone — which is precisely why it is a political target.

### [3] Fiscal context (why banks are in the crosshairs)
Healey faces a squeezed fiscal position: OBR-estimated headroom against the fiscal rule (day-to-day spending covered by revenue by 2029-30) has shrunk from ~£23.6bn (March) to as little as **~£8.5–12bn**, partly due to a global bond sell-off (10-year gilt yields hit ~5.27%, an ~18-year high) and the Iran-war shock. With manifesto pledges constraining income tax/VAT/NI on "working people," **banks are the most politically palatable revenue source**. Healey is reported to have already sent the OBR a plan to raise the surcharge.

### [4] Fintech / sector angle & why it matters
A higher surcharge principally hits large balance-sheet lenders, not fintechs/neobanks (most below the £100M profit allowance), so **Monzo, Starling, Revolut et al. are largely insulated** — and a higher incumbent cost base is arguably mildly favourable competitively. But higher bank taxation can crimp lending capacity and incumbent investment in digital/partnership deals that fintechs rely on. Watch for confirmation **on 28 October**: the rate (8% vs a smaller step), the allowance threshold, and whether the bank levy also moves. Until the Budget speech, treat all figures as **reported intentions, not policy**.

## Челлендж / ред-тим

<!-- enrichment:challenge -->
### Red-team questions
1. **Signal vs fact:** This is a pre-Budget press report ("poised to", "under discussion"), not an enacted or confirmed measure. The actual Budget is 28 Oct — the rate could differ (e.g. a smaller step to 5–6%, or no change) or be dropped. Does the headline overstate certainty?
2. **Wrong Chancellor in the framing?** The note title says "UK Chancellor"; the task prompt says "Rachel Reeves". Both are stale/ambiguous — the signal is **John Healey's** (Reeves removed 20 July 2026). Verify attribution before publishing.
3. **"Higher bank taxes" is vague:** is it the surcharge (3%→8%), the balance-sheet bank levy, a one-off windfall tax, or a combination? These are distinct instruments with very different mechanics and yields.
4. **Is "8%" firm?** Some reports say "reversing Hunt's cut" (implying 8%); others cite smaller hikes or a £3bn target. The £2bn vs £2.25bn vs £3bn vs £19bn (windfall) figures come from different sources/scenarios — don't blend them.
5. **"Windfall tax" mislabel:** restoring the surcharge to its pre-2023 level is a reversal, not a true windfall tax. Headlines conflate the two. Which is actually proposed?
6. **Source quality:** City AM / Guardian report; much corroboration is from low-authority aggregators (britbrief, zetik, biggo, vercel blogs). Core facts (share moves, 3%→8%, ~£2bn) are confirmed by CNBC/Yahoo/FXStreet — treat the rest sceptically.
7. **Share-move attribution:** were the 16 Oct bank-share falls driven by the tax report specifically, or by the broader gilt sell-off / Iran-war risk-off? Correlation vs causation.
8. **Earnings-hit estimate (~6%):** whose estimate, and does it assume full 8% + no mitigation (allowance uplift, pass-through to customers, lending repricing)?
9. **Does it affect fintechs at all?** Surcharge applies above a ~£100M profit allowance — most neobanks/fintechs are below it, so the direct fintech impact is minimal. Is this story even "fintech" vs pure banking/macro?
10. **Allowance threshold:** could the Treasury raise the £100M allowance alongside the rate (as in 2023), muting the effective hit on smaller lenders? That changes the story materially.
11. **Lobbying efficacy:** banks have blocked/diluted such moves before (2015 levy phase-down, 2023 surcharge cut). Could the pre-Budget leak itself be a negotiating/trial-balloon tactic?
12. **Competitiveness claim (46% vs NY 28%):** this is UK Finance's own figure — self-interested; independent verification?
13. **Fiscal-headroom numbers (£8.5bn vs £12bn vs £23.6bn):** these are pre-Budget OBR estimates that shift with each forecast; which is current as of 22 Oct?
14. **Timing/duplication:** is there an earlier same-signal note already in the vault (e.g. a mid-Oct share-fall item)? Grep found none, so treat as fresh.
15. **Second-order effects:** could a higher surcharge accelerate incumbent cost-cutting (cf. [[HSBC plans deep UK wealth management job cuts in AI push]]) or dampen fintech partnership/investment budgets?

**Importance: 3/5 — macro/regulatory signal with real market impact (sector share moves, >£2bn at stake, flagship UK Budget), but it is a pre-Budget *signal* not enacted policy, has limited direct fintech relevance (hits large balance-sheet incumbents, not neobanks), and the specifics (rate, yield, instrument) remain unconfirmed until 28 Oct.**

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** UK banking sector, policy/taxation event (sector-wide, not a single company). The ask: Chancellor John Healey's 28 Oct 2026 Budget is widely trailed to raise the **bank surcharge from 3% to 8%**, i.e. restoring the pre-April-2023 level (Hunt cut it 8%→3% in Apr-2023 when headline CT rose 19%→25%) — per Bloomberg/Reuters/CNBC, as of 16–21 Oct 2026. Current all-in rate on relevant UK banking profits ≈ **28%** (25% CT + 3% surcharge); an 8% surcharge would take it to ≈ **33%**. Estimated extra revenue: "a little over **£2bn/yr**" (estimates cited by the BBC). For scale: total banking-sector receipts (PAYE + CT + bank levy + surcharge) were **£39.1bn** in FY2025/26 (+8.5% YoY), of which the balance-sheet **bank levy** raised **£1.4bn** (short-term rate 0.10%, long-term 0.05%) — per GOV.UK/OBR. Structure: concentrated oligopoly (Lloyds, NatWest, Barclays, HSBC dominate), sector-specific taxation is the lever because big UK banks are posting rising profits on still-elevated net interest margins. "Why now": Budget fiscal hole + eroded headroom from higher borrowing costs; banks are a politically convenient, profitable target (de-PR: "windfall"-style framing, but mechanically it's a surcharge hike, not a one-off windfall tax).

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs that matter here: **share of group profit eligible for the UK surcharge** (exposure), RoTE, CET1/capital-return capacity. Differential exposure is the whole story:
- **Lloyds** — ~85% of group profit UK-surcharge-eligible → most exposed; Barclays' own analysts cite ~20% RoTE and favour Lloyds over NatWest on capital.
- **NatWest** — ~85% eligible → equally exposed; domestic-focused.
- **Barclays** — only ~33% eligible (large US/IB mix) → ~2–3% net-profit hit; running >£15bn total capital returns 2026–28, latest £500m buyback.
- **HSBC** — only ~20% eligible (globally diversified) → least exposed.
Basis of competition here = geographic profit mix / diversification away from UK ringfenced banking. Recent moves: bank shares fell on the news (16 Oct: Lloyds −1.9%, NatWest −1.6%). UBS still sees value on accelerating loan growth despite tax risk (Oct 2026).

**Comps & multiples (forward P/E, consensus — `(sell-side)`):**
- Barclays — **7.8x** forward earnings (lowest exposure, highest IB mix)
- NatWest — **8.2x**
- Lloyds — **9.2x** (one note cites 6.4x on a different basis — reconciliation [UNSOURCED])
- UK bank sector ~**10x** → all three trade at a discount to sector.
**Sell-side EPS/profit impact of 8% surcharge (Goldman Sachs):** Barclays −**2–3%** net profit; Lloyds & NatWest −**6–7%**. Arithmetic sanity check: surcharge Δ = 5pp on UK-eligible profit; a bank with ~85% eligible profit sees ~0.85 × 5% ≈ ~4.3pp of pre-surcharge profit, pre-offset — consistent with the 6–7% net figure once base effects are included `(analysis)`. Internal comps (base): [[Connecting the Dots fintech exodus could cost London IPOs billions]] (UK-listing competitiveness drag); [[Australia to ban credit and debit card surcharges]] (unrelated "surcharge" — payments, not bank tax; excluded as non-comparable). No prior UK-bank-tax deal comp in base → "no data".

**Risk flags.**
1. **Capital-return compression** — a 6–7% profit hit at Lloyds/NatWest directly shrinks the distributable pool, so buyback/dividend guidance is the real transmission channel; shares already de-rating pre-Budget, so a surprise above 8% or added levy is the tail risk.
2. **Competitiveness / disintermediation of UK listing & lending** — UK Finance/CBI warn sector-specific tax erodes London competitiveness; second-order effect = marginal lending/pricing passed to borrowers (mortgages/SME), feeding the "challengers fill the SME gap" dynamic seen elsewhere in-base.
3. **Headline/execution risk around 28 Oct** — figure is "under discussion"/trailed, not legislated; actual rate, thresholds (£100m allowance) and any levy tweak unknown → binary event risk into the print.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** `(analysis)` This is a potential sector **de-rating catalyst** concentrated on domestic UK banks (Lloyds/NatWest) vs diversified peers (Barclays/HSBC), widening the valuation spread by profit-mix. Falsifiable thesis: if the surcharge lands at 8% as trailed, domestic names underperform diversified peers into/through 28 Oct; it's wrong if the Budget softens (lower rate, phased, or offset against the bank levy) or if rising NIM/loan-growth guidance offsets the tax drag. Trigger to watch: the 28 Oct Budget text + Q3 earnings commentary from Lloyds/NatWest on capital-return intentions.
