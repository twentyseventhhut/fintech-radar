## Контекст

<!-- enrichment:context -->
**[0] TL;DR.** The UK's Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) took **74 enforcement actions against illegal "finfluencers" in 2025**, up ~174% from **27 in 2024** and up ~7,300% from a **single action in 2023**. Most were soft measures: in 2025 the FCA issued **50 warning alerts**, ran **11 interviews under caution**, sent **7 cease-and-desist letters**, authorised **3 criminal actions** and made **3 arrests**. The data came from a Freedom-of-Information (FOI) request by broker-comparison site BrokerChooser; Finextra reported it on ~9 Oct 2026.

**[1] What's actually new.** The headline is a sharply steepening enforcement curve against social-media financial promotions, now quantified via FOI. The "74 actions" figure and the 1→74 (2023→2025) trajectory are the new, concrete data points. NB a figure-mismatch in the aggregated note text: "Only 15 of those actions led to criminal measures" is **not** a 2025 subset — external sources attribute the 15 criminal measures (and 12 criminal actions / 3 arrests) to the **cumulative 2020–2025** period, not to 2025's 74. In 2025 specifically, only 3 actions were criminal and 3 were arrests.

**[2] Numbers / mechanics.**
- Finfluencer enforcement actions: 2023 = 1; 2024 = 27; 2025 = 74 (+174% YoY; +7,300% vs 2023).
- 2025 breakdown: 50 warning alerts, 11 interviews under caution, 7 cease-and-desist letters, 3 criminal actions, 3 arrests.
- 2020–2025 cumulative: ~50 warning alerts, ~40 interviews under caution, 12 criminal actions, 3 arrests, 15 criminal measures total (~13% of actions reaching criminal outcomes).
- Context measures (2025, wider FCA strategy): ~650 social-media takedown requests, 2,329 scam-firm warnings (vs 2,240 in 2024); authorised-firm financial promotions amended/withdrawn jumped to 19,766 in 2024 (+97.5% vs 10,008 in 2023).
- June 2025 "week of action": 9 international regulators coordinated; 3 arrests, 6 criminal proceedings, 11 warning/cease-and-desist letters, 50 warning-list alerts, 650 takedown requests.

**[3] Why it matters.** The UK financial-promotions regime (tightened for crypto in Oct 2023, extending FCA perimeter over promotions targeting UK consumers) is now biting at scale, but almost entirely through warnings rather than prosecutions — only a sliver reach criminal courts. That pattern (volume up, criminal conversion low) is the real story for brokers, crypto firms and the creator-marketing economy: reputational/warning risk is near-certain, hard criminal consequences remain rare. It sits alongside parallel moves globally: UAE SCA finfluencer licensing, SEBI/Google verification of finfluencers, and Bank of Russia's softened investment-blogger regime.

**[4] Open questions.** Does the "27 in 2024" baseline reconcile with earlier FCA statements (summer-2024 charging of 9 reality-TV figures, 20 under caution)? How many of the 74 are repeat subjects vs unique individuals? What is the enforcement-to-conviction lag, and do the 3 criminal actions in 2025 progress to convictions? Does rising warning volume reflect more illegal activity or just more FCA capacity?

## Челлендж / ред-тим

<!-- enrichment:challenge -->
**Red-team / challenge questions**

1. The note says "Only 15 of those actions led to criminal measures" — but that 15 is the 2020–2025 cumulative figure, not a subset of 2025's 74. Does conflating the periods overstate 2025's criminal bite (actually only 3 criminal actions + 3 arrests in 2025)?
2. Is "74 actions" a count of *enforcement actions* or of *targets/individuals*? Repeat subjects could inflate the count without more breadth.
3. The data comes from a single FOI request routed through BrokerChooser (a commercial broker-comparison site) — is there a marketing angle, and is the FOI's figure fully reconciled with FCA's own published financial-promotions data?
4. Does "27 in 2024" square with the FCA's own 2024 disclosures (9 reality-TV figures charged, ~20 interviewed under caution)? The baselines may be measured differently year to year.
5. "+7,300% since 2023" rests on a base of exactly 1 — is the percentage a meaningful trend or an artefact of a near-zero starting point?
6. Warning alerts (50/74) are low-cost and don't require proof to the criminal standard. Is the surge real deterrence or just cheap volume to show activity?
7. How many warning alerts/takedowns actually resulted in content removal or behaviour change, versus being ignored by offshore/anonymous accounts?
8. Crypto promotions vs general investment/FX finfluencers — what share of the 74 is crypto-specific, and does the regime scope cover all equally?
9. Jurisdiction: many finfluencers operate from outside the UK. Can the FCA enforce beyond warnings against non-UK persons, and do the 3 arrests reflect that limit?
10. Is this genuinely distinct from the Feb 2026 HTX suit and the June 2025 "week of action," or is it the same enforcement wave re-reported with a new FOI number?
11. Does the headline conflate finfluencer-specific actions with the FCA's broader 2,329 scam-firm warnings and 19,766 promotion amendments?
12. Fintech angle: does this materially affect compliance-tech demand (e.g. Adclear-type promotion-vetting tools), or is it a general-regulatory item with thin fintech read-through?
13. What's the enforcement-to-conviction lag? Low 2025 criminal counts could simply reflect cases still in the pipeline rather than weak enforcement.
14. Prior-year comparison integrity: were "actions" defined consistently in 2023, 2024 and 2025, or has the FCA's categorisation changed, inflating YoY growth?

**Importance: 2/5** — Real, quantified regulatory trend with global parallels, but it is a general-regulation data point (FOI re-reporting of a known enforcement wave), with a figure-conflation risk in the source text and only thin direct fintech-company read-through.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector context — UK financial-promotions regime.** The FCA's finfluencer push sits inside the s21 financial-promotions gateway created by FSMA 2023 (live since Feb 2024): non-authorised promoters can only market to UK consumers if an FCA-authorised firm with specific "approver" permission signs off, and Consumer Duty obligations now bite on those approvals. The 2025 data point reflects enforcement, not rule-making — the regime itself is already in force, so the relevant read is rising supervisory intensity against unauthorised/undisclosed promotion, heavily crypto-skewed.

**Scale and mix of 2025 actions.** Press reporting frames the rise as +174% YoY to 74 actions, composed of ~50 warning alerts, 11 interviews under caution, 7 cease-and-desist letters, 3 authorised criminal actions and 3 arrests, plus ~650 social-media post takedowns. The bulk is soft enforcement (alerts/takedowns); criminal outcomes remain a small minority — consistent with a regulator building deterrence breadth rather than landmark penalties.

**Comps / cross-border.** This is a coordinated global trend, not a UK one-off. June 2025 saw a multi-regulator "week of action" (nine authorities incl. ASIC, plus Canada, Hong Kong, Italy, UAE). ESMA's April 2025 market-abuse guidelines extended supervisory expectations to coordinated finfluencer campaigns (Italy's CONSOB amplifying), and the SEC/FINRA line holds the engaging firm — not just the influencer — responsible, with crypto "advice" potentially triggering investment-adviser registration. IOSCO (FR/08/2025) formalised the theme internationally. Takeaway: a durable, multi-jurisdiction compliance vector, not a cyclical blip.

**Who bears the cost.** Compliance burden lands on (a) brokers/trading apps and crypto firms that use influencer marketing — now liable for promoter content and needing authorised s21 approvers (fewer firms hold the permission, so approval is no longer a formality); (b) the shrinking pool of authorised approvers taking on review liability; and (c) crypto firms specifically, who under the new regime (gateway open 30 Sep 2026, regime live 25 Oct 2027) lose the ability to lean on third-party s21 approvers and must get authorised to keep marketing to UK consumers. Net effect: structural, recurring demand for promotion-review, affiliate/influencer monitoring, disclosure and social-media surveillance tooling — tailwind for regtech (cf. internal notes on compliance-AI raises: Zania $18M Series A, ComplyAdvantage, Themis/Pasabi social monitoring).

**Risk flags (skeptical):**
1. **Headline/number discrepancy.** The digest says "from one action in 2023 to 74 in 2025" with "15 criminal measures." External reporting instead frames +174% off a 2024 base (~27 prior-year) and only ~3 criminal actions / 3 arrests in 2025. The "1 in 2023 → 74 in 2025" base effect and the 15-criminal figure are not corroborated by the sources found — treat the specific trajectory and criminal count as unverified.
2. **Enforcement ≠ revenue event.** This is a regulatory/activity signal; it does not directly move a listed name. Any "regtech tailwind" thesis is diffuse and already partly priced into the compliance-software narrative — no clean tradable catalyst here.
3. **Soft-enforcement bias.** Most of the 74 are alerts/takedowns, not sanctions; volume growth may partly reflect easier counting (automated takedown requests) rather than proportionate escalation in penalties — risk of overstating the "crackdown."
4. **Jurisdiction-specific.** UK-only metric; cross-border comps are directionally aligned but methodologies differ, so the 74 figure is not globally comparable.
