Aspire launches AI accounting tool AspireBooks in Singapore
 
Aspire has launched AspireBooks, an AI-powered accounting product built into its financial platform. Available in Singapore, it marks the company's move into accounting software.
AspireBooks combines bookkeeping, reconciliation and financial reporting in the same system customers use to manage payments and other finance tasks. It is aimed at businesses operating across currencies and markets that want to avoid moving data between separate banking and accounting tools.
The launch puts Aspire into a market long led by specialist accounting software providers including Xero and QuickBooks. It also expands Aspire's offering beyond payments and finance operations into core accounting workflows.
Businesses have traditionally handled money movement and accounting in separate systems, leaving finance teams to classify transactions and reconcile records after the fact, Aspire said. AspireBooks is designed to change that by updating books as transactions occur, with users reviewing and approving entries rather than rebuilding records at month-end.
According to Aspire, the software uses AI agents to classify transactions, suggest accounting categories and tax treatment, and match payments to invoices and bills. This keeps financial records current and keeps profit and loss statements, balance sheets, trial balances, and general ledger reports up to date.
Multicurrency accounting is central to the launch. Customers can record and manage transactions across several currencies, a feature aimed at businesses with cross-border operations.
The product also includes a feature linked to Aspire's Model Context Protocol integration, allowing customers to ask questions about financial statements in plain language and receive recommendations. A multi-entity function is due to follow, enabling businesses to manage books for several entities in one place.
Aspire developed the product with Singapore Accountants, which provided input on Singapore accounting standards and supported customer testing. The collaboration reflects Aspire's focus on local compliance as it enters a software segment where accuracy and alignment with standards are closely scrutinised.
Aspire cited internal research in support of the launch, saying 41% of its customers still manage bookkeeping manually and 63% would consider using an AI-based accounting tool. The figures suggest an opening for software that reduces manual work while remaining embedded in existing finance processes.
Andrea Baronchelli, Co-Founder and Chief Executive Officer of Aspire, said the launch is intended to address the longstanding split between banking and accounting systems.

 "In all my years as a founder, I've seen how much time businesses spend trying to close their books. It's a recurring headache, largely because corporate banking and accounting have always operated as two separate worlds," said Andrea Baronchelli, Co-Founder and Chief Executive Officer of Aspire. 

"With AspireBooks, we're bringing these two worlds together so accounting happens as a natural part of running a business, rather than becoming a separate exercise at the end of every month," Baronchelli said.
Haroon Naseer, Director of Financial Reporting at Singapore Accountants, said the product was shaped to reduce routine work for finance teams while keeping accountants involved in oversight and compliance.
"For growing businesses, the real value of accounting lies in decision-making, not data entry. By embedding accounting within the platform where money already moves and applying AI to the repetitive work of categorisation and reconciliation, AspireBooks keeps the books current and frees the accountant's time for review, compliance and advice. We are proud to have helped shape it as a founding accounting partner," said Haroon Naseer, Director of Financial Reporting at Singapore Accountants.
AspireBooks is priced at S$39 a month in Singapore, with a three-month introductory free period. Aspire serves more than 50,000 businesses in more than 30 markets and employs more than 500 people across nine countries.

The embedded "money-moves-where-accounting-happens" pitch is the same thesis as Brex/Ramp in the US and Monit in Indonesia; it is a materially new revenue line (SaaS subscription on top of a transaction-fee neobank model), not just a feature release.

Competitive context: [[Indonesian spend management platform Monit raises $2.5M]] shows the regional AI-spend/accounting adjacency.

**[4] So what / outlook.** Expect Aspire to push AspireBooks as a retention/ARPU lever across its 50k+ base, then extend to other SE Asia and new markets (US/EU) where it now holds licences. The strategic risk is channel conflict with its accounting-software integration partners and the compliance bar of being an accounting system of record rather than a data feed. Watch for: disclosed adoption/attach rates, multi-entity GA, replication in Indonesia/Vietnam/HK, and whether Xero/QuickBooks respond with deeper banking embeds.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** AspireBooks pushes Aspire from SME banking/spend into cloud accounting software. Global accounting-software market was ~$20.8–21.0bn in 2025, projected to ~$38.7–40.1bn by 2033 at ~8.1–8.4% CAGR, with cloud/SaaS the dominant delivery model (~60–78% share in 2025, per Fortune Business Insights / Global Growth Insights via secondary citations, as of 2025). Structure: globally fragmented across geographies and company sizes, but the SME horizontal is anchored by two incumbents — Xero and Intuit QuickBooks — plus local/regional players; entry barriers are regulatory/standards-based (local GAAP, tax filing, audit trust) and switching-cost-based (books are sticky once a business closes a year on them). Why now: (1) AI agents make auto-categorisation/reconciliation credible enough to shift accounting from month-end rebuild to real-time, lowering the labour cost that kept SMEs on manual bookkeeping; (2) the "banking + accounting as two separate worlds" wedge — Aspire already moves the money, so it can post entries at source, a structural advantage specialist accounting SaaS (which must ingest bank feeds) lacks `(analysis)`.

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs for accounting SaaS: ARR, subscriber count, ARPU, net revenue retention, Rule of 40. Key players: incumbents Xero (~4.4M subscribers early 2025; ARR >$1.9bn FY25) and Intuit/QuickBooks (Global Business Solutions ~$11.1bn FY25 revenue, +16% YoY), both named in the source as the market Aspire is entering; disruptors are embedded-finance/spend platforms adding accounting from the money-movement side (Aspire here; Starling/Ember [[Starling launches business tools ahead of HMRC digital tax rules]] adding Making Tax Digital tools; Pleo [[Pleo launches embedded finance solution for SMBs]]; Corpay [[Corpay launches AI capabilities for spend management]]). Basis of competition: for incumbents — depth of accounting + accountant/advisor ecosystem + local compliance coverage; for Aspire — distribution (50,000+ existing SME customers across 30+ markets) and the embedded, transaction-time bookkeeping angle. Recent moves: AspireBooks launched Oct 2026, Singapore-only, S$39/mo with a 3-month free intro, built with Singapore Accountants for local standards; multi-entity support "due to follow." Aspire's position: niche/catching-up in accounting (single-market launch, no installed accounting base vs Xero/QuickBooks' millions of subscribers and deep accountant channel), but with a genuine distribution + data moat on its own customer base `(analysis)`. Internal Aspire funding: Series B US$158M (equity+debt) led by an undisclosed growth firm with DST Global, B Capital et al., July 2025.

**Comps & multiples.** Aspire is private — no public multiple; last round US$158M (equity+debt, round not a valuation disclosure) `[UNSOURCED]` post-money. Public comp arithmetic (as of mid/late-2026, secondary citations):

Distribution not computed as a formal median — only Xero/Intuit are clean public comps and they differ in scale/mix; qualitative read: SME accounting SaaS trades ~5–9x revenue, growth-justified.

**Risk flags.**
1. **Entering incumbent territory with no accounting base.** Xero/QuickBooks own the SME accounting standard, the accountant/advisor distribution channel and the trust of filing-grade accuracy; a single-market S$39 launch is a toe-hold, and accounting is high-switching-cost — SMEs rarely re-platform books mid-year, so land will be slow even with 50k existing customers.
2. **Accuracy/compliance liability on AI-posted entries.** AI auto-categorisation and tax-treatment suggestions carry audit/filing risk; "users review and approve" shifts liability to the customer, but errors in a regulated accounting product (vs a spend dashboard) damage trust fast — the PR is silent on accuracy rates and accountant override economics.
3. **Single-market, single-standard dependence + execution.** Singapore-only at launch with multi-entity "to follow"; cross-border/multicurrency is the pitched differentiator but each new market re-opens the local-GAAP/tax-filing barrier, so the moat is distribution, not product, and scaling accounting into 30+ markets is a long regulatory grind.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** `(analysis)` This is embedded finance pushing up the stack into accounting — the same convergence seen at Starling/Ember and Pleo — testing whether "the platform where money already moves" can disintermediate specialist accounting SaaS at the SME end. Falsifiable thesis: if Aspire can convert its 50k+ base and the cited 63%-would-consider-AI-accounting demand, AspireBooks becomes a defensible attach that lifts ARPU/retention; what breaks it — low cross-sell into AspireBooks within 2–3 quarters, or stalling at Singapore without a second compliant market, would signal accounting is a feature, not a wedge. Trigger to watch: attach/adoption metrics and the multi-entity + second-market rollout.
