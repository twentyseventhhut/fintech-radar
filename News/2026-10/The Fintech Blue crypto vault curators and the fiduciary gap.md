Considerations for Issuers on the  SEC's Innovation Exemption 

On September 17th, the SEC released the Innovation Exemption: a temporary order 

granting conditional exemptive relief to Tokenized Securities Venues (“TSV”) to trade 

tokenized versions of U.S. public company stock in an environment that sits outside of the 

National Market System (aka Regulation NMS), which governs all US equities trading 

today, while the SEC considers the need for additional action to facilitate onchain trading. 

Specifically, the Innovation Exemption provides as follows: 

A framework for trading tokenized equities:  The order permits TSVs to trade certain 

categories of tokenized equities (subject to issuer opt  - outs and other restrictions, 

described below).  The order states “  TSVs can trade tokenized NMS stocks that are 

tokenized by, or on behalf of, the issuer of the underlying NMS stock or a security 

tokenized by a third party that is unaffiliated with the issuer of the underlying NMS stock.  ”

Eligible tokenized stocks must be a full digital representation of the underlying stock and 

provide holders with equivalent shareholder rights, including voting, dividends, and 

liquidation rights. Synthetic products that provide economic exposure without o  wnership 

of the underlying stock are not eligible under the exemption. Under the terms of the 

Exemption, tokenized securities traded on TSVs are to be traded in pairs against 

stablecoins, cryptocurrencies, or tokenized money market funds, but not cash.  TSVs can 

be  gin operating  as quickly as 30 days after filing  public  notice with the SEC. ____________________________________________________________________________________

opts  - out  : Before a TSV may trade stock tokenized by an unaffiliated third party, it must

Volume  and scope  limitations  : Tokenized NMS stocks traded on a TSV are subject to

How do the national market system rules work today, and what do they deliver for 

investors and issuers in terms of price transparency? 

National market system rules  normal  require securities markets to display, in real time, the 

best bids and offers for all securities they trade, and to report that data without delay to a 

centralized securities information processor (SIP). The SIP aggregates the best bids and 

offers across a  ll connected markets and publishes the National Best Bid and Offer 

(NBBO)  , the single best bid and offer for a security across all U.S. equities markets at any 

given  time during the  trading day. Markets must also report trade execution data to the 

SIP in near real time. 

Together, this system enables issuers, broker  - dealers, and investors to compare which 

markets can offer the best available prices at any given time, and to better understand 

market behavior, what is driving it, and what may happen going forward.  These rules 

deliver price transparency across many individual markets. They are essential to ensuring ____________________________________________________________________________________ 

> 4

investors and their brokers have ready access to the information needed to obtain best 

execution at any given time. 

What does it mean if a trading venue does not display its best prices, report them to 

the SIP, or do so in near real time? 

T he Innovation Exemption does not require TSVs  to display their best prices  , report them 

to the consolidated  tape or  publish trade execution data until 10 minutes after trades 

occur,  unlike traditional equity markets  . This  effectively creates a shadow market for 

equity securities that can become disconnected from the broader market in both pricing 

and price transparency. Issuers, investors, and broker  - dealers participating in TSVs will 

lack the information available elsewhe  re to understand, at any point in time, how TSV 

prices compare to prices for the same securities in the broader market. It will also impair 

their ability to understand what has just happened in the markets, or what is likely to 

happen next, with respect to  the prices of those securities. 

Where these rules do not apply, investors will not know  , or will not know in a timely 

manner, or without undue effort or expense  , whether the prices they receive on a TSV are 

better, worse, or the same as what is available elsewhere. Investors may not appreciate 

that the stock ticker they see on their television screens or devices may be inconsistent 

with the prices they actually re  ceive when buying or selling on a TSV. 

The above impairs an issuer's ability to maintain a complete, accurate, and 

contemporaneous understanding of the price and trading behavior of its own securities. 

In addition, to the extent investors are confused or receive poor outcomes on a TSV 

where the  issuer authorized its stock to trade, investors may assign blame to the issuer.

Applied Optoelectronics cited uncertainty over price, liquidity and volatility, while Nasdaq said it would opt out of having its own shares tokenized under the SEC's new framework.

Nine U.S.-listed companies have objected to tokenized versions of their stock trading on OKXICE, the joint venture between crypto exchange OKX and NYSE parent Intercontinental Exchange, according to a public notice from the venue.

The notice, dated Thursday, lists objections from Cerebras Systems (CBRS), AppLovin (APP), Applied Digital (APLD), Rocket Lab (RKLB), CoreWeave (CRWV), Verizon (VZ), Applied Optoelectronics (AAOI), Broadcom (AVGO) and UnitedHealth Group (UNH). It does not give reasons for any of the objections.

A spokeswoman for Applied Optoelectronics told the Wall Street Journal the fiber-optic maker objected because it was unsure how tokenization would affect its stock price, liquidity and volatility. She also cited the risk of investors being confused about the company's involvement in an offering it did not sponsor or endorse, and said the company saw no clear benefit "to AOI or our shareholders" that justified the uncertainty.

Eight of the nine objectors were among the 63 tickers OKXICE named on Oct. 4 as stocks it intended to offer. Cerebras was not on that list. OKXICE told the Journal it still plans to launch trading for dozens of U.S.-listed companies, including Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Tesla, JPMorgan Chase and Walmart.

OKXICE co-chair Andrew Cuomo, the former governor of New York who took the role when the venture formed in June, said in a recent interview with the Journal that he expects companies to be skeptical of tokenizing their stock. He described the venue as a first step toward tokenizing securities.

## Nasdaq plans to opt out

Nasdaq said it would opt out of having its own shares tokenized under the SEC's framework, according to an FAQ on its website. A Nasdaq spokesperson told the Journal the company does not want its shares trading on a venue that does not display best prices in real time or report to the consolidated tape, and said the exchange remains committed to tokenization.

The SEC approved a Nasdaq pilot to trade tokenized equities, The Block reported earlier this year. Nasdaq has argued that tokenized trading outside the national market system could drift away from the broader market on price and transparency, according to the Journal. Citadel Securities raised similar concerns about a "shadow" U.S. equity market in a letter to the SEC last year.

## How objections work

OKXICE plans to operate under the SEC's innovation exemption, a five-year order issued Sept. 17 that lets tokenized securities venues trade tokenized versions of U.S.-listed stocks without registering as exchanges. When a third party rather than the company itself tokenizes a stock, the venue must notify the company at least 30 calendar days before trading begins, according to summaries of the order by law firms Sullivan & Cromwell and O'Melveny & Myers.

The company then has 30 calendar days to file a notice of issuer objection. If a company objects in time, their objection bars that venue from offering the stock token, though companies are not required to affirmatively approve listings — silence means the listing may proceed. An objection applies only to the venue that sent the notice, so each new venue starts its own 30-day clock.

Securitize President Brett Redfearn told the Journal that companies could object to one venue without rejecting tokenization altogether. Stocks tokenized by Securitize, which launched tokenized U.S. stocks on Oct. 8, are expected to trade on OKXICE, the Journal reported. Rodrigo Seira, a partner at law firm Cooley, told the Journal the SEC is using the exemption to gather information for its permanent rules.

OKXICE notified the SEC of its plans on Oct. 4, The Block previously reported. The venue will run permissioned liquidity pools on X Layer, a layer-2 network, with each stock paired against USDC, USDG or USDT. Traders must pass identity and sanctions screening and hold a non-transferable "soulbound" token to access the pools, according to the notice, which does not give a launch date.

## AMC separately criticized Robinhood's stock tokens

AMC Entertainment has separately publicly criticized Robinhood's stock tokens, which Robinhood describes as debt securities issued by an offshore affiliate and not offered in the States. AMC CEO Adam Aron called the tokens tied to AMC "contemptible, outrageous, disgusting, detestable, inexcusable, vile" in a Sept. 3 post on X, and said AMC had no connection to them.

Robinhood SVP Johann Kerbrat called AMC's criticism "mostly a marketing stunt" in an interview with The Block earlier this month. He said the exemption's caps on volume and asset types would limit Robinhood's ability to bring its stock tokens to the U.S.

Morgan Stanley expects to support tokenized equities in the first quarter of 2027, The Block reported earlier this week.

The value of tokenized stocks totaled about $3.5 billion as of Oct. 15, up from about $680 million at the start of the year, according to The Block’s data.

OKXICE and the nine companies did not immediately respond to The Block's requests for comment.

Citi and Bank of America restrict AI agents from accessing accounts
Sinziana Albu
19 Oct 2026 / 5 Min Read
Citigroup and Bank of America have joined JPMorgan Chase in limiting how autonomous AI tools interact with consumer bank accounts, according to the Financial Times . The restrictions come as US lenders tell regulators that AI agents could move idle deposits into higher-yielding products on customers’ behalf. Questions over liability for AI-authorised payments also remain unresolved.
Citi has stated that it does not allow third-party AI agents or assistants to log into customer accounts or carry out transactions directly within its digital banking platforms. The bank said the policy is designed to safeguard customer accounts and information. Customers can still grant limited, read-only access to their account information to Certain Open Banking providers, so data sharing remains possible while transactional access is closed.
Liability and human authorisation
In an interview with CNBC on 14 October 2026, Bank of America indicated that the bank is limiting AI agents’ access to consumer deposits. The restriction will stay in place while the bank develops protocols to establish which party is ultimately liable for payments authorised by AI. It was also mentioned that the bank believes a human should remain in the loop to approve money leaving an account, and it is working with agent providers to avoid cases where consumers cannot trace funds an agent has moved.
During the bank’s third-quarter 2026 results call, JPMorgan Chase said the bank would also block AI agents from accessing client accounts, citing the risk of ‘rogue agents’ that could number in the thousands. JPMorgan is building its own tools to protect customers and, until they are ready, will not allow outside agents to operate within its systems.
Deposit outflow concerns
The moves follow Meta’s launch of its Muse AI personal assistant in September 2026. The launch added to concerns that consumer-facing bots could shift large volumes of idle cash out of bank deposits in pursuit of better returns. AI companies are also introducing a growing number of tools aimed at personal finance management.
A report by Apollo’s chief economist warned that AI assistants could automatically sweep household cash into accounts paying between 3.3% and 5%. The national average rate on US checking accounts is 0.1%. JPMorgan, Bank of America, and Wells Fargo together hold USD 1.6 trillion in non-interest-bearing deposits, equal to 16% of their liabilities. Paying a 3% rate on those balances would cost the three banks USD 47 billion a year, close to half of their combined annual earnings. The economist argued that widespread use of such agents could erode the low-cost deposit base banks use to fund lending, which would affect the wider financial system.
Regulatory dimension
According to a person familiar with the discussions, Wall Street executives have contacted the Federal Reserve about the rapid rise in consumers using AI agents for financial transactions. The Fed declined to comment. Regulators have privately welcomed the banks’ restrictions. Furthermore, officials believe legal clarification may be needed on how customers authorise autonomous systems to transact on their behalf, and on who is liable when something goes wrong.
The restrictions put access control at the centre of the debate over agentic finance in the US. Banks are separating read-only data access from transactional permissions while liability frameworks remain undefined. A senior partner at consulting firm Simon-Kucher said banks will need to give customers reasons to keep savings with them, for example, expanded loyalty programmes linked to the borrowing rates on offer. In practice, that means making deposit products ‘stickier’.

# JPMorgan expands 'JPM Coin' deposit token to Ethereum for institutional clients

The token now supports peer-to-peer transfers within the Ethereum Virtual Machine ecosystem, including Base and Ethereum.

By Timmy Shen • Edited by Adam James

Business•October 19, 2026, 4:31AM EDT

#### Quick Take

*   JPMorgan announced Monday that it has brought its USD-denominated deposit token, JPM Coin, to Ethereum for institutional clients.
*   The token now supports peer-to-peer transfers within the Ethereum Virtual Machine ecosystem, including Base.

JPMorgan has brought its USD-denominated deposit token JPM Coin (JPMD) to Ethereum for institutional clients, as the bank continues its tokenization drive.

In a Monday statement shared with The Block, Kinexys by JPMorgan said Ethereum's "established depth of onchain liquidity and tokenized assets" makes it a natural next public blockchain for JPMD, which was first launched in November 2025 on Base.

JPMD serves as a representation of JPMorgan's existing bank deposits on a public blockchain, enabling institutional clients to make onchain native payments with enhanced speed and efficiency, according to its website.

The token now supports peer-to-peer transfers within the Ethereum Virtual Machine ecosystem, including Base and Ethereum.

Advertisement

"Tokenized financial infrastructure is no longer theoretical. It’s already restructuring liquidity, settlement, and digital asset workflows," Emma Landriault, global head of Kinexys Labs at Kinexys by J.P. Morgan, said in the statement.

RWA Market Cap by Blockchain (The Block)

The latest expansion builds on the lender's broader push to bring traditional instruments onchain. In December 2025, it launched its first tokenized money-market fund on Ethereum, followed by a second fund in May 2026.

The bank said Monday that the Kinexys platform has handled over $4 trillion in cumulative transactions to date, with an average daily transaction volume of $7 billion.

"We're delighted to see J.P. Morgan Payments bring JPM Coin to Ethereum. It builds on a clear trajectory: JPM Coin is already live on Base, J.P. Morgan Asset Management has tokenized money market funds on Ethereum, and the Kinexys platform itself is built on Ethereum Virtual Machine technology," said Matthew Dawson, co-founder of Ethereum Institutional.

"It's a clear signal that institutional finance is moving onto public, verifiable infrastructure," Dawson added.

 Digital asset lending vaults are investment vehicles that operate on a blockchain. Portfolio credit quality risk

Curator risk
Blockchain risk
Protocol risk
Vault security and governance risk

### [0] What this is
An analytical essay from **The Fintech Blueprint** (dated 2026-10-17) that threads together several contemporaneous events into one thesis: as finance moves onchain, the old TradFi guardrails of **custody, fiduciary duty and open architecture** are being reconstructed under new names — "curators," "innovation exemptions," "deposit tokens" — but the accountability layer has not caught up. The core argument is that a **fiduciary gap** is opening between the entities that now steer customer/investor capital onchain (vault curators, tokenized-securities venues, AI money-movement agents) and the parties legally answerable when things go wrong.

This is an essay, not a single corporate event — enrich the thesis across its threads. Separate the author's framing from the datable facts below.

### [2] Why it matters (the thesis)
The through-line: **TradFi spent a century building accountability around whoever directs other people's money — fiduciary duty, best-execution/NBBO, custody segregation, "know your counterparty."** Onchain, the directing role has reappeared (curators pick risk; TSVs set the only venue a token trades on; AI agents move cash) **before** the matching duty/liability has. Each thread is the same gap from a different angle: curator ≈ un-chartered fund manager (hence S&P's VRA trying to price "curator risk"); the Innovation Exemption creates a "shadow market" with no consolidated tape (hence issuer opt-outs); AI agents move money with no clear liable party (hence the bank blocks). The optimistic read (essay's own): onchain = radical open architecture, and institutions (S&P rating vaults, JPM issuing bank-money onchain) are the ones closing the gap.

### [3] Open questions / what to watch
- Does S&P's VRA (or a rival from Moody's/Fitch) actually publish ratings on named vaults, and do institutions gate allocations on them?
- Does the SEC convert the temporary Innovation Exemption into permanent rules with a consolidated-tape/NBBO-equivalent — or do issuer opt-outs keep tokenized equities a niche?
- Do regulators (Fed) set a liability standard for AI-agent-initiated payments, and does open banking carve out "read-only vs transact"?
- Does curated-vault TVL keep compounding, and does a curator blow-up (a "LUNA moment" for a vault) force the fiduciary question into court?

## Челлендж / ред-тим

<!-- enrichment:challenge -->
Red-team / skeptical review (essay = "The Fintech Blueprint," 2026-10-17). The piece synthesizes real events but leans on a tidy "fiduciary gap" frame; stress-test both the facts and the argument.

1. **The "$9B curators" figure is soft.** Independent trackers span ~$9B to ~$11B of curated TVL depending on definition (which protocols/vaults count). Is the essay's number a point-in-time snapshot already stale, and does it conflate total DeFi-lending TVL with specifically *curated*-vault TVL?
2. **Curator ≠ fiduciary (legally).** The essay's central analogy may be rhetorical: a vault curator typically has *no* fiduciary duty to depositors, often operates via disclaimers/DAO, and may be anonymous. Does calling them fiduciaries overstate the parallel — or is that precisely the "gap" the piece means?
3. **Does S&P's VRA actually close the gap?** VRA is explicitly *not* a credit rating, is silent on yield, and (as of launch) had published *no* assessments of named vaults. Is the essay treating a framework announcement as if it were operative risk pricing?
4. **Survivorship/incentive on curator data.** Curated-TVL leaderboards (Wallfacer, protocol dashboards) are partly marketing. Are the TVL/curator-share numbers audited, or self-reported and reflexive (TVL inflated by points/incentive farming)?
5. **Tokenized-stock scale is tiny.** Tokenized-stock market cap ~$3.5B vs ~$50T US equities. Is the "shadow market" / issuer-control thread a real systemic concern yet, or a governance precedent being set while stakes are trivial?
6. **Issuer objections ≠ rejection of tokenization.** Securitize's Redfearn notes issuers can object to one venue without rejecting tokenization; Nasdaq remains "committed to tokenization." Does the essay frame opt-outs as a referendum on tokenization when they're venue-specific (per-TSV, no blanket opt-out)?
7. **AI-agent blocks: deposit-defense vs customer-protection.** Banks cite liability/rogue agents, but Apollo's math (sweeping $1.6T idle deposits into 3–5% accounts could cost ~$47B/yr) suggests the real driver is protecting a cheap funding base. Is the "fiduciary" framing partly cover for deposit protectionism?
8. **Read-only vs transactional is already the settled line.** Banks still allow read-only open-banking access and only block money movement — so is the "banks blocking AI" thread less a novel gap than a straightforward liability-until-rules-exist posture?
9. **JPMD is permissioned bank money, not open DeFi.** JPMD only moves between approved institutional wallets. Does using it as evidence of "radical open architecture" contradict its closed, whitelisted design?
10. **rwa.xyz / aggregate figures drift.** The $331B stablecoin+RWA, $164B Ethereum, $14.7B tokenized-Treasuries numbers move daily and vary "distributed vs represented" (note the Canton $322B "represented" line that dwarfs distributed value). Are headline totals being quoted without the methodology caveat?
11. **False-equivalence across threads?** Curators, TSVs, and AI agents are very different actors under very different (or no) regimes. Does bundling them under one "fiduciary gap" obscure that only some involve a true agency relationship?
12. **Open-architecture analogy (Schwab 2006 → onchain).** Schwab's $305B→$4.9T third-party-fund growth happened *inside* a regulated custodian/fiduciary wrapper. Is the onchain analogy apt when the wrapper (custody + duty) is exactly what's missing?
13. **Who is actually liable on a curator blow-up?** The essay implies a gap but doesn't test it: in a vault loss, is recourse against the curator, the protocol, the frontend, or nobody? No litigated precedent is cited.
14. **Selection/recency bias.** Five separate October 2026 headlines are curated to fit one thesis. Would a neutral observer see a single "gap" narrative, or five loosely related regulatory/market events?
15. **Source quality on the curator claim.** Cryptobriefing / KOL-marketing links in the note's source list are low-authority for a load-bearing "$9B" stat; the robust anchors are S&P (VRA), The Block/CoinDesk (objections), CNBC/Axios (AI agents).

Importance: 4/5 — This is a genuinely strong synthesis piece from a respected analyst (Lex Sokolin's Fintech Blueprint) that names a real, recurring 2026 structural theme — the reconstruction of custody/fiduciary/best-execution guardrails onchain under new labels (curators, TSVs, agents) — anchored to several hard, same-week, primary-sourceable events (S&P VRA, the nine OKXICE objections, Citi/BofA/JPM agent blocks, JPMD on Ethereum). It reframes rather than breaks news, the headline curator figure is soft, and the unifying "gap" analogy is partly rhetorical, so it falls short of 5/5; but as cross-cutting context on where TradFi accountability and onchain finance are colliding it is well above routine.

## Market Research

**Sector & drivers.** The essay's spine is a regulatory/fiduciary gap opening between two fast-growing on-chain pools. (1) **DeFi lending/curated vaults:** total deposits ≈ **$10bn as of Sep-2026, up from $1.5bn Sep-2024** (per S&P Global Ratings VRA launch, Oct-4-2026) — a ~6.7x in two years; the curator-managed slice (Morpho/Euler/etc.) is larger on some counts — Morpho alone carried ~$11.3bn TVL as of Oct-21-2026 (all-time high $11.5bn Oct-6, per DefiLlama) and "curated vaults" across protocols are cited at **>$11bn** (DefiLlama/Sentora). (2) **Tokenized RWA:** the note's $331B figure conflates stablecoins + RWA; stripping stablecoins, tokenized RWA ex-stables is ~**$30–32bn (Jun–Jul 2026, ~3x YoY)**, of which **tokenized Treasuries ≈ $14.7bn** (note) are the dominant class — BlackRock BUIDL ~$2.2–2.8bn, Franklin Templeton ~$2.5bn. Tokenized *equities* are tiny and new: **~$3.5bn as of Oct-15-2026, up from ~$680m at the start of the year** (The Block data). **Structure:** both are concentrated. In vault curation the **top-3 curators ≈ 76% of TVL** (Steakhouse, Sentora, Gauntlet — per DefiLlama research); in tokenized Treasuries two issuers (BlackRock, Franklin) hold the bulk. **Why now:** the SEC's Sep-17-2026 *Innovation Exemption* lets Tokenized Securities Venues trade third-party-tokenized US stock with only a 30-day issuer opt-out and **no NBBO / 10-min-delayed tape** — a "shadow market" Nasdaq itself opted out of and 9 issuers (Broadcom, Verizon, UnitedHealth, CoreWeave…) objected to on OKXICE. In parallel, Citi/BofA/JPMorgan are **blocking third-party AI agents** from moving customer money while liability is undefined — the same "who is the fiduciary?" vacuum, on the banking side.

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs: **curated TVL / AUM, curator fee take (perf + mgmt), bad-debt/impairment events, net interest margin.** Curator economics: Morpho Vault V2 caps fees at **50% of yield (performance) + 5% of assets (management)**; in practice perf fees run 0–25% (Steakhouse top end) — so a curator on $2bn TVL earning, say, 10% of a few-hundred-bps spread is a real revenue line, but one with **no balance sheet and no custody**. Key players / recent moves: **Steakhouse Financial** (~$2.0–2.3bn curated TVL, #1), **Gauntlet** (~$1.46–1.9bn; closed a **$125m Series C from SBI Holdings**, Jun-2026 — see [[Gauntlet lands $125m Series C from SBI Holdings]]), **Sentora**, Chaos Labs, Block Analitica, Re7. Distribution is going mainstream: Coinbase and Robinhood earn products each passed **$500m** on curated vaults by Sep-2026; Stripe's Tempo wired in Morpho/Gauntlet ([[Stripe-backed Tempo taps DeFi lender Morpho to expand beyond payments]]). **Position / moat (analysis):** curators' moat is reputation + risk-modelling track record, not capital — a thin, reputational moat that resets with every incident; the S&P VRA (Oct-4-2026, [[S&P launches Vault Risk Assessment for digital assets]]) is the first attempt to externalize that trust into a rated scale, with **"Curator risk" named as one of six factors**.

**Risk flags.**
1. **Fiduciary vacuum (the thesis).** Curators make discretionary allocation calls that "closely resemble a regulated investment adviser" yet owe **no fiduciary duty** (unregistered; none of the majors licensed). SEC Commissioner Peirce (Jul-2026) warned discretionary vault curation may already trigger securities law. Second-order: if the SEC reclassifies curators as advisers, the no-custody / no-balance-sheet model that enables the land-grab becomes a compliance liability — re-pricing the whole stack.
2. **Curator-quality / contagion risk.** Bad debt from oracle misconfig and composability (Stream/xUSD 2025; PT-reUSD ~$36m cascade hitting ~15 Morpho vaults; KelpDAO ~$177–236m on Aave in 2026). Isolated-market designs (Morpho) contained some of this, but curator skill is **uneven and unrated until now** — the gap S&P's VRA is monetizing. A single curator blow-up can impair retail flows now arriving via Coinbase/Robinhood.
3. **"Shadow-market" transparency risk on the RWA side.** The Innovation Exemption's no-NBBO / 10-min-delayed-tape design means tokenized equities can dislocate from the real market; 9 issuers already objected. Investors could receive worse prices without a disclosed fiduciary standing behind the venue — the same gap as (1), transplanted to tokenized stock.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** (analysis) The essay's falsifiable thesis: **the next re-rating event in on-chain finance is a *fiduciary* one, not a yield one** — S&P's VRA (Oct-4) is the leading indicator that institutions won't scale into $10bn+ of curated vaults without an external trust layer, exactly because the curator owes no duty. **Watch:** (a) whether the SEC/Peirce follow-through classifies discretionary curators as advisers (catalyst that breaks the no-balance-sheet model); (b) first published VRA ratings and whether capital migrates toward highly-rated curators (consolidating the already top-heavy ~76% TVL). **What would make the thesis wrong:** institutional AUM keeps compounding with *no* regulatory reclassification and *no* new rating-driven flow concentration — i.e., the market stays comfortable with an unrated, non-fiduciary allocator holding billions.
