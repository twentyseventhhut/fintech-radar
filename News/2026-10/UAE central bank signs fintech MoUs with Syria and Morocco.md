UAE, Morocco central banks explore linking instant payment systems
The MoUs contribute to stronger bilateral cooperation and enhance the efficiency of cross-border financial transactions between the two brotherly countries
ABU DHABI: The Central Bank of the United Arab Emirates (CBUAE) and Bank Al-Maghrib (the Central Bank of the Kingdom of Morocco) have signed two Memoranda of Understanding (MoUs) to strengthen cooperation in banking supervision and Islamic finance, and interlink payment and financial messaging systems.
The MoUs contribute to stronger bilateral cooperation and enhance the efficiency of cross-border financial transactions between the two brotherly countries.
The MoUs were signed by Khaled Mohamed Balama, Governor of the CBUAE, and Abdellatif Jouahri, Governor of Bank Al-Maghrib, at the CBUAE headquarters in Abu Dhabi.
Under the first MoU, both parties will enhance cooperation on regulatory and supervisory issues and exchange supervisory information on banks and financial institutions. They will also coordinate on supervisory practices and regulations, exchange expertise and build institutional capacity, strengthen coordination among Shariah governance bodies for Islamic banking, and develop cross-border Shariah-compliant financing, including trade finance and infrastructure investment.
The second MoU aims to explore the possibility of interlinking instant payment platforms, national card switches and financial messaging systems in both countries to facilitate the processing and settlement of cross-border transactions and enable mutual acceptance of domestic payment cards, in line with the regulatory and supervisory requirements of both countries.
The MoU also covers the exchange of expertise in developing retail and wholesale central bank digital currencies (CBDCs), the exploration of ways to use them in cross-border payments between the two countries, and cooperation on fintech, regulatory and supervisory frameworks for virtual assets, including crypto-assets and stablecoins, and related consumer protection mechanisms.
Balama said, “The signing of the two MoUs with Bank Al-Maghrib reflects our commitment to expanding financial and banking cooperation with the brotherly Kingdom of Morocco, in line with the vision of the UAE’s wise leadership and in support of the mutual interests of both countries.
"Through this cooperation, we look forward to exchanging supervisory expertise, developing Islamic finance solutions, and exploring opportunities to strengthen links between payment systems, contributing to a more efficient and innovative financial sector and supporting economic and trade relations between the two countries.”
Jouahri said the two MoUs mark an important step in consolidating the partnership between Bank Al-Maghrib and the Central Bank of the UAE, and expanding coordination in supervision, regulation and Islamic finance.
"This partnership also opens up opportunities to develop cross-border financial transactions and accelerate their processing, and to explore ways to use central bank digital currencies in payments between the two countries, contributing to greater efficiency in financial transactions and stronger cooperation between the two institutions," he added.

# Context-enrichment: UAE central bank signs fintech MoUs with Syria and Morocco
_Analytical notes (not a post). Importance: 3/5._

> Date note: the item is dated 2026-10-06 (digest/retrieval), but the actual signings are **2 Oct 2026** (CBUAE–Central Bank of Syria) and **3 Oct 2026** (CBUAE–Bank Al-Maghrib, two MoUs), per The National / Arab News / Morocco World News.

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
Three MoUs across two counterparties, all signed at CBUAE HQ in Abu Dhabi, all **framework/intent-stage — not live rails**:
- **CBUAE ↔ Central Bank of Syria (2 Oct):** a broad financial-and-technical cooperation MoU. Scope is wide but soft: payment systems, monetary policy, cash management, fintech, capacity building, climate/sustainable finance, financial inclusion/literacy, credit-information & risk frameworks, and supervisory know-how (licensing, enforcement, compliance, consumer protection). Signed by Balama and Central Bank of Syria Governor Raslan.
- **CBUAE ↔ Bank Al-Maghrib (3 Oct), two MoUs:** (1) banking supervision + Islamic finance (supervisory info exchange, Shariah-governance coordination, cross-border Shariah-compliant trade/infra finance); (2) a payments/innovation MoU to **"explore the possibility"** of interlinking instant-payment platforms, national card switches and messaging systems, mutual acceptance of domestic cards, plus exchanging expertise on retail/wholesale CBDC and on virtual-asset (stablecoin/crypto) regulation. Signed by Balama and BAM Governor Jouahri.

**Why framed this way / what it reveals:** the operative verbs are "explore", "aims to", "exchange expertise" — classic MoU language. Techtimes is explicit that the Oct 3 deals "establish a regulatory framework... they do not create a live payment rail," with technical integration "typically taking years." The Syria MoU is even softer — pure capacity-building, no payments-linkage mechanism named (no Aani, no Buna, no SWIFT tie-in). So the de-PR'd reality: UAE is staking a central-bank-diplomacy flag in two very different markets, not shipping product. The real signal is **positioning**, not infrastructure.

## [1] Competitors / peers
- **Multilateral alternative already exists:** both Morocco and the UAE are participants in **Buna** (the Arab Monetary Fund's Abu Dhabi-based cross-border payment system, 100+ participant institutions, multi-currency incl. AED/SAR/EGP). The Morocco bilateral interlinking is therefore **partly duplicative on paper** — it is a parallel/alternative track to the multilateral rail both already belong to. The MoU coverage pointedly does **not** mention Buna.
- **CBDC interoperability track record is weak:** UAE is a full **mBridge** participant (first Digital Dirham on the MVP, Jan 2024), but BIS exited/handed off mBridge in Oct 2024, and a May 2026 Forbes piece argues multilateral CBDC interoperability ("mBridge and Agora") has effectively stalled. → the CBDC clause in the Morocco MoU is aspirational against a backdrop of multilateral CBDC projects underdelivering.
- **UAE's own live linkages set the comparison bar:** India UPI↔Aani (signed Feb 2024, still rolling out, not fully bidirectional-live); Egypt already receives instant remittances from UAE (live on Egypt side). Against these, Morocco is at the earliest "explore" stage — behind, not ahead.

**Why the landscape is this way:** the UAE is running two overlapping strategies at once — multilateral (Buna/mBridge) and a web of bilateral MoUs (India, Egypt, Saudi, now Morocco). Bilateral deals are faster to sign and photogenic but risk fragmenting into incompatible point-to-point links; the multilateral rail is slower but avoids N² integrations. Choosing bilateral-with-Morocco despite shared Buna membership suggests the multilateral rail isn't delivering the corridor economics the UAE wants.

## [2] Company/country fit
- **Syria:** sits on a genuinely historic reintegration arc — fall of Assad (Dec 2024) → US/EU sanctions lifted (2025) → first SWIFT transaction in ~14 years (Jun 2025) → first SWIFT message to the NY Fed (Nov 2025) → Syria opens accounts at CBUAE and Bank of Canada (Dec 2025) → this MoU (Oct 2026). The MoU is the logical next rung: capacity-building for a central bank rebuilding from near-zero. Fits cleanly.
- **Morocco:** large remittance market. ~800k Moroccan expats in the UAE; global Moroccan diaspora remittances hit ~MAD 122bn (~$13.4bn) in 2025, BAM forecasting ~MAD 128bn for 2026; typical remittance cost ~8% (the fee-reduction prize the MoU targets). Islamic-finance clause fits BAM's "participatory banking" build-out.

**Why the UAE acts this way:** UAE is the world's **3rd-largest remittance sender** (~AED 183bn / ~$50bn outflows in 2024). Its structural interest is to own the rails under its outbound remittance flows and to position Abu Dhabi/CBUAE as the convening hub for Arab + post-sanctions-market financial reconstruction — soft power plus corridor capture. Syria is a greenfield reconstruction option (early-mover optionality); Morocco is a volume corridor.

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
- **Novelty:** low on mechanism, meaningful on positioning. Nothing technically new is created. The Syria MoU's novelty is purely that it exists (a major Gulf central bank formally engaging a reintegrating Syria). The Morocco payments clause restates the standard UAE interlinking template (instant-payment + card-switch + messaging + CBDC + VASP).
- **Traction: zero so far** — no corridor live, no volumes, no go-live date. Compare the UAE's *own* live benchmarks: Aani 1.5M+ users domestically; UPI accepted at 60k+ UAE outlets. The Morocco/Syria deals have none of that yet.
- **Where margin/value sits:** if Aani↔GSIMT (Morocco's clearing system) ever goes live, the value-add is remittance-fee compression (8% → low single digits) and FX/settlement capture — but that accrues over years and depends on execution neither MoU commits to. Until then the "value" is optionality and diplomacy, not economics.

**Why the value-add is (not yet) real:** MoUs are cheap to sign and carry no binding timeline; the binding constraint is technical integration + legal/AML alignment across two jurisdictions (and, for Syria, correspondent-banking de-risking that sanctions legacy still complicates). The press framing of "UAE adds Morocco to its payment network" overstates — it is a feasibility commitment.

## [4] What's next / sentiment
- **Watch items:** (1) any Aani↔Morocco GSIMT go-live date or pilot — the real catalyst; (2) whether Syria's MoU converts into correspondent-banking/investment flows (the thing that actually matters for a reintegrating economy); (3) whether these bilaterals get routed through Buna after all. 
- **Risks:** execution drift (MoUs that never operationalize); duplication with Buna; CBDC clauses stranded by the broader stall in multilateral CBDC interoperability; Syria correspondent-banking de-risking.
- **Sentiment:** regionally positive/strategic; analysts treat the Morocco payments language as intent, not deployment. Second-order read: the proliferation of bilateral UAE MoUs is itself a signal that the **multilateral Arab rail (Buna) is underdelivering**, pushing the UAE to a hub-and-spoke bilateral model — which is faster but structurally more fragile and harder to scale than one shared rail.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** This sits in Arab/MENA cross-border payment integration — a central-bank-led push to replace correspondent-banking chains with directly linked instant-payment rails, card switches and messaging systems. The regional anchor is **Buna**, the multi-currency CBPS fully owned by the Arab Monetary Fund (launched Feb 2020, under ARPCSO): per The Asian Banker, Buna runs 6 currencies (USD, EUR, SAR, EGP, JOD, AED), ~110+ financial institutions, ~15,000 transactions/month at ~15% MoM growth (via theasianbanker.com, undated — treat as illustrative scale, not audited). The economic "why now" is remittance cost: per techtimes citing World Bank RPW Q1 2025, banks globally average ~14.55% total transfer cost — the most expensive channel tracked — and the UAE-Morocco effort explicitly targets ~8% fees for ~800,000 Moroccans in the UAE (via techtimes.com, 2026-10-05). Morocco received ~$12bn in total diaspora remittances in 2025, +4.3% YoY (per World Bank data, via multiple 2026 reports) — the UAE share is a subset, not the full figure. Structure: fragmented bilateral rails, value today captured by correspondent banks and remittance operators; barriers are regulatory (sanctions, AML/compliance parity) and network effects (card-scheme acceptance). (analysis)

The Syria leg is categorically different: it is early-stage capacity-building, not rails-linking. Syria only re-entered SWIFT in 2025 (first message to the NY Fed 2025-11-20) after the Caesar Act was repealed by US Congress in Dec 2025 (per Enab Baladi / AGBI / GFMag, 2025). The CBUAE-Syria MoU covers regulatory expertise, payment systems, monetary policy and compliance — foundational, with a banking sector flagged for "capital shortfalls, weak compliance frameworks, underinvestment."

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs for this layer: corridor remittance volume, all-in transfer cost (%), settlement time, and institution/currency coverage on the shared rail. Key actors are central banks and AMF-owned infrastructure, not fintechs: CBUAE's domestic instant rail is **Aani** (see [[UAE instant payments platform Aani tops 12.5 million users]], 2026-04 — 12.5m users), which press reports (techtimes) name as the UAE side of a potential Morocco link; Morocco's side is Bank Al-Maghrib's instant-transfer system. Basis of competition: not price between rivals but build-vs-join — bilateral links vs the multilateral Buna hub vs mBridge-style CBDC rails. CBUAE's position: a regional hub-builder, pursuing parallel tracks (domestic Aani, Buna membership via AED, mBridge CBDC pilot, and now bilateral MoUs). Moat is institutional/scale — it is the issuing authority and AMF member, not a contestable product. (analysis)

**Risk flags.**
1. **Announce-vs-live gap (both legs "explore").** The Morocco payment MoU uses "explore the possibility of interlinking" and CBDC "exchange of expertise"; the ECB-SNB comp shows such feasibility phases run a year+ with no guaranteed go-live. Second-order: headline implies integration that may be years from settlement, if ever.
2. **Syria sanctions/compliance risk.** Syria reconnected to SWIFT only in late 2025 and still has weak AML/compliance frameworks; any CBUAE rail-level exposure carries correspondent-banking de-risking and secondary-sanctions tail risk. Second-order: capacity-building is low-risk, but premature payment-system coupling could taint UAE institutions' correspondent relationships.
3. **Disintermediation of the bilateral model by multilateral/CBDC rails.** Bilateral card-switch/instant links compete with AMF's Buna and mBridge; a web of one-to-one links is operationally heavier than one hub. Second-order: today's MoUs could be superseded before delivery, stranding integration spend.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** For the sector this is incremental hub-building, not a step-change: the UAE is positioning Aani/Dirham rails as the connective tissue of Arab/North-African payments, de-PR'd it is two "explore" MoUs plus one capacity-building MoU. Falsifiable thesis: if an Aani-Morocco instant link or mutual card acceptance actually goes live within ~18 months, it validates bilateral rails over Buna as the near-term integration path; the trigger to watch is a dated pilot/go-live announcement (not another MoU). What breaks it: the corridor instead routes through Buna or a CBDC pilot, leaving these MoUs as diplomacy. (analysis)
