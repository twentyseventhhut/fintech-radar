Payrails launches Merchant of Record product built to be outgrown
Today Payrails is launching Payrails Merchant of Record (MoR), built to be outgrown.
Built by payments experts and running on Payrails orchestration, Payrails Merchant of Record lets companies use MoR in some markets and their own direct acquiring in others, from one platform, with full visibility into how payments perform. In each market a company assigns to MoR, Payrails becomes the legal seller. Payrails MoR calculates, collects and remits sales tax, VAT and GST, and handles invoicing, compliance, fraud prevention and disputes. If a company chooses to move a market to its own setup and bring these operations in-house, it can do so without re-integrating, keeping all the benefits of the Payrails platform.

 What it is: Payrails Merchant of Record acts as the legal seller for your digital products in the markets you choose.
 Who it's for: AI, SaaS , mobile app and digital goods companies selling internationally, including subscription apps and AI products billed by usage.
 What's our unique approach: A hybrid model: run MoR and your own merchant IDs side by side on one platform, and switch between orchestration and MoR as your business needs change 
Saved cards live in Payrails Token Vault , independent of any single provider, and move with you when you “graduate” a market from the MoR 
Merchants get full visibility into authorization rates, fraud prevention, chargebacks and other performance data via the Payrails portal

Why we built Payrails MoR
AI has changed how fast software companies go global. According to Stripe, the 100 largest AI companies on its platform now earn 48% of their revenue outside their home market. A product can have paying customers in dozens of countries in its first days, and each of those customers expects to pay in their own way: PayPal  in the US, Pix in Brazil, BLIK in Poland, UPI in India. Each sale also comes with a tax obligation: sales tax in California, VAT in Germany, GST in Australia.
A merchant of record solves that. It becomes the legal seller, so you can sell without needing an entity, tax registrations or a tax adviser in each market. The bigger challenge often comes later, once you have scaled. Many MoR products are not built for merchants seeking to optimize payments.
In a traditional MoR setup: 
Saved cards are held by the MoR provider 
Payments are often routed through a single acquirer
Merchants gain limited visibility into why payments fail 
By the time a few markets make up most of your revenue, you need to know how payments perform there: why they’re declined, where fraud comes from, which products attract disputes. In many MoR setups, that data is hard to see and harder to improve. Leaving can mean a new integration, a new checkout and asking every returning customer for their card again. The product that got you into a market becomes the thing keeping you from taking it to the next level.
Payrails MoR starts from the other end: we assume you'll outgrow it, one market at a time.

Merchant of record questions, answered
 What is a merchant of record? 
A merchant of record (MoR) is the legal entity that sells a product to the end customer. It appears as the seller on the receipt and the card statement. It is responsible for:
collecting and remitting sales tax, VAT and GST
issuing compliant invoices
meeting local consumer and payments regulations
handling fraud prevention and chargebacks
Companies use a merchant of record to sell in markets where they don't have their own legal entity, or where they'd rather not take on local tax and compliance themselves.
 What's the difference between a merchant of record and a payment service provider (PSP)? 
A PSP processes payments on your behalf, but you remain the legal seller, so the tax registrations, invoicing and compliance in each market are yours. A merchant of record becomes the legal seller: it resells your product to the buyer and takes those obligations on. Many companies use both: a PSP where they have an entity, and an MoR where they don't.
 What's the difference between a merchant of record and a payment facilitator? 
A payment facilitator lets businesses accept payments under its master merchant account, but each business is still the seller and still owns its tax and compliance obligations. A merchant of record is the seller, so those obligations sit with the MoR.
 When does a company need a merchant of record? 
Usually when it sells digital products to buyers in countries where it has no entity and no tax registration. Many jurisdictions tax digital sales where the buyer is located:
The EU charges VAT on digital services based on the buyer's country.
US states apply sales tax once a seller passes economic nexus thresholds, often $100,000 in annual sales.
A merchant of record meets those obligations so you don't have to register in each place.
 Why do AI and SaaS companies use merchants of record? 
AI and SaaS products are global from launch, while legal entities and tax teams take months to set up. An MoR closes that gap. It's especially common for:
AI products billed by usage or subscription
Mobile and subscription apps selling outside the app stores
Digital goods such as games and online courses
 How much does a merchant of record cost? 
MoRs typically charge a percentage of each transaction plus a fixed fee, often around 5% plus a fixed per-transaction fee. It's higher than standard payment processing because the fee also covers tax compliance, regulatory work and transaction risk. For a new market, it's often cheaper than building that capability in-house. The gap narrows as the market's volume grows.
 What are the downsides of using a merchant of record? 
The main things to weigh are:
Cost at scale
Less control over checkout and payment performance
Lock-in: depending on the provider, saved cards and customer history may stay with the MoR, payments may run through a single acquiring setup, and performance data may be limited
Reserves: some MoR players might have extensive holding period for your payouts
Before signing, ask how you'd leave.
 When should you move from a merchant of record to your own entity? 
Consider moving a market to your own entity when:
the MoR fee on that market's volume costs more than running tax and compliance yourself
you want direct acquiring relationships and negotiated rates there
you have expertise to manage payment operations effectively and efficiently 
buyers in that market need to contract with you directly
 What happens to saved cards and subscriptions when you leave a merchant of record? 
That depends on where the stored payment credentials live. If they stay with the provider, leaving means asking returning customers and subscribers to enter their card details again, and some won't. With Payrails MoR, saved credentials live in Payrails Token Vault, independent of any single payment provider, so they move with you when you graduate.
 Can you use a merchant of record in some countries and your own entity in others? 
With Payrails, yes. You can run Payrails MoR in the markets you choose, and your own merchant IDs and payment providers everywhere else. It's all under one account with the same API and SDK.

Can I see authorization rates, decline reasons, fraud and chargeback data?
How many acquirers do you route through, and can a failed payment be retried elsewhere?
What happens to my saved cards if I leave?
Can I use the MoR in some markets and my own entity in others?
Are there reserves or payout holds, and on what terms?
Does my brand appear on the checkout, invoices and card statement?
What are the costs?
What does it cost to move a market to my own entity?

# Context-enrichment: Payrails launches Merchant of Record product
_Analytical notes (not a post). Importance: 3/5._

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
On 2026-10-07/08 Payrails (Berlin, enterprise payment orchestration) launched **Payrails Merchant of Record (MoR)** — live from day one. Payrails becomes the **legal seller** in markets a company assigns to it: it calculates/collects/remits sales tax, VAT and GST (including filings), issues co-branded invoices, and takes on fraud, chargebacks and consumer/payments compliance. Target customers: AI, SaaS, mobile-app and digital-goods sellers going global (usage-billed AI, subscription apps).

The one genuinely differentiated claim is the **"hybrid / built to be outgrown"** model: run MoR in some markets and your own merchant IDs (direct acquiring) in others, from one account/API, and "graduate" a market to your own setup "without re-integrating." Saved cards live in **Payrails Token Vault**, provider-independent, so they move with you — the explicit pitch against MoR lock-in ("spend years trying to get back out," CEO Orkhan Abdullayev).

**Why framed this way / what it reveals:** Payrails is a payments-orchestration vendor, not a tax-software vendor — so it anchors the pitch on *payment performance* (auth rates, retries, multi-processor routing across 100+ integrations) and *portability*, precisely the two axes where incumbent MoRs (single acquirer, opaque data, sticky saved cards) are weakest. That is a real competitive wedge, but also an admission: MoR is a feature bolted onto its existing orchestration stack, sold to customers who "asked for it" (COO Emre Talay) — i.e. demand-pulled, not a new capability moat. **No pricing was disclosed** (industry norm ~5% + fixed fee, per trade context, not a Payrails figure) and **no launch customers, GMV or market count were named** — the usual silences for a day-one launch.

## [1] Competitors / peers
MoR for digital goods is a crowded, consolidating space:
- **Paddle** — the SaaS-MoR incumbent; Series D $200M at ~$1.4B (KKR, 2022-05). Already pushing the AI-builder angle: [[Paddle powers Lovable Payments with embedded merchant-of-record integration]] (2026-04) embeds MoR into an AI dev workflow — direct thematic overlap with Payrails' AI/SaaS targeting.
- **Stripe** — acquired **Lemon Squeezy** (2024-07) and shipped **Stripe Managed Payments** (GA ~2026-04, 195 markets). The 800-lb gorilla; Payrails even *integrates* Stripe as a processor, so it competes on the MoR layer while depending on Stripe below it.
- Others: FastSpring, Digital River, 2Checkout/Verifone, Lemon Squeezy, newer entrants (Polar, Dodo).

**Position:** Payrails is a **late entrant** to MoR-the-category but enters from a defensible side door — it already runs enterprise orchestration for scaled merchants (Preply, DeepL, Eneba, Flix), so its MoR is "payments-first." **Why the land lies this way:** classic MoRs started as tax/compliance software and treat payments as plumbing; Payrails inverts that. Second-order: the differentiation (visibility + no lock-in) only matters to customers *large enough to eventually leave* an MoR — which narrows the TAM to upper-mid-market/enterprise, exactly where Payrails already sells, and cedes the self-serve long tail to Paddle/Stripe/Lemon Squeezy.

## [2] Company history / fit
Payrails: founded 2021, emerged from stealth 2022-03 with $6.4M seed (a16z, HV Capital); ex-Delivery Hero fintech team (Abdullayev, Talay, Thouzeau). Seed extension ~$14.4M (2023, labeling open). **Series A $32M** led by HV Capital's Growth Fund with EQT Ventures, General Catalyst, a16z (2025-06), ~$52.8M total — see [[Payrails raises $32M Series A for payment performance]]. Distribution partnerships: [[Santander's Getnet partners with Payrails on payments]] (2025-09) and [[Ecommpay partners with Payrails for global enterprise payments]] (2026-02).

**Why Payrails acts this way (structural logic):** pure orchestration is a thin, commoditizing take-rate business competing with Gr4vy, Solidgate Routing 2.0, CellPoint, ACI. MoR is a **margin-up, stickier adjacency** — it bundles tax/compliance/liability (worth 5%-ish vs orchestration bps) and deepens lock-in with the customer relationship. Launching MoR ~16 months after a $32M Series A is a textbook "use the round to expand into a higher-margin line" move. The "built to be outgrown" honesty is itself a sales tactic aimed at enterprises burned by Paddle/Stripe stickiness.

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
**Genuinely new:** the *hybrid, per-market graduation* with provider-independent Token Vault is a real mechanism delta — no other major MoR openly markets "we assume you'll leave, and we won't hold your saved cards hostage." That addresses the single biggest, well-documented MoR complaint (switching cost, lost returning subscribers).

**But traction is zero so far:** this is an announcement, not adoption. No named MoR customers, no volumes, no migration case studies — the "graduation without re-integrating" claim is **untested marketing**. Independent pickup is thin (The Paypers restated the release; no confirmed PYMNTS/Finextra/TechCrunch/Sifted piece).

**Who captures the margin (analysis):** the MoR fee covers tax engines (likely third-party), acquiring (Payrails routes to Stripe/Adyen/etc.) and compliance. Payrails' durable edge is the *orchestration + portability* layer, not the tax plumbing, which is buyable. If the hybrid model works, it reframes the category question from "which MoR" to "can a payments-orchestrator out-position tax-software MoRs on performance and exit-optionality" — but only if real customers actually graduate markets as advertised.

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
Backdrop: AI/SaaS go global from launch (Payrails cites Stripe's stat that the top AI companies now earn ~48% of revenue outside the home market — verifiable via Stripe Sessions 2026, with the precise "top 100" denominator loosely sourced). MoR demand is structurally rising, and 2024-26 has been a land-grab (Stripe buying Lemon Squeezy, Paddle+Lovable, Managed Payments GA). Payrails is riding a real wave.

**Why the market goes this way + second-order:** as MoR becomes table-stakes, differentiation shifts from "do you handle tax" to "payment performance + exit-optionality" — Payrails' exact bet. **Counterintuitive risk:** the "built to be outgrown" promise is self-cannibalizing — a successful customer eventually migrates its biggest markets *off* MoR to Payrails' lower-margin orchestration, so the MoR revenue is partly a lead-gen funnel into cheaper orchestration, not a durable high-margin annuity. Also, Stripe owning the acquiring layer Payrails routes through is a disintermediation overhang. Risks: unproven at scale, no disclosed economics, crowded field, dependence on underlying PSPs.

3. **Self-cannibalizing / low-stickiness by design.** The headline pitch is "built to be outgrown" + portable Token Vault + reintegration-free exit. Honest positioning, but it structurally caps MoR take-rate duration and LTV — the better it works, the faster customers graduate to direct acquiring (where Payrails earns the thinner orchestration fee, not the ~5% MoR fee).

**What this changes (idea-lens).** (analysis) This is a new entry into a consolidating sub-market (Stripe+Lemon Squeezy, Paddle+Lovable), repositioning MoR from a lock-in product to an "optional, exitable" one — a differentiator that pressures incumbent MoR take rates if it resonates. Falsifiable thesis: if the hybrid/portable model matters to buyers, Payrails should name AI/SaaS MoR logos and disclose MoR volume within 2-3 quarters; catalyst/trigger to watch = first disclosed MoR customer count or a response (price cut / portability feature) from Paddle/Stripe. Thesis is wrong if MoR stays an unquantified add-on for existing orchestration clients with no standalone MoR logos by mid-2027.
