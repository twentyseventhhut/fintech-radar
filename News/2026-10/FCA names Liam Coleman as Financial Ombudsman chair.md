The FCA Board has appointed Liam Coleman as chair of the Financial Ombudsman Service, with the approval of the Treasury.
Liam will take up the permanent role on 10 October 2026 for a 3-year term, having served as interim chair for the last year.
Ashley Alder, chair of the FCA, commented: 'Liam has brought strong leadership and valuable insight to the Financial Ombudsman Service over the past year. His extensive experience across financial services, regulation and public service organisations will be invaluable as we work together to support a redress system that is fair, predictable and effective for consumers and firms.'
Commenting on the appointment, Mr Coleman said: 'I am honoured to take on the permanent role as chair of the Financial Ombudsman Service. It plays a vital role in upholding fairness and trust in the financial system. I am pleased to be able to continue to work with the rest of the Board, the executive team, and our wider stakeholders.'
Liam is also group chair of London & Quadrant Housing Trust. He has extensive financial services and public-sector experience, including previous roles at the FCA, NHS Foundation Trust, Nationwide Building Society, Royal Bank of Scotland and Cooperative Bank.
Notes to editors
The FCA is required by the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (FSMA) to appoint the chair of the Financial Ombudsman Service, with the appointment being approved by the Treasury. 
The Board of the Financial Ombudsman Service is responsible for ensuring that the service is properly resourced and able to carry out its work effectively and independently.
The Financial Ombudsman Service was set up to resolve complaints between financial businesses and their customers. It can help with most financial services – including banking, insurance, loans, mortgages, pensions and investments. From 1 April 2019, its remit was extended to cover complaints from larger small and medium sized businesses and about claims management companies.
The FCA and the Financial Ombudsman Service are working with the government to transform the UK’s redress system and the way in which the Ombudsman operates within it to deliver a service that is consistent, predictable and fair for both firms and consumers, now and in the future.

## Контекст

<!-- enrichment:context -->
**What happened.** On 10 October 2026 the FCA Board, with Treasury approval, confirmed **Liam Coleman** as the permanent chair of the Financial Ombudsman Service (FOS) for a 3-year term. Coleman had served as interim chair for the prior year ([[FCA appoints Liam Coleman as interim Financial Ombudsman chair]], 2025-07), a post he took up on 10 October 2025 after succeeding Baroness Zahida Manzoor and after the initial permanent-recruitment campaign failed. The permanent appointment therefore formalises a year of acting leadership — a continuity signal rather than a change of direction. FCA chair Ashley Alder framed it around delivering "a redress system that is fair, predictable and effective." Coleman is a 30-year financial-services veteran (ex-CEO Co-operative Bank; ex-treasury roles at RBS and Nationwide), a former FCA non-executive director, and group chair of London & Quadrant Housing Trust.

**Why it matters — the redress-reform backdrop.** The FOS appointment lands in the middle of the most consequential overhaul of the UK consumer-redress system in years. Drivers:

- **Motor-finance commission crisis.** The August 2025 UK Supreme Court judgment (on discretionary / undisclosed commissions under the Consumer Credit Act 1974 and the "unfair relationship" test) opened the door to an industry-wide FCA redress scheme. The scheme (PS26/3, consultation confirmed 2026) covers regulated agreements from 6 April 2007 to 1 November 2024; the FCA now expects ~12.1m eligible agreements, ~£7.5bn of compensation and ~£9.1bn total sector cost (trimmed down from an earlier ~£11bn estimate). The FOS is planning to resolve ~245,000 cases in 2026/27, ~60,000 of them motor-finance commission. A legal challenge to the scheme is set for February 2027.
- **Governance turmoil at FOS.** Former chief executive **Abby Thomas** (CEO from Oct 2022) resigned in 2025 amid criticism of the FOS's role in the motor-finance saga, shortly after Chancellor Rachel Reeves called for reform and ahead of a Treasury Committee appearance. Coleman's steadying hand covers a period without a settled permanent CEO.
- **Structural reform.** In March 2026 HM Treasury published its consultation response and the FCA+FOS a joint consultation on reform: recalibrating the "fair and reasonable" test, a **10-year absolute time limit** for complaints, a formal FOS↔FCA referral mechanism, case-fee changes to deter speculative claims-management-company volume, and criteria for "mass redress events." The stated goal is to stop the FOS from effectively setting policy / creating liabilities retrospectively — a key industry grievance.

**Read-across.** The FOS sits at the centre of how far-reaching redress events (motor finance, and the earlier template set by PPI) ultimately cost UK lenders and how predictable that liability is. Fintech and bank exposure runs through consumer-lending, BNPL and complaints handling — cf. the FCA's parallel conduct agenda under Consumer Duty ([[FCA confirms BNPL protections under Consumer Duty from July 2026]]). A permanent, FCA-aligned chair signals the reform programme proceeds on its current, firm-friendlier trajectory.

**Red-team questions**

1. Is this genuinely new, or just the permanent confirmation of a role Coleman already held for a year? (Mostly the latter — continuity, not change. Low novelty.)
2. Does the chair of FOS actually set redress policy, or is it a non-executive governance role? (Board is entirely non-executive; it ensures resourcing/independence — it does not adjudicate cases or set FCA rules.)
3. Is the title accurate? FCA "names" him, but FSMA requires Treasury approval — the FCA Board appoints and Treasury approves. Title is fine.
4. What's the real news hook — the person, or the FOS reform he's overseeing? (The reform; the appointment is a hook into motor finance / redress overhaul.)
5. Why did the first permanent-recruitment campaign fail in 2025? Does that signal the role is seen as a poisoned chalice amid the motor-finance crisis? (Plausible but unconfirmed — worth flagging, not asserting.)
6. Is there still no permanent CEO at FOS after Abby Thomas's exit? Who runs day-to-day? (CEO succession is the more material governance question than the chair.)
7. Does Coleman's FCA-insider background (ex-FCA NED) compromise FOS independence from the regulator, given the new formal FOS↔FCA referral mechanism? (Legitimate independence tension — the FOS is meant to be operationally independent.)
8. Are the motor-finance numbers I cited current? (£9.1bn total cost / £7.5bn compensation / 12.1m agreements are the trimmed figures; earlier ~£11bn is superseded — I should not mix versions.)
9. Is the Feb 2027 legal challenge to the redress scheme a risk this appointment speaks to? (Indirectly — Coleman leads the body that resolves cases outside/alongside the scheme.)
10. Does this appointment move any share price or create any quantifiable fintech/bank impact today? (No — it's governance; impact is second-order via the reform agenda.)
11. Could the body-text "redress system… fair, predictable and effective" language be mistaken for a new policy? It's standard boilerplate reused from the interim release.
12. Is there a conflict-of-interest angle — Coleman chairs L&Q Housing Trust and (per 2025) NHS trusts simultaneously? (Multiple chairships; capacity question but common for NEDs.)
13. Have we already covered this? Only the 2025-07 interim appointment exists; no same-day or same-month duplicate. This is a distinct follow-up event.
14. Does the audience (fintech) care about a UK ombudsman chair? (Marginally — matters to UK consumer-lending/BNPL firms exposed to complaints and mass-redress; niche for global fintech.)
15. Am I over-stating the significance by loading the motor-finance context onto a routine leadership notice? (Yes, risk present — the context is the story; the appointment itself is minor.)

**Importance: 2/5** — A routine, continuity governance confirmation (interim → permanent) of a non-executive chair. Zero direct market impact and limited novelty on its own. It earns a 2 (not a 1) only because it is a hook into the materially important FOS/motor-finance redress reform (£9.1bn sector cost, 10-year complaint limit, FOS↔FCA overhaul, post-Abby-Thomas governance) — context that genuinely matters to UK lenders and BNPL/consumer-credit fintechs. As a standalone leadership item, it is low importance; be honest about that in any post.

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** UK statutory-redress plumbing, not a market with a TAM — but the numbers moving through it are large enough to matter for firm P&L. The FOS is mid-way through a once-in-a-generation reform: FCA+FOS+HMT jointly consulted on "modernising the redress system" (CP25/22 July 2025 → joint follow-up CP26/9 on 16 Mar 2026 → FOS policy statement 11 Aug 2026), with the government confirming intent to legislate in seven areas (per FCA/HMT, via A&O Shearman / regulationtomorrow, Mar 2026). Headline reforms: a 10-year absolute time limit on bringing complaints (FCA may carve out long-dated products e.g. pensions); a new "mass redress event" definition tied to earlier FCA s.404-style intervention powers; and a reframed "fair and reasonable" test anchoring FOS to the law/rules as they stood at the time of the act — explicitly narrowing the ability to apply hindsight standards (per FCA CP26/9, via A&O Shearman / globalfinregblog, Mar 2026). Running in parallel: the £9.1bn motor-finance consumer redress scheme confirmed 30 Mar 2026 (PS26/3) — ~£7.5bn compensation + ~£1.6bn admin, ~12.1m eligible agreements (Apr 2007–Nov 2024), nearly all resolved by end-2027, ~£800–830 average payout (per FCA, via Osborne Clarke / 9fin, Mar 2026). Why now: motor finance is the live stress-test of a system everyone agrees was overwhelmed by professional-representative-driven volume; the chair is being made permanent precisely at the point reform moves from consultation to implementation.

**Competitive landscape.** Not competitive — this is the apex arbiter sitting between the FCA (rule-maker) and ~50k+ regulated firms. "KPIs" of the FOS itself: case volume, case-fee economics, and clearance rate. Volumes fell from ~306k (2024/25) to ~210k (2025/26), guided to ~199k (2026/27) as motor-finance-commission and professional-rep cases drop off; FOS plans to resolve 266,500 cases in 2026/27 (per FOS Plans & Budget 2026/27). Case-fee reform after two years held flat: respondent firms £680/case in 2026/27, professional representatives newly charged (£80 if consumer wins / £260 if firm wins; firm fee drops to £500 in those rep-brought cases), compulsory levy £86m from 1 Apr (per FOS, via ITIJ, 2026). Coleman's profile fits the moment — ex-Co-op Bank CEO, ex-RBS deputy group treasurer, ex-Nationwide group treasury director, plus NHS/housing-trust public-sector chairing — i.e. a balance-sheet-and-governance operator for an institution whose central problem is funding, resourcing and predictability rather than consumer advocacy.

**Comps & multiples.** No valuation — leadership/governance item, so trading multiples are "no data" by nature. Internal comps are the relevant signal:
- [[FCA appoints Liam Coleman as interim Financial Ombudsman chair]] (2025-07) — same person, interim; permanent appointment is continuity, not a change of direction. Note the admission that the *initial permanent recruitment campaign failed* — a scarcity/credibility signal worth keeping in mind.
- [[FCA confirms BNPL protections under Consumer Duty from July 2026]] / [[FCA sets out BNPL oversight proposals]] — FOS remit expands as BNPL enters the regulatory perimeter → new complaint inflow channel.
- Fraud-redress pressure feeding FOS caseload: [[Monzo wrongly denied refunds to thousands of fraud victims]], [[Revolut tops UK bank fraud complaints for third year]], [[TSB reports surge in higher-value courier fraud cases]] — the demand side of the FOS workload the reform is trying to make "consistent, predictable and fair."

**Risk flags.**
1. **Retrospective-standards narrowing (firms win, consumers lose scope).** The reformed "fair and reasonable" test + 10-year absolute limit shift risk off firms and onto consumers for old/long-dated products; second-order — fewer open-ended legacy liabilities to provision (helpful for lender capital planning), but reputational/political backlash risk if a future mass-harm event falls outside the window. A permanent chair locks in execution of this trajectory.
2. **Case-fee economics stress the model.** Held flat two years, now rising on thin reserves and reform costs; FOS is funding a major transformation while volumes fall — a scissors between declining per-case revenue base and fixed reform spend. If volumes undershoot further, the levy/case-fee balance gets renegotiated again, re-pricing firms' complaint-handling cost.
3. **Motor-finance execution overhang.** £9.1bn scheme must clear ~12.1m agreements by end-2027 largely through firms' own schemes, with FOS as backstop/appeal layer; any slippage dumps volume straight back onto the FOS just as it is cutting headcount to a lower-volume steady state — a capacity-whipsaw risk.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** (analysis) Read-through for firms: the center of gravity of UK redress is tilting toward *finality and predictability* — bounded tails, narrower hindsight, earlier FCA intervention on systemic harm. For lenders and fee-paying firms that is structurally positive for provisioning certainty; for claims-management companies and professional reps it compresses the economics (new adverse fees). Falsifiable thesis: if the Aug-2026 FOS policy statement + legislation deliver the 10-year cap and the law-at-the-time test substantially intact, expect a step-down in firms' legacy-redress provisioning language in 2026/27 results. What breaks it: a fresh mass-redress event (next candidate after motor finance) that forces the FCA back to broad s.404 schemes, re-opening the tail the reform was meant to close.
