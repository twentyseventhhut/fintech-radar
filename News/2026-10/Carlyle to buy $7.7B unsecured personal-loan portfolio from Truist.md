_Transaction reflects Carlyle’s growing role as a capital partner to banks managing their balance sheets_

NEW YORK, NY – October 16, 2026 – Global investment firm Carlyle (NASDAQ: CG) today announced that its Global Credit platform has entered into a definitive agreement to acquire an approximately $7.7 billion portfolio of unsecured personal loans from Truist. The portfolio consists primarily of fixed-rate, fully amortizing loans to super-prime and prime borrowers and represents a seasoned pool of consumer loans.

“Banks are increasingly turning to private capital to help manage their balance sheets, and this transaction reflects the scale and specialized underwriting capabilities that Carlyle’s asset-backed finance platform brings to those relationships,” said Akhil Bansal, Partner and Head of Asset-Backed Finance at Carlyle. “It underscores our ability to commit capital and execute at scale, and our conviction in high-quality consumer credit as a core part of the asset-backed finance opportunity.”

The transaction builds on Carlyle’s experience working with banks on large and complex portfolio transactions, while further strengthening its longstanding relationship with Truist.

This investment is being led by Carlyle’s Asset-Backed Finance (“ABF”) team within the firm’s Global Credit platform. Carlyle ABF invests across private fixed income and asset-backed assets, providing financing solutions to and acquiring portfolios of financial assets from businesses, specialty finance companies, and asset owners. As of June 30, 2026, Carlyle’s Global Credit platform had approximately $211 billion in assets under management.

Sidley Austin LLP acted as legal advisor to Carlyle on the transaction.

The transaction is expected to close in the fourth quarter of 2026, subject to customary closing conditions.

# Context-enrichment: Carlyle to buy $7.7B unsecured personal-loan portfolio from Truist
_Analytical notes (not a post). Importance: 4/5._

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
On Oct 16, 2026 Carlyle's Global Credit platform signed a definitive agreement to buy an ~$7.7B portfolio of unsecured personal loans from Truist, led by Carlyle's Asset-Backed Finance (ABF) team. The press release frames it blandly as "a seasoned pool of consumer loans to super-prime and prime borrowers" — but it de-PR's to something sharper: **this is substantially all of LightStream, Truist's unsecured online lending platform (acquired 2012), being wound down.** LightStream stopped originating new loans in October 2026 (confirmed by Credible/NerdWallet reviews); existing borrowers keep paying. So this is not a simple portfolio trade — it's the **exit of an entire consumer product line.**

The economics the PR omits but Truist's Q3 disclosures reveal: the sale yields ~$7.2B in net proceeds and creates ~$850M (≈20 bps) of CET1 capital, and cuts net charge-offs by ~7 bps/yr. The ~$500M gap between $7.7B face and $7.2B proceeds is the discount + fees — i.e. Carlyle is **buying at a few points below par**, consistent with a seasoned amortizing book (not distressed, but not par either). Expected to close Q4 2026; Sidley Austin advised Carlyle.

**Why structured this way:** Truist's new CEO Mike Lyons is running a "balance-sheet reset" — LightStream is a loan-only, no-deposit, sub-target-return business that doesn't fit relationship banking. Selling it books a ~20bps capital gain AND de-risks the charge-off line, while Carlyle (which doesn't need deposits or a branch network) can hold prime amortizing paper in a vehicle whose cost of capital is fit-for-purpose. The deal is co-timed with Truist's Q3 print (and a $1.45B securities loss + the $5.5B Regional Acceptance Corp sale) — a coordinated capital/earnings cleanup, not a one-off.

## [1] Competitors / peers (bank-asset-to-private-credit buyers)
This is one data point in a **structural migration of consumer credit from bank balance sheets to private credit**:
- **Blackstone** — reportedly buying HSBC's ~A$26-30B (~US$17-20B) Australian loan book; the scale leader.
- **Sixth Street** — up to $4B of consumer loans from Affirm.
- **Blue Owl** — forward-flow to buy up to $5B personal loans from SoFi, + $2.4B from Pagaya. Cf. internal [[Octane closes $1.4B forward flow deal with institutional investors]], [[Cross River extends $250M forward-flow for Figure crypto loans]], [[YouLend secures $225 million forward-flow facility from Värde Partners]].
- **Carlyle itself** — cf. [[Citigroup and Carlyle partner to finance fintech firms]] (2025, a forward-looking lending JV) and [[Private credit firms hunt for credit card debt as banks pull back]] (2026-05, the thesis in one headline).
- Context on direction of travel: [[JPMorgan to put up to 15% of credit ETF into private credit]], [[Demand surges for private-credit secondaries]].

**Position:** Carlyle is a credible #2-tier buyer behind Apollo/Blackstone/KKR in ABF scale, but this is a marquee US consumer deal. **Why the land lies this way (2nd order):** banks sell because Basel III endgame + consumer-credit stress make unsecured consumer paper capital-expensive on a deposit-funded sheet; private credit buys because perpetual/insurance capital (the "Big 5" now run ~$1.5T of perpetual capital) is desperate for yielding assets and doesn't face the same capital charge. The margin the bank gives up (the ~20bps capital relief it values more than the loan yield) is exactly the margin the private-credit vehicle harvests. So the win isn't underwriting skill — it's **regulatory/funding arbitrage.**

## [2] Company history / fit
Truist (BB&T + SunTrust, merged 2019) acquired LightStream via the 2012 SunTrust purchase of the former FirstAgain. Under Lyons, Truist is explicitly exiting non-core, loan-only businesses: the RAC indirect-auto book ($5.5B, generated ~$1B CET1) was sold earlier in 2026 and now LightStream. Cf. the relationship history: [[Plaid and Truist announce open banking data-access agreement]], [[Truist launches open banking experience with Mastercard]], [[Zelle and Truist pilot bill payments over Zelle network]], [[Truist launches Merchant Engage SMB payments platform]], [[Truist plans 100 new branches in high-growth US markets]] — all point to a strategy of **doubling down on relationship/deposit/payments franchise and shedding standalone balance-sheet lending.**

For Carlyle, this extends a run of ABF/credit expansion: [[Carlyle acquires 74% stake in Edelweiss Nido Home Finance for $232 million]], [[Carlyle acquires Intelliflo to expand wealthtech portfolio]]. **Why Carlyle acts this way:** its Global Credit AUM is ~$211B of $485B total; growing fee-earning ABF AUM via large bank-portfolio deals is the clearest path to the recurring, perpetual-capital fee multiple the market rewards over lumpy PE carry.

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
Not a new mechanism — bank-to-private-credit portfolio sales are a well-worn 2024-26 trade (global sales >$300B since 2022). **What's genuinely notable:** (1) the size for a *single US unsecured consumer* book ($7.7B is top-tier for this asset class, vs forward-flow deals that cap at $2-5B); (2) it's an **outright legacy-book sale + platform wind-down**, not a forward-flow to fund new originations — so it's balance-sheet surgery, not growth financing; (3) real, quantified bank benefit (20bps CET1, -7bps NCO) rather than a vague "partnership."

**Traction / who captures the margin (2nd order):** the durable value for Carlyle is fee-earning AUM + the net spread on prime amortizing paper financed at a credit-fund cost of capital. The risk: this is *seasoned* consumer unsecured paper bought into a late-cycle consumer-credit-stress environment (the very stress that pushed banks out). If prime-consumer charge-offs rise, Carlyle wears losses the bank just offloaded. So the margin Carlyle captures = the capital relief the bank paid away, **minus** the credit risk the bank was trying to shed — a fine line. Mark (analysis): the ~$500M discount is thin cushion if the macro turns.

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
Deal closes Q4 2026 (customary conditions). Expect Truist to keep selling non-core books toward its 15-18% ROTCE 2027 target; expect Carlyle and peers to keep hoovering bank consumer portfolios. Regulatory backdrop: Basel endgame + bank supervisors' preference to see risk move off insured-deposit balance sheets *encourages* this; but regulators and the IMF are simultaneously warning on private-credit opacity (cf. the "private credit meltdown" narrative circulating in H1 2026). **Counterintuitive 2nd-order:** moving consumer credit from transparent, stress-tested banks into lightly-disclosed private vehicles doesn't eliminate the risk — it relocates it to holders (often insurers/pensions via perpetual capital) with less mark-to-market discipline. Capital "relief" at the bank = risk concentration elsewhere. The systemic question isn't "is Truist safer" (yes, marginally) but "where does the next consumer-credit air-pocket surface, and who's holding it."

## Market Research

<!-- enrichment:market_research -->
**Sector & drivers.** This is a bank-to-private-credit balance-sheet trade: a bank offloads a seasoned consumer-loan book to a private-credit buyer. Not technically an SRT (the loans leave Truist's balance sheet entirely, a true sale), but it is adjacent — same driver, banks shedding capital-intensive consumer assets to free regulatory capital. Scale of the shift: global bank loan-portfolio sales have exceeded $300bn since 2022, driven by regulatory capital requirements and margin pressure (per Alternatives Watch / Credit Crunch, as of Oct 2026). The five largest private-credit managers (Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, Carlyle, KKR) run a combined ~$1.5tn in perpetual/permanent capital, ~40% of their combined AUM, projected toward ~$5tn by decade-end if growth holds (per Benzinga/analyst roundups, 2026). Why now: Basel endgame-era capital rules + post-rate-cycle margin pressure make sub-target consumer books (Truist says LightStream "generated returns below corporate targets") cheaper to sell than to hold; private credit, flush with insurance-backed permanent capital, is the natural buyer. Second-order effect: consumer credit risk is migrating off regulated, deposit-funded, Fed-supervised balance sheets into lightly-regulated, insurance-funded vehicles.

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs for ABF/consumer portfolio deals: purchase price vs par (discount), portfolio yield/WAC, credit quality mix (super-prime/prime share), seasoning, and the buyer's cost of (permanent) capital. Key buyers competing for bank consumer books: Blackstone (reportedly acquiring HSBC's ~A$30bn Australian loan book; forward-flow deals), Blue Owl (forward-flow to buy up to $5bn personal loans from SoFi, $2.4bn from Pagaya), Ares (JV with Goal Investment Mgmt for consumer installment/solar/home-improvement loans), Apollo, KKR — basis of competition is cost of capital, underwriting/servicing capability and speed of execution at scale, not price alone. Carlyle's position: catching up to Apollo/Blackstone in ABF but scaling fast — Global Credit AUM ~$211bn as of Jun-30-2026 (up ~5% YoY), and a new closed-end ABF strategy that topped $12bn at first close, up >30% YoY (per Carlyle Q1/Q2 2026 results). Moat (analysis): specialized ABF underwriting + a repeat relationship with Truist (this "builds on" prior deals) — a distribution/relationship moat more than a structural one.

**Comps & multiples.** This is a loan-book purchase, not an equity round, so trading multiples don't apply; the relevant figure is price-vs-par. Carlyle pays for a ~$7.7bn gross portfolio; Truist expects ~$7.2bn net proceeds and ~+20bps CET1 at close (per Truist Q3 2026 earnings call / 8-K, Oct-16-2026). The ~$0.5bn gap (≈ $7.7bn − $7.2bn = ~6.5%) blends transaction costs, servicing/retained economics and any discount to par — so the implied purchase discount is ≤~6.5%, consistent with a seasoned super-prime/prime fixed-rate amortizing pool (small discount = high credit quality). Clean discount-to-par not separately disclosed → `[UNSOURCED]`. Internal comps (bank books sold / risk transferred to private capital):
- [[DBS completes $1 billion synthetic securitisation deal]] — $1bn corporate-loan SRT, loans retained, capital freed (true SRT, contrast to this true sale).
- [[Klarna plans new BNPL risk transfer to fuel US growth]] — ~$516m BNPL SRT to free capital for expansion.
- [[Mox Bank buys $1bn loan portfolio from Standard Chartered]] — intra-bank portfolio sale, $1bn.
- [[Demand surges for private-credit secondaries]] — PC secondaries volume ~$20bn in 2025 (Evercore), context for appetite.
At ~$7.7bn, this is roughly 8x the DBS/Mox tickets — one of the larger single consumer-book trades in-base; in-line with the $300bn+ cumulative bank-disposal wave rather than an outlier.

**Risk flags.**
1. Risk migration / systemic-opacity: consumer credit is moving from a Fed-supervised, deposit-funded balance sheet to an insurance-funded private vehicle. The FSB (May 2026), IMF (Apr 2026 GFSR) and Fitch have all flagged private credit as a vulnerability — Fitch's PC default rate hit 5.8% in Jan 2026 (a record), and ~$20bn of redemption requests hit PC funds in Q1 2026. Even a super-prime book is now priced into a stressed-sentiment sector; a downturn tests valuations in vehicles with less loss-absorbing transparency. Why it matters: mispriced/inflated private ratings (IMF: ~7,000 private securities rated by specialized agencies in 2023 vs ~2,000 in 2019) could mask losses until they crystallize.
2. Adverse-selection / "why is Truist selling": Truist is exiting LightStream because returns are below corporate targets. A seasoned pool sold by a motivated seller invites the question of whether the retained vs sold vintages differ in quality — Carlyle is buying the economics a bank judged sub-par to hold. (analysis)
3. Consumer-credit cycle timing: fixed-rate, fully-amortizing unsecured consumer loans carry no rate upside and full duration exposure to a US consumer that is late-cycle; prime/super-prime cushions this but doesn't remove it. Economics (yield, expected loss) undisclosed by either side — "who's silent" flag.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** This is consolidation of the bank-disposal trade, not a re-rating: Carlyle joins Apollo/Blackstone/Blue Owl/Ares in institutionalizing banks-as-originators / private-credit-as-holders for US consumer loans. Falsifiable thesis (analysis): if US consumer delinquencies stay benign through 2027, these forward-flow/portfolio deals become a durable ~$300bn+/yr channel and regional/super-regional banks keep shedding non-core consumer books for CET1. Trigger/what breaks it: a consumer-credit deterioration (rising prime delinquencies) or a regulatory move to pull insurance-funded consumer ABF under tighter capital/ratings scrutiny would freeze the bid and widen discounts — watch Fitch's PC default rate and insurers' NAIC treatment of ABF. (analysis)
