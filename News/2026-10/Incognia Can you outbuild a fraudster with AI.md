You can’t out-build a fraudster with AI 
Subscribe to The Signal: FinServ

The biggest fraud problem I’m seeing right now is how quickly AI is making attacks easier to build, test, and adapt.
AI tools can help surface vulnerabilities, understand how fraud controls work, and develop tampering techniques faster than before.
We’re seeing this across our customers, especially around app integrity, spoofing, and tampering.
I’ve been talking about this for a while.
A few months ago, I shared that we had starting seeing a surge in app integrity-related issues not too long after AI coding tools went mainstream.
At the time, I said we couldn’t attribute it of it directly to AI for sure. But the timing was hard to ignore.
Since then, I’ve become even more convinced that this is one of the defining fraud challenges in the industry right now.
We’re having to update our own defenses more frequently.
And we’re starting to see public examples that show exactly how AI can be used to reverse engineer fraud technology.
A recent Reddit post shows what this looks like in practice
A recent Reddit post is one of the clearest examples I’ve seen of this happening in practice:
 
The author used AI to reverse engineer the SDK behind a leading fraud prevention solution and understand the signals it was collecting inside the browser and how they’re used to identify users and assess risk.
This is exactly the kind of activity I’ve been talking about.
AI is getting very good at analyzing software, reverse engineering how it works, and identifying vulnerabilities much faster than would have been possible manually.
That’s why I think code obfuscation, and security by obscurity more broadly, are going to become much less effective as defensive strategies.
Obfuscation can still make reverse engineering harder. But if AI keeps getting better at peeling that complexity back, you have to assume attackers will eventually understand how the control works.
And once they do, they have a much better starting point for identifying where it can be tampered with and developing ways around it.
And the barrier to doing this is getting lower.
You no longer need the same amount of time or specialized expertise to reverse engineer complex software entirely by hand.
The part that really stands out came at the end of this post:
 I expect there to be a lot more of this.
AI is accelerating both sides of the fight, and it’s asymmetric
AI coding tools are also making internal engineering teams more productive.
Earlier this year, we started hearing from companies that wanted to build more fraud prevention capabilities in-house because AI had made software faster and cheaper to develop.
I understand the logic.
If your engineers can build something materially faster, building internally starts to look a lot more attractive.
But fraudsters have the same tools.
And the fight is asymmetric.
A financial institution can’t push a new fraud control into production the moment an engineer finishes building it. It has to be tested and reviewed. You have to understand how it will affect legitimate customers, and there are security and regulatory requirements to work through.
Fraudsters don’t have those same constraints.
They can experiment until something works. If an approach fails, they move on to the next one.
That’s why I think AI is creating a bigger advantage for the attacker than it is for the defender.
This changes the economics of build vs. buy
Historically, a lot of the build-vs.-buy decision has focused on the initial build.
Can our team build this capability? How quickly can we ship it? What will it cost to get version one into production?
AI makes those questions easier to answer in favor of building.
But the bigger cost is what happens after launch.
If fraudsters are finding new ways to tamper with controls more quickly, those controls also need to be researched, hardened, tested, and updated more often.
That maintenance burden can become much more expensive over time.
And I think that’s where some internally built fraud prevention capabilities can start to become economically unfeasible, even if AI made them cheaper to build in the first place.
For us, that maintenance burden is part of the job
Investing in fraud technology does not make the arms race disappear.
It changes who is responsible for staying in it.
For us, researching new tampering and spoofing techniques and updating the technology in response is part of the job.
I don’t think our customers should have to become experts in every new attack technique just to keep a fraud control useful.
That’s part of the ongoing engineering burden that we, as vendors, should absorb. As attack methods change, our technology has to change with them.
That’s how I think about build vs. buy in this environment.
The question is becoming less about whether your team can build something, and more about whether you want to own that adversarial engineering problem indefinitely.
Decide which arms race you want to own
AI is making fraud technology easier to build.
But it’s also making fraud controls easier to study, test, and attack.
That changes what matters in a build-vs.-buy decision.
Fraud prevention is adversarial. Whatever you build will eventually be studied and adapted against, and AI is making that cycle much faster.
So the real question is whether you want to own the ongoing cost of keeping up with it.
Some capabilities are worth owning internally.
For the rest, I would want the company I buy from to treat the next attack as its problem too.
If your team is seeing more app integrity issues, reply and let me know what’s changed. I’m interested in comparing notes on how quickly these attacks are evolving.
André Ferraz

## [0] What exactly happened (de-PR'd)
Incognia CEO/co-founder André Ferraz published an opinion/newsletter editorial, "You can't out-build a fraudster with AI." No new product, money, or disclosed data — it is a sales-aligned thesis. The claim chain:
1. AI (coding + analysis tools) makes fraud attacks faster/cheaper to build, test, reverse-engineer, adapt.
2. The fight is **asymmetric**: defenders at regulated FIs must test, security-review, assess impact on good users, and clear compliance before shipping a control; attackers just iterate until something works. → AI therefore helps the attacker more than the defender (analysis — plausible but asserted, not quantified).
3. This changes **build-vs-buy**: AI lowers the *initial* build cost (so "build in-house" looks tempting), but the *post-launch* maintenance/hardening treadmill is where cost explodes and where in-house controls "become economically unfeasible." → so buy, and pick a vendor who "owns the adversarial-engineering problem indefinitely" (i.e., Incognia).
4. Evidentiary props: (a) a **redacted Reddit screenshot** of someone allegedly using AI to reverse-engineer "a leading fraud-prevention SDK's" browser signals; (b) the assertion that **code obfuscation / "security by obscurity" will become much less effective**.

**Why framed this way (the tell):** every step routes to the company's business model. Incognia sells a device/location-signal SDK; its moat is *data (unfakeable location behavior)*, not *code secrecy*. So "obscurity is dead" costs Incognia nothing to concede while it damages competitors whose defense leans on obfuscation, and "the maintenance burden kills in-house build" is a direct "buy, don't build — buy us" pitch. The one general-security truism it leans on (obscurity ≠ security, i.e. Kerckhoffs's principle) is genuinely mainstream and century-old — which lends the self-serving conclusion borrowed credibility.

## [1] Competitors / peers (fraud-prevention landscape)
The build-vs-buy argument is the real competitive frame: Incognia vs an FI's own engineers, AND Incognia vs other vendors.
- **Closest vendor peers (device/signal layer, like Incognia):** Fingerprint(JS), SEON, Castle, Arkose Labs, HUMAN Security; Sardine's device/behavior product. BioCatch is behavioral biometrics — the camp Ferraz implicitly knocks elsewhere ([[Incognia on proving humanity without an eyeball scanner]] sits in this device-vs-biometrics debate).
- **Adjacent-but-different (not direct comps):** transaction/ML scoring (Sift, Feedzai), identity-network decisioning (Forter), chargeback-guarantee (Riskified, Signifyd), identity verification (Socure).
- **Market money/scale context:** Socure raised $156M at a **$5.2B** valuation (Aug 2026), $364M ARR; Sardine pushing "foundation models for fraud" (Oct 2026); Fingerprint shipped an open-source MCP server for device intelligence (Mar 2026) — directly in Incognia's lane, mirroring Incognia's own MCP move in [[Incognia launches AI Agent Detection for financial institutions]]. Fraud-detection market sized ~$36–41B (2026) → ~$80B by 2030 at ~17% CAGR (vendor-funded research; Incognia's device/location slice is far smaller).
- **Position:** Incognia is a ~$46.5M-raised, ~240-person vendor punching via thought-leadership. On the specific "AI arms race / obscurity is dead" argument it is a fast follower of a broad 2025 security-industry theme (e.g. Security Boulevard's Nov-2025 "AI can crack your fraud prevention in hours" — which argues the *opposite* remedy: VM-based obfuscation hardening). So the argument is not novel to Incognia; the vendor-specific spin (buy us, we absorb the treadmill) is.

**Why the landscape is this way (second order):** AI commoditizes both the *attack* and the *in-house build* simultaneously. That squeezes the mid-market vendor from both sides — if AI makes building cheap, why buy? Incognia's counter is to relocate the value from "code you build once" to "an adversarial-engineering service we run forever." That is a smart reframing precisely because it moves the moat off a thing AI erodes (code) onto a thing AI doesn't (a funded team continuously re-hardening + a proprietary location-signal dataset). Whether that moat holds depends on data/scale, not rhetoric.

## [2] Company history / fit
- Pivoted out of **In Loco** (location ad-tech, Recife, Brazil, ~2011; COVID killed location advertising) → redirected the location tech to fraud → **Incognia** (~2020). HQ now San Jose/Palo Alto; engineering roots Brazil.
- Funding: **$46.5M total** — Series A $15.5M (Point72 Ventures, Jun 2022), Series B $31M led by **Bessemer** (Prosus, FJ Labs, Point72, Valor), closed Jan 2024. **Valuation never disclosed.** ~239–246 staff (Mar 2026). Claims profitability on <half of Series B.
- Product: mobile-first SDK = device fingerprinting + **precise location behavioral biometrics** (GPS/Wi-Fi/Bluetooth/motion → per-user "location fingerprint"), tamper/emulator/app-clone + remote-access-tool detection. 2026 product cadence: AI Rule Builder ([[Incognia launches AI Rule Builder for fraud detection]]), Browser ID, Verified IP Location, Location 2FA, and AI Agent Detection + MCP server.
- **Why Incognia makes this argument now:** Series B is >2.5 years stale (Jan 2024), revenue reportedly tripled (~200% YoY, est. ~$42M, 170% NRR — all self-reported, unaudited; see [[Incognia report only 16% of firms catch mule-account fraud early]] and the Jan-2026 traction PR), and peers are raising at multi-billion valuations. The editorial cadence reads as **pre-Series-C narrative-building** — establish Ferraz as the fraud-AI thought leader, frame the market so "buy a specialist" is the obvious conclusion, then raise. Structural pressure: a mid-size device-signal vendor must justify a software multiple in a market where AI threatens to commoditize the build — so it sells *continuity of defense* (a service), not a tool.

## [3] Novelty / value-add / traction
- **Novelty of the ARGUMENT: low-to-moderate.** The macro premise (AI lowers attacker cost; defenders are bottlenecked by review/compliance) is directionally corroborated by multiple (mostly vendor/survey) sources — deepfake/identity-fraud indices projecting large 2026 increases, ATO up ~21% YoY. But "obscurity is dead" is textbook security doctrine, and the "AI cracks fraud SDKs in hours" line predates this piece. What is genuinely sharp is the **build-vs-buy reframing**: historically that decision anchored on *initial* build cost; Ferraz correctly moves it to *lifetime maintenance under adversarial pressure*. That is a real, non-trivial analytical point (analysis).
- **Traction / evidence — thin and self-serving.** The central anecdote (Reddit user AI-reverse-engineering "a leading fraud SDK") is a **redacted screenshot, no vendor named, no date, no independent corroboration** — unverifiable as cited. The "companies want to build in-house because AI made software cheap" claim is "we started hearing from companies" — no data, no sample. No numbers in the piece at all.
- **Where the value-add / margin really sits (why ladder):** If the moat is "we absorb the adversarial-engineering treadmill," the value is real only if (a) Incognia's continuous re-hardening is genuinely faster/cheaper at scale than an FI's, and (b) its *data* (billion-device location graph) is the thing attackers can't cheaply replicate with AI. Point (b) is the actual defensible moat — a location/behavior dataset is not reverse-engineerable from an SDK the way code logic is. So the piece's strongest (unstated) argument is: when AI erodes code-secrecy, value migrates to whoever owns the hardest-to-fake *signal* and the funded team maintaining it. That shifts the central question from "is AI good or bad for defenders?" to **"who owns an un-AI-replicable data moat + the capital to run the treadmill forever?"** — which favors scaled vendors (and, awkwardly for Incognia, the $5B+ peers more than a $46M-raised one).

## [4] What's next / market sentiment
- Expect continued newsletter cadence on the same themes and a likely **Series C** push (stale round + reported revenue growth + peers raising big). Continued US/Europe expansion ([[Incognia becomes most downloaded fraud prevention SDK in Europe]], GDPR data-minimization angle) and agentic-AI defense ([[Incognia launches AI Agent Detection for financial institutions]]).
- Sentiment: the "AI arms race in fraud" is a crowded, real 2025–26 theme — nearly every fraud vendor is publishing a version. Risk for Incognia: the argument is becoming commodity thought-leadership; differentiation must come from *data scale*, not essays.
- **Counterintuitive second-order:** if "AI makes in-house build cheap" is true, the near-term threat to a vendor like Incognia is NOT fraudsters — it is well-resourced FIs/big-tech building device-integrity in-house with AI. Ferraz's "maintenance burden" argument is partly a *defense of the vendor category itself* against AI-enabled disintermediation, dressed as advice. That is the real stakes: AI could commoditize mid-tier fraud vendors faster than it empowers fraudsters.

**Sector & drivers.** Fraud detection & prevention (FDP) is a large, fast-growing market: per The Business Research Company (via researchandmarkets/tbrc listings), ~$60.75bn in 2025 → ~$73.62bn in 2026 at ~21% CAGR; MarketsandMarkets/Mordor cite ~17–20% CAGRs to 2031 — directionally consistent, mid-to-high teens growth (no single authoritative TAM; figures are secondary analyst reports, treat as order-of-magnitude). The device/behavioral-biometrics + location-intelligence sub-vertical Incognia sits in is a fragment of this. Structure: fragmented, with value splitting across the stack — signal collectors (device fingerprint, behavioral biometrics, location), decisioning/orchestration (rules + ML), and case management. Entry barriers are the proprietary signal graph (device/location history at scale) and the *adversarial maintenance* cadence, not the initial build. "Why now": generative-AI coding tools have lowered the cost of both building fraud controls in-house AND reverse-engineering vendor controls — the piece's own thesis, corroborated by sector reporting that AI-driven attacks scaled sharply in 2025–26 (per Pindrop/Appdome/SecurityBrief 2026 commentary) and that SDK-based static logic is increasingly reverse-engineerable (per Appdome, 2026). **Risk flags.**
1. **Thesis is vendor marketing (conflict of interest).** The "you can't out-build a fraudster, so buy from us" argument is authored by the CEO whose product is the "buy" option — the build-vs-buy framing is self-serving; the maintenance-burden claim is asserted, not quantified. Second-order: discount the conviction, watch for disclosed detection metrics instead.
2. **Incognia's own stack is SDK/signal-based — subject to the same reverse-engineering it warns about.** The Reddit example (AI reverse-engineering a fraud SDK's browser signals) is the attack surface Incognia also exposes; "obfuscation is dead" cuts both ways. Second-order: the moat degrades to pure patch-cadence speed, an operating-cost race with no durable technical lock-in.
3. **Commoditization / disintermediation by AI coding tools.** If AI makes in-house fraud controls materially cheaper to build (the note concedes this), the TAM for point vendors can compress at the low end even as the high end (regulated banks needing owned adversarial engineering) stays sticky. Second-order: pressure on SMB/mid-market pricing; vendors must move up-market or into orchestration.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** `(analysis)` The AI arms race reframes FDP from a "better detection model" contest to an *adversarial-maintenance-as-a-service* contest — advantage accrues to vendors who can patch faster than attackers can probe, favoring consolidation under distribution-rich owners (cf. Visa/BioCatch) and squeezing standalone niche players like Incognia toward M&A or a platform/orchestration pivot (the Oct-2026 AI Agent Detection + MCP Server launch reads as exactly that pivot). Falsifiable thesis: if AI keeps lowering reverse-engineering cost, expect (a) more vendor consolidation and (b) a shift in vendor messaging from "accuracy" to "time-to-patch SLAs" through 2026–27. Wrong if a durable detection moat (proprietary cross-customer signal network) proves un-replicable by in-house teams — watch disclosed NRR / detection-rate benchmarks for evidence.
