Sidney Powell 
 CEO and Co-Founder 
On March 9, 2023, $42B left Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) in the space of a 10-hour business day.
Depositors didn’t wait in an orderly queue outside a branch like they might have done in the 1930s, limited to whatever cash was physically in the vault and whatever hours the doors were open. Instead, SVB depositors opened an app, anxiously jabbed their screens a few times, and moved their money before anyone else could. The bank opened on March 9 and was closed by regulators the next morning.
The point is how quickly it all unraveled, and how fast depositors pulled their money. For most of the 20th century, a bank account was really the only viable choice. It was the only place technology and regulation let ordinary people park spare cash, and FDIC insurance made putting it there feel safe enough to stop the runs that had become common.
None of those three conditions holds the same way anymore. Most importantly, the technology constraint is gone. Once money can move as fast as it did out of SVB, the question stops being where deposits are safest and starts being where they can best be put to use.
It’s a significant market, too. The American Bankers Association itself, citing an April 2025 Treasury estimate, puts $6.6 trillion in transactional deposits as the pool theoretically exposed to this checking account exodus.
 If not checking accounts, then where? 
I think the answer, for most people, moves away from the bank account, and mostly away from checking accounts specifically. Most people leave spare cash sitting in a checking account paying a fraction of a percent, not because it's the best place for it, but because of a lack of convenient alternatives. That’s the shift I believe we’re going to see in the coming years, and it’ll trigger a retail banking exodus to tokenized credit products.
Bank accounts will still hold a small operating balance, but most will be put to work somewhere with an actual mandate attached: A defined term, a defined yield, a defined level of risk. It’s not a new concept, it’s what corporate treasurers have done for decades, holding idle cash in T-bills instead of a checking account. Someone chooses a 6-month position because they don't need that money for 6 months, and it earns more there than it does sitting idle. But retail customers don’t have a private, savvy treasurer managing their balances.
Imagine if they did.
 Advent of the agents 
That's what I believe is coming next, except the treasurer isn't a person on a salary, it's an agent that never sleeps and charges a fraction of a management fee.
Instead of a person logging into a banking app once a week to check a balance, an agent holds the keys and moves funds continuously between products with explicit mandates: A 6-month position here because that portion of savings won't be touched for 6 months, a shorter-duration slice there for money that might be needed sooner. This will all be wrapped up in a nice fintech UX while a far more exciting back end, hidden from the customer, takes off.
Robo-advisors? I hear you ask. Robo-advisors have been doing mandate-driven, continuous rebalancing for years. It’s the rails underneath that have changed. A bank account requires a bespoke integration and a human in the loop for anything above a small threshold, which means an agent moving between five providers needs five separate permissioned relationships, each running on its own business hours.
Onchain infrastructure is one standard rail that any agent can transact on the same way, regardless of which product it's moving into, and it doesn't stop for a weekend. A spending limit or an approved counterparty can be a rule the wallet enforces itself. Anyone can check every position, every move, every balance in real time.
 Fintechs are already shifting onchain 
A retail customer doesn’t need to understand the onchain plumbing underneath, and the products already live are starting to prove that. Robinhood Earn puts Maple credit underwriting behind an app 30 million people already use. Bitso, with more than 9M users across Mexico, Argentina, Brazil, and Colombia, added stablecoin yield next to its core banking features. Littio built a Colombian neobank around the same idea. Plasma One launched in the UK doing the same thing from the ground up rather than bolting it onto an existing product. None are selling "onchain." They're selling a rate and a term instead of a flat balance that never moves.
The strongest signal we’re seeing a shift is that all of these companies spotted the same opportunity and arrived at the same structure, in different markets, serving different customers, independently of each other. It means the shape of the product isn't one company's bet, it's what the category converges on once the constraint that used to force everyone into a checking account stops applying.
On one side, onchain credit providers like Maple have spent years building the tokenized credit products that generate this kind of yield. On the other side, fintechs have built the distribution to put it in front of retail customers. Between them, supply has been sitting ready, waiting for retail demand to catch up.
 Banks already see this coming, and they're not being quiet about it 
Banks aren't waiting to see whether deposits actually move out of checking accounts and into products like these before reacting. A New York Fed staff paper published in February found that banks holding stablecoin-related deposits end up operating more narrowly, sitting on larger reserves and lending a smaller share of what they hold.
Fed researchers have separately modeled a scenario where a stablecoin-driven deposit shift could pull $1T out of the banking system if that liquidity doesn't recycle back through it. The Fed hasn't seen that scale of outflow yet, but in my opinion, this is the regulatory establishment independently confirming the direction this is heading, before the scale has caught up with it.
The economics explain why banks should be worried. Most currently pay close to nothing on deposits, somewhere around 10 basis points, and keep the rest of the spread for themselves. No one competed on that rate, because a checking account wasn’t really a product with terms, it was just the default.
That only works as long as customers have nowhere else to put idle cash. Once a real alternative exists, banks have two options, and both cost them. Raise what they pay to hold onto deposits, and the spread compresses. Hold the rate where it is, and the deposits leave anyway. No version of this leaves bank earnings intact.
 Not risk-free, just held differently 
The honest objection is that none of this makes risk disappear, it just moves it somewhere else. Community banks are the most exposed piece of this story. The reserves behind stablecoins concentrate in a handful of large banks rather than spreading out the way retail deposits do today, so a smaller bank could lose deposits to this shift without gaining anything back.
The people building agent wallets aren't pretending otherwise. Current guidance across that ecosystem is that agent wallets are built for operating funds rather than core savings. Self-custody or qualified custody remains the recommended route for the bulk of what someone holds.
What an agent actually needs from a product like Maple is a set of clear rules it can work within:
Defined strategies an agent can choose between
A structure simple enough for an agent to read and act on
Positions and balances checkable in real time
A real choice on the mix itself, trading duration and risk for a better rate, rather than accepting whatever a bank happened to offer that week
 Over the next five years: Banks will narrow 
None of this ends with banks gone. It ends with them performing a smaller, more specific job. Banking needs to evolve toward tokenized credit products: A defined term, a defined yield, a defined level of risk. Instead of one flat rate on a checking account, your agent picks a position that matches what you actually need, 6 months here because that money isn't needed sooner, 12 months there because it earns more and you can afford to wait.
That's going to be the natural evolution of banking. And it just happens to be getting built onchain.
 Sidney Powell 
 CEO and Co-Founder 
 Sidney Powell is the co-founder and CEO at Maple. Sid serves as the company's voice in onchain asset management, yield, and institutional lending.

### [0] What this is

A **commentary/thesis essay** by **Sidney ("Sid") Powell, co-founder and CEO of Maple Finance**, an on-chain asset-management / tokenized-credit protocol (issuer of yield tokens like syrupUSDC / syrupUSDG). The core argument: because the three historical reasons retail cash sat in bank checking accounts — technology constraint, lack of alternatives, FDIC-backed safety — no longer bind, depositors will increasingly move idle cash out of ~10bp checking accounts into tokenized credit products with "a defined term, a defined yield, a defined level of risk," orchestrated by AI agent wallets. Banks don't disappear; they "narrow." It is a **vendor-authored** piece: Maple is on the supply side of exactly the products the essay predicts will win, so read it as a directional bull case, not neutral research.

### [1] Core factual claims — fact-check

- **"$42B left SVB in a 10-hour business day (March 9, 2023)"** — CORRECT. This is the widely cited figure for the SVB run that preceded its March 10, 2023 closure; it is the canonical modern example of a digital-speed bank run.
- **"ABA, citing an April 2025 Treasury estimate, puts $6.6T in transactional deposits as exposed"** — ACCURATE and well-sourced. The figure traces to the **Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee (TBAC) "Digital Money" presentation of April 30, 2025**, which flagged ~$6.6T of US commercial-bank *transactional (non-interest-bearing) deposits* as the pool theoretically "at risk" of migrating to stablecoins over time. Note: this is an upper-bound pool, not a prediction. (BofA's Brian Moynihan has separately cited a "~$6T" figure.)
- **"Banks pay ~10 basis points on deposits"** — directionally fair for non-interest / basic checking balances, though it cherry-picks: high-yield savings and money-market accounts pay far more; the 10bp figure applies to the sticky transactional float banks most want to defend.
- **"A New York Fed staff paper published in February [2026] found banks holding stablecoin-related deposits operate more narrowly — larger reserves, lending a smaller share"** — ACCURATE. This is **NY Fed Staff Report No. 1185, "Stablecoin Disintermediation" (Lee & Tou, Feb 2026)**, which documents (via on-chain data linked to Fedwire) that partner banks saw ~147% higher reserves-to-assets, ~67% higher interbank payments, 38–55% greater intraday reserve volatility, and a ~14 percentage-point drop in loans-to-assets after stablecoin partnerships. A companion paper, **SR 1179 "Stablecoins vs. Tokenized Deposits: The Narrow Banking Debate Revisited" (Huang & Keister, rev. Feb 2026)**, frames the whole debate as a modern replay of the 1930s narrow-banking question.
- **"Fed researchers modeled a scenario where a stablecoin-driven shift could pull $1T out of the banking system if liquidity doesn't recycle"** — plausible and consistent with Fed research, though the essay states the key caveat *as* the headline risk. The crucial qualifier (which Powell does acknowledge) is "**if that liquidity doesn't recycle back**" — see [2].
- **Distribution name-drops** — verifiable and on-brand: **Robinhood** ("Earn" built on Maple credit underwriting; Maple→Robinhood Chain via syrupUSDG is covered in [[Maple brings onchain credit engine to Robinhood Chain via syrupUSDG]]), **Bitso** (~9M LatAm users), **Littio** (Colombian neobank, see [[Littio enables 24 7 dollar and euro transfers via Bre-B]]), **Plasma One** (UK). These are real products; whether they represent "retail banking exodus" vs. early niche adoption is the contested leap.

### [2] The debate — both sides (the load-bearing disagreement)

**Powell's / crypto side (deposits leave, banks narrow):** idle checking float is structurally mispriced; once a frictionless, 24/7, programmable alternative exists, agents reallocate it to term/yield products. The NY Fed disintermediation evidence is read as "the establishment confirming the direction."

**Bank / official counter-side (it's recycling + composition, not a one-for-one drain):** The dominant Fed counterargument — e.g. the Fed's **"Banks in the Age of Stablecoins"** notes (Dec 2025 / May 2026) — is that a stablecoin outflow does **not** automatically contract aggregate bank deposits. If issuers hold reserves **as bank deposits**, total system deposits are largely unchanged (just re-labeled wholesale vs retail). If reserves go into **T-bills**, the cash still recycles back to banks via dealer redeposits. So stablecoins may **reduce, recycle, or restructure** deposits depending on who holds the tokens and how reserves are invested — the $1T "drain" is the *no-recycle* tail, not the base case.

**The regulatory firewall Powell underweights — the GENIUS Act:** The **GENIUS Act (signed July 18, 2025)** *prohibits* covered payment-stablecoin issuers from paying interest or yield to holders merely for holding the token. That directly blunts the "stablecoins out-compete deposits on rate" mechanism — a *payment* stablecoin legally can't pay the yield. Powell's thesis survives this only because his products are **tokenized credit** (defined-term investment products), not payment stablecoins — a distinction the essay blurs. The live fight is the **"exchange/affiliate rewards loophole"**: the ABA + 40+ banking groups are lobbying to extend the ban to exchanges/affiliates, and the **OCC's GENIUS implementing rule (Oct 2026)** notably *dropped* a proposed hard yield crackdown and left yield "case-by-case," which community banks (ICBA) warn could still drain ~$850B of lending capacity off a ~$1.3T outflow model. Related: [[Connecting the Dots Stablecoin competition heats up post-GENIUS]], [[Stablecoin payments surge 70% after GENIUS Act]].

**Banks aren't passive:** incumbents are building defensive **tokenized-deposit** rails rather than conceding — see [[US banks build collaborative tokenized deposit network]], [[BNY explores tokenized deposits for payments network]], [[Bank of Montreal plans tokenized cash platform]], [[SoFi launches SoFiUSD, first stablecoin from a US national bank]]. Tokenized *deposits* keep money/lending fused inside the bank — the opposite of Powell's "pried apart" future.

### [4] Why it matters / so-what

The disintermediation question is one of the most consequential open debates in fintech: if even a fraction of the $6.6T transactional-deposit pool reprices toward tokenized credit, it compresses the bank funding spread that underwrites US lending — exactly what the NY Fed's Feb 2026 papers now quantify (14pp loans-to-assets drop at partner banks). But this specific artifact is a **CEO op-ed from an interested supplier**, restating figures (SVB, $6.6T TBAC, NY Fed, $1T) that are already in the public record and already covered here, and presenting the contested "exodus" conclusion as near-inevitable while underweighting (a) the GENIUS yield ban, (b) the deposit-recycling counterargument, and (c) banks' own tokenized-deposit defense. Its value is as a crisp statement of the bull thesis, not as new evidence.

## Челлендж / ред-тим

<!-- enrichment:challenge -->
Red-team of the essay's claims (author = Maple CEO Sidney Powell; conflicted supplier of the exact products predicted to win):

1. **Is the author independent?** No — Sid Powell is co-founder/CEO of Maple, which issues the tokenized-credit products (syrupUSDC/USDG) the essay says will absorb deposit outflows. This is a bull-case op-ed, not neutral research. Discount accordingly.

2. **Is the $6.6T figure a prediction?** No. It is the *total pool* of US transactional (non-interest-bearing) deposits flagged as theoretically "at risk" in the TBAC Digital Money presentation (Apr 30, 2025) — an upper bound of what *could* be exposed, not a forecast of what will move. The essay uses it correctly but it reads as scarier than it is.

3. **Does the GENIUS Act undercut the thesis?** Partly, and the essay glosses this. GENIUS (signed Jul 18, 2025) *bars* payment-stablecoin issuers from paying yield to holders — the single most direct "stablecoins beat deposits on rate" channel is legally closed for payment coins. The thesis survives only by routing through *tokenized credit / investment products* (not payment stablecoins), a distinction the essay blurs. Open: how durable is that distinction under future rulemaking.

4. **Is the $1T Fed "drain" the base case?** No — it's explicitly the *no-recycling* tail. Fed research ("Banks in the Age of Stablecoins," Dec 2025) argues outflows largely recycle: if issuers hold reserves as bank deposits, system deposits barely change; if in T-bills, cash returns via dealer redeposits. Net effect is more likely *restructuring* of deposits than a one-for-one drain. The essay cites the scary number and the caveat but leans on the former.

5. **Does the NY Fed paper "confirm the direction"?** It confirms a *mechanism* at partner banks (SR 1185: ~14pp lower loans-to-assets, ~147% higher reserves-to-assets), not that mass retail exodus is happening. SR 1179 frames it as the old narrow-banking debate — a normative policy choice, not an inevitability. Powell over-reads descriptive evidence as directional endorsement.

6. **Is "~10bp on deposits" representative?** Cherry-picked. It's roughly true for sticky non-interest checking float (what banks most defend) but ignores HYSA/MMAs paying multiples of that. The spread story is real; the framing maximizes it.

7. **Are the distribution examples proof of an "exodus"?** Overstated. Robinhood Earn, Bitso, Littio, Plasma One are real and real-user products, but they show *early niche adoption of on-chain yield*, not that the $6.6T transactional pool is leaving checking accounts. Convergence of a few fintechs on a structure ≠ category-wide retail migration.

8. **What's the strongest counter the essay concedes?** Community-bank risk and reserve concentration in a few large banks — Powell admits it. Consistent with ICBA's warning of ~$850B lost lending capacity off a ~$1.3T outflow model, and with the OCC's Oct 2026 rule declining a hard yield crackdown.

9. **Do banks have a defense he ignores?** Yes — tokenized *deposits* (US bank consortium, BNY, BMO, SoFiUSD) keep money and lending fused inside the bank, directly contesting the "pried apart" future. The essay frames banks as reactive/doomed; they are actively building competing rails.

10. **AI-agent-wallet premise — proven?** No, speculative. Agent wallets continuously rebalancing retail cash across term products is a forward bet; the essay even concedes agent wallets are "for operating funds, not core savings," which undercuts the "retail banking exodus" headline. Open.

11. **Is any of this new information?** No. SVB ($42B), the $6.6T TBAC figure, the NY Fed papers, the $1T model are all public record and already in the vault. The essay repackages known facts into a thesis; it breaks no news.

12. **Does the thesis depend on sustained high rates?** Partly open. The "defined-yield beats 10bp checking" pitch is most compelling when short rates are high; a materially lower-rate environment compresses the gap and the incentive to move idle cash. Not addressed.

13. **Reserve concentration / systemic risk** — the essay admits risk "moves, doesn't disappear": stablecoin reserves pool in a few large banks, so disintermediation could *concentrate* rather than diversify system risk. A fair, non-trivial concession that cuts against the clean "evolution" narrative.

14. **Timeframe falsifiability** — "over the next five years banks will narrow" is vague and hard to falsify; no quantified milestone (e.g. X% of transactional deposits migrated by year N). Rhetorical, not testable.

15. **Freshness vs. prior coverage** — closely adjacent to [[Maple grows AUM 18% YoY to $4.7B as DeFi TVL falls 39%]] (same company, 2026-10-19) and the GENIUS/stablecoin-vs-deposits cluster, but this is a *distinct artifact* (a named-author thesis essay, not an earnings/product item), so FRESH rather than a duplicate. No single prior note already carries this exact essay/thesis.

Importance: 2/5 — Topic is genuinely consequential (bank deposit disintermediation, GENIUS, NY Fed evidence), but the artifact itself is a conflicted vendor CEO op-ed that breaks no news, restates already-covered public figures, and presents a contested conclusion as near-inevitable while underweighting the GENIUS yield ban, the deposit-recycling counterargument, and banks' tokenized-deposit defense. Useful as a crisp bull-thesis marker; low as standalone signal.

## Market Research

**Sector & drivers.** The disintermediation thesis sits on a real but still-small base: total stablecoin supply was ~$301bn on 2026-10-21 (USDT ~$183.5bn / USDC ~$72.3bn ≈ 83% combined, per DefiLlama via Spark, 2026-10-19), up from ~$27bn end-2020 — ~12x in 5.5 years. Tokenized Treasury/MMF products are the yield-bearing analogue the note implies: grew from <$1bn early-2024 to >$15bn by April 2026 (BlackRock BUIDL ~$2.25bn on 2026-10-06, Franklin BENJI ~$760m, Ondo OUSG+USDY ~$3bn mid-2026). Structure: the deposit base it is disintermediating is huge and concentrated — US commercial-bank deposits ~$19.7tn (Sept 2026); the note's own anchor, a Treasury/ABA estimate, frames $6.6tn of *transactional* deposits as theoretically exposed, and a Fed scenario models up to $1tn of outflow if liquidity doesn't recycle. Why now: GENIUS Act regime created a licensed stablecoin rail, and the regulatory fight has shifted to *yield* — the OCC finalized rules (effective 2027-01-18) barring issuers from paying direct interest, leaving the affiliate/exchange "rewards" channel (the exact channel Maple/fintech distributors use) under case-by-case review. The thesis is live-direction, not live-scale: ~$301bn of stablecoins vs ~$19.7tn of deposits = ~1.5% — the exodus is modeled, not yet observed (the author concedes "the Fed hasn't seen that scale yet").

**Competitive landscape.** Sector KPIs: AUM/TVL, net APY paid to holders, and reserve/collateral quality. Maple is a supply-side onchain credit originator (overcollateralized institutional loans) wrapped as yield-bearing syrupUSDC/USDT/USDG; distribution comes from fintechs named in the note (Robinhood Earn, Bitso ~9M users, Littio, Plasma One). Basis of competition is rate × term × risk-disclosure, not brand. Recent moves: Maple AUM ~$4.6bn H1-2026 (+81% YoY), ~$4.82bn Aug-2026 (TVL ~$2.9-3.07bn DefiLlama); syrupUSDC ~$2.78bn @4.8% APY, syrupUSDT ~$913m @4.3%, syrupUSDG hit $200m in 8 days on Robinhood Chain (per KuCoin/TipRanks, Aug 2026). Position: a credible niche leader on the *credit-origination* side (analysis) but structurally dependent on others' distribution and on the regulatory tolerance of the yield loophole — it does not own the customer. Yield-ban pass-through risk is the KPI that matters most and is outside Maple's control.

**Comps & multiples.** In-base: [[Maple grows AUM 18% YoY to $4.7B as DeFi TVL falls 39%]] (own Q3 print, AUM +18% YoY vs DeFi TVL -39% — relative outperformance); [[Ondo launches Ondo Private Markets for tokenized exposure]] and [[Ondo, J.P. Morgan, Mastercard, Ripple complete tokenized Treasuries pilot]] (nearest tokenized-yield peer); [[Circle reports Q4 and full fiscal year 2025 results]] (the dominant compliant issuer, the "money" layer Maple rides on). Trading multiples: not computable — Maple (SYRUP token) and the tokenized-fund peers do not disclose comparable public EV/Revenue (protocol "revenue" ≠ GAAP revenue); no clean numerator/denominator → **no data** on EV/Rev, P/E. Only Circle is public-markets priced; it is not a like-for-like comp (issuer float economics, not credit origination). Qualitative comparison only; distribution not computed (<3 comparable public figures). [UNSOURCED] for SYRUP market-cap-to-AUM.

**Risk flags.**
1. **Regulatory kill-switch on yield.** The whole thesis runs through the GENIUS/OCC yield-ban loophole; the OCC rule (eff. 2027-01-18) already subjects affiliate/exchange reward programs to case-by-case review and >40 bank associations are lobbying to close it. If the pass-through channel is curbed, the retail "rate vs flat balance" pitch loses its engine — this is the single largest, fastest-acting risk.
2. **Author = interested party (de-PR).** The note is a Maple-CEO essay; it cites a NY Fed paper and Fed $1tn scenario as "independent confirmation" but the scale is admittedly unrealized (~1.5% of deposits). Treat the $6.6tn/$1tn as *exposed/modeled ceilings*, not flows — the piece is talking its own book toward syrupUSD demand.
3. **Credit + concentration risk moved, not removed (the note's own admission).** Yield is loan interest, so holders take borrower-default/credit risk and depeg risk, not FDIC-insured deposit safety; reserves concentrate in a few large banks, stranding community banks. Maple also depends entirely on third-party distribution (Robinhood/Bitso/Plasma) — it carries credit + counterparty risk while owning neither the customer nor the rail.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** This is a directional re-rating of where retail idle cash *can* sit, not yet a flow — watch two triggers: (a) the final shape of OCC/Clarity Act affiliate-yield enforcement through 2027 (binary for the thesis), and (b) whether syrupUSDG-style distribution deals convert modeled exposure into observed deposit beta. Falsifiable thesis: if stablecoin+tokenized-MMF supply has not meaningfully outgrown ~$300-350bn relative to the ~$19.7tn deposit base by the yield-rule effective date, the "banks hold less" claim stays theoretical and Maple remains a crypto-native credit niche rather than a retail-deposit substitute (analysis).
