MLB cracks down on using AI via dugout iPads to help shape in-game decisions
MLB had allowed teams to add some custom applications to their dugout iPads, but the league has reversed course and banned those apps due to concerns that live data was feeding AI-powered programs, influencing in-game decision-making. Rick Osentoski / USA Today 
In a crackdown that has rankled some front offices across the league, Major League Baseball has effectively outlawed the growing practice of using league-provided dugout iPads to access generative artificial intelligence, which some teams had increasingly leaned upon to help shape in-game strategy.
Advertisement
According to a commissioner’s office memo obtained by The Athletic , teams were pushing the boundaries of guidelines governing the use of technology by “in many cases” installing custom apps that expanded the use of the iPads “beyond their originally intended purpose to include recommendations regarding substitutions, pitch calling, and other in-game decisions traditionally made by players and coaches.”
As much as a third of the league used the dugout iPads for at least one of these purposes, according to people with knowledge of the technology who spoke on the condition of anonymity to discuss the issue candidly. The league memo was issued June 11 and gave teams more than a month to adjust before the ban took effect on Wednesday, in time for the start of the season’s second half Thursday.
The mid-season policy change has been met with frustration by some front-office members tasked with innovation. “It’s caused quite the stir,” said one high-ranking official of an MLB team’s research and development department. But even members of more data-friendly front offices say the trend raised eyebrows within the industry because of how many decisions made by managers and coaches could be aided by or even potentially replaced by technology, such as pitch calling or player substitutions.
MLB seemed to harbor similar concerns. The league’s review ultimately found “all clubs were compliant” with the league’s rules governing sign stealing and electronic-device usage, per MLB’s memo. The tech ban therefore did not come with punishment.

However, the issue proved urgent enough for the league to act mid-season in hopes of curbing the growth of these apps.
Said one front office executive: “Gotta stop the cheating before there’s cheating now.”
MLB declined comment but referred to the memo, which was issued by the league’s executive vice president of baseball operations, Morgan Sword. The players’ union also declined comment.
League-provided iPads were first introduced into dugouts league-wide in 2016 , though their usage was more tightly regulated in 2020 after sign-stealing scandals rocked the sport.
Advertisement
“For the first time, we’re going to be allowing players to view live in-game video via an iPad in the dugout or bullpen during the game,” former MLB executive Chris Marinak said in March 2021 . “The iPads are issued by MLB and fully controlled so that the players can only use the app that we’ve put on the device. They can’t access the internet for browsing or social media or any other kind of functions. The iPads are completely locked down and monitored with the software that we have on there.”
In-game video has been and remains available but only on a delay, usually only once an inning is completed. Rules posted in every clubhouse also prohibit non-field personnel from coming into the dugout during the game, an effort to limit inputting live information into the iPads.
But over time, players and teams have pushed for a loosening of some restrictions surrounding the iPads. Coaches and players wanted the ability to customize what the tablets could do for them, and the league allowed these liberties.
Entering this season, the league-issued iPads provided access to data through three tabs, according to a person with knowledge of the system who spoke on the condition of anonymity to be able to speak candidly. The first contained all the MLB-provided Statcast data and multiple video angles. The second tab contained all data related to the Automated Ball-Strike system (ABS). The third tab — known across the league as the “custom tab” — housed each team’s specialized data, akin to information found in old-school, paper-filled binders. Examples include matchup info, defensive positioning and player tendencies, which teams argued were necessities.
Going forward, teams can still upload static information, which is data that would be available before first pitch, though clubs were told that anything uploaded would ultimately remain subject to review by MLB.
Advertisement
Pitch-calling from the dugout may be at the center of this battle and is one of the usages the league appears to be targeting.
 The Athletic canvassed multiple players, general managers, coaches, research and development analysts and other front-office executives with knowledge of dugout iPad usage for their thoughts on the change. They weren’t authorized to speak publicly about the league’s reasoning for the change or their own teams’ use of customizable apps. A consensus emerged: some teams input information from the ongoing game to improve the generative AI models’ ability to anticipate the next pitch that would be thrown, or to recommend what should be thrown.
Jack Lambert, director of baseball operations at Driveline Baseball, an independent player development operation, said he believed pitch-calling was a logical way teams could leverage the tech.
“With someone live-scoring the game, teams could chart pitch type and location to get dynamically updating game plans,” Lambert said. “Those plans could determine if their approach at the plate should change as they face the lineup a second (or) third time in the game, and could also be applied to live pitch-calling.”
While it’s unknown exactly how many teams have used the league-provided iPads for pitch-calling, the practice of calling pitches from the dugout — rather than delegating that responsibility to catchers or pitchers, as is traditional — has grown. The Miami Marlins spearheaded the trend of having pitches called from the dugout, and other teams have subsequently adopted it. Multiple people briefed on the set-ups said that currently as many as six teams are making “pitch suggestions” from the dugout, as some clubs call them.
It’s unclear exactly how many teams were using the iPads with live in-game information, because teams are reluctant to talk about their innovations and lose any possible edge. But the league’s effort to change what’s visible on the tablets suggests the league was concerned.
Now, teams seemingly must return to the old-fashioned way of running numbers before the game. Fans will still get to blame the human managers for strategic mistakes, at least for now.
 —  The Athletic ’s Evan Drellich, Brittany Ghiroli and Jayson Stark contributed to this report.

Major League Baseball effectively banned custom apps on league-issued dugout iPads after finding ~1/3 of clubs were feeding live in-game data into generative-AI tools to shape substitutions, pitch-calling and other in-game decisions traditionally made by humans. ### [1] Facts / mechanism
- **The rule.** A commissioner's-office memo dated **June 11, 2026** (issued by EVP of baseball operations **Morgan Sword**, sent to GMs, assistant GMs and video coordinators) disabled the iPads' **"custom tab."** MLB-issued iPads carry three tabs: (1) Statcast data + delayed video, (2) Automated Ball-Strike (ABS) system data, (3) the **custom tab** housing each team's specialized data/apps. Only the custom tab is being removed. Ban took effect **Wednesday July 15 / going into the season's second half** (post–All-Star break, ~July 16–17, 2026).
- **What the apps did.** Teams installed custom apps that expanded iPad use "beyond their originally intended purpose to include recommendations regarding substitutions, pitch calling, and other in-game decisions." Some clubs live-scored the game and fed pitch type/location into generative-AI models to anticipate/recommend the next pitch. As many as **six teams** were making "pitch suggestions" from the dugout; the **Miami Marlins** pioneered dugout pitch-calling. Sources say the **NY Mets** were among teams that used the AI loophole (per Adam Ottavino).
- **The concern.** Generative AI potentially replacing human manager/coach judgment mid-game — a competitive-integrity / "spirit of the game" issue rather than a rules breach.
- **No punishment.** MLB's review found "all clubs were compliant" with sign-stealing and electronic-device rules; no discipline. Going forward teams may still upload **static, pre–first-pitch data**, subject to MLB review. iPads first entered dugouts league-wide in **2016**, tightened after the 2020 sign-stealing scandals.
- **Vendor/tech.** No specific AI vendor named; tools referred to generically as "generative AI"/"AI-powered programs." iPads are MLB-issued and locked down; custom apps were team-built.

**Sector & drivers.** This is not a market event; the read-through is institutional AI-tool restriction / shadow-AI governance. MLB banned custom dugout-iPad apps mid-season (memo 11 Jun 2026; ban live 15 Jul, second-half opener) after finding that as much as a third of clubs were feeding live in-game data into generative-AI tools to guide substitutions, pitch-calling and other decisions (per The Athletic, via StrictlyVC/Engadget, 16–17 Jul 2026). The broader sector this maps to — enterprise/institutional AI governance — is in a policy-catch-up phase: per Lenovo research (Apr 2026, via Vectra/Naveera coverage) ~70% of enterprise AI now runs outside IT oversight ("shadow AI"), and the EU AI Act's high-risk-system enforcement begins 2 Aug 2026 (per multiple governance vendors) — no data on a clean TAM for the AI-governance tooling market, so none asserted. Structure: fragmented, immature — tooling/policy lags adoption. "Why now": institutions are discovering unsanctioned generative-AI use inside sanctioned, "locked-down" hardware, and reacting with restriction rather than enablement.

**Competitive landscape.** The governance pattern (not a competitive market): the recurring KPIs are adoption-outside-oversight, incident/breach cost, and time-to-policy. Two failure modes recur across institutions — (a) restriction/ban (MLB here; also OpenAI limiting GPT-5.6 preview rollout at White House request, Jun 2026), vs (b) governed enablement (Santander open-sourcing its AI-governance stack, Jun 2026; MAS proposing AI risk-management guidelines for financial firms, Nov 2025; Jack Henry's AI bank-security push). MLB sits firmly in the restriction camp: it disabled the "custom tab" and reverted teams to pre-game static data only, with all uploads now subject to league review. Notably, the review found all clubs compliant with existing sign-stealing/electronic-device rules — so the ban is pre-emptive, not punitive (analysis).

**Risk flags.**
1. Restriction rarely works (regulatory/behavioral): governance research (via Sphere/Strac/Naveera, 2026) consistently finds bans push AI use underground — ~half of users keep personal accounts post-ban. Second-order effect: MLB may have driven the same behavior off-iPad (personal devices, pre-game workarounds) rather than eliminating it, and now has weaker visibility than under a monitored-tooling regime.
2. Enforcement/visibility gap: the ban relies on locking the "custom tab" and reviewing static uploads, but the league admits it doesn't know how many teams actually used live data — a governance control without measurement is hard to audit, so recurrence risk is high (analysis).
3. Precedent risk for institutions generally: an institution reversing previously granted AI liberties mid-cycle signals that "sanctioned + locked-down hardware" is not a sufficient control — the vector is the human feeding live data into an external model, not the device. Read-through: data-governance perimeters need to sit at the data/prompt layer, not the endpoint.

**What this changes (idea-lens).** (analysis) The signal to watch is not baseball but the pattern: institutions with locked-down, monitored tooling are still discovering shadow generative-AI use and defaulting to prohibition over governed enablement — the opposite of what enterprise-governance research recommends. Falsifiable thesis: restriction-first responses (MLB-style) will prove leaky and get replaced by enablement-plus-monitoring stacks (Santander-style) within 12–18 months; trigger to watch — whether MLB moves from an outright custom-app ban toward a league-approved/sanctioned AI tool, or whether other rule-bound institutions (leagues, regulated finance) similarly reverse and re-permit under controls. What breaks the thesis: bans hold and become the norm because the misuse (live in-game/live-data influence) is judged categorically unacceptable regardless of controls.
