Tesla driver in fatal Texas crash pressed accelerator 100%, NTSB confirms
The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) said Wednesday that the driver of a Tesla who crashed into a house in June had pressed the accelerator pedal to 100%, overriding the company’s Full Self-Driving (Supervised) software. 
Data recovered from the Tesla showed that the vehicle was traveling more than 70 miles per hour when it struck a house in Katy, Texas, killing 76-year-old resident Martha Avila, according to the NTSB. The family of the victim has since sued the alleged driver, 44-year-old Michael Butler, and Tesla, claiming negligence. Butler has also been charged with manslaughter.
The safety board shared the information as part of a preliminary report on the progress of its investigation into the crash. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration is also probing the incident.
The data confirms Tesla’s account of the crash, which the company shared in the days after it happened in order to show that its advanced driver assistance system wasn’t to blame. “[T]his [allegation] makes no sense. FSD drives slowly through neighborhood streets and this was a high speed crash!” Tesla CEO Elon Musk wrote on X shortly after the crash.
The NTSB said Wednesday that the 44-year-old driver was using Full Self-Driving (Supervised) on Rose Hollow Lane, a residential two-lane road with a speed limit of 30 miles per hour, prior to the crash. Security camera footage obtained by the safety board showed the car accelerating through an intersection, leaving the road, and hitting the house. The “weather was clear, the roadway was dry, and daylight conditions were present,” according to the NTSB.
Tesla requires that drivers using Full Self-Driving (Supervised) pay attention to the road and be ready to take control at any moment. Butler allegedly told authorities that he had “passed out” and that he was using Tesla’s driver assistance system. Police reportedly discovered that his Google searches included the terms “Tesla FSD not aggressive enough 2026,” “Tesla not aggressive enough,” and “Tesla FSD too timid,” according to local ABC news affiliate station KTRK TV .

### [0] What happened
On 2026-07-15, the NTSB released a **preliminary report** on the fatal June 19, 2026 crash in Katy, Texas, in which a 2025 Tesla Model 3 left a residential road on Rose Hollow Lane (30 mph limit) at >70 mph and struck a house, killing 76-year-old resident Martha Avila. Recovered vehicle data show the driver — 44-year-old Michael Butler — **pressed the accelerator pedal to 100%, manually overriding FSD (Supervised)**. Butler faces a manslaughter charge and, with Tesla, a negligence suit from Avila's family; he told authorities he had "passed out." Police reportedly found Google searches such as "Tesla FSD not aggressive enough 2026." The finding **corroborates Tesla's own account** (shared days after the crash, with Musk publicly asserting FSD was not to blame). Both NTSB and NHTSA are investigating.

## Челлендж / ред-тим

### Red-team questions
1. "Confirms Tesla's account" — is that the NTSB's framing or a Tesla-friendly spin? The NTSB report states the data (100% accelerator, >70 mph); the "vindication" narrative is largely from Tesla/Musk and pro-Tesla outlets.
2. Is this **preliminary** or final? It is preliminary — no probable cause yet; framing it as case-closed for Tesla is premature.
3. Does "override" mean FSD was disengaged or still active? Pressing the accelerator overrides FSD speed/braking control but does not necessarily disengage steering — worth not overstating "driver was fully in control."
4. Does this finding actually reduce Tesla's civil liability? The negligence suit names Tesla too; product-defect theories (e.g., allowing 100% override in a 30 mph zone) can survive a "driver pressed pedal" finding.
5. Is there any evidence the driver's "passed out" claim vs. the deliberate 100% throttle + "FSD not aggressive enough" searches implies intent/impairment rather than a system fault? Yes — cuts against Tesla-fault narrative but is unproven in court.
6. Does this crash still feed the broader NHTSA FSD probe even if the driver overrode the system? Likely yes as a data point, but its weight is diminished if the override finding holds.
7. Is there any TSLA stock-price move attributable specifically to this NTSB note? No evidence of a discrete move; it is one headline amid a 3-probe docket.
8. Could NHTSA reach a different conclusion than NTSB? They are separate agencies with separate probes; NHTSA's regulatory (recall) authority is the one that matters for the fleet.
9. Is the "100%" figure from Tesla's own EDR data, and is that data independently verified? Data was recovered from the vehicle; NTSB reports it — but chain-of-custody / Tesla-supplied telemetry is a recurring critique in FSD cases.
10. Does this materially change the robotaxi/Cybercab thesis? No — Cybercab risk hinges on unsupervised FSD performance and EA26002, not a supervised-mode manual override.
11. Are there any conflicting reports on whether FSD or Autopilot was engaged? Sources consistently say FSD (Supervised); no Autopilot conflict found.
12. Is the source base diverse or echo-chamber? Mix of neutral (TechCrunch, ABC13, Engadget, CNBC) and pro-Tesla (TeslaNorth, notateslaapp); core facts corroborated across neutral outlets.
13. Could axing the AV brake-pedal rule ([[US proposes axing AV brake-pedal rule in boost for Tesla]]) interact with liability for override-capable systems? Speculative; flagged as an open regulatory thread.
14. Is there a fintech/tech angle beyond auto safety? Only indirectly via TSLA valuation being FSD/robotaxi-levered; the note itself is an auto-safety item.

**Importance: 2/5** — From a fintech/tech-investor lens this is a low-to-moderate item: a preliminary NTSB finding on a single crash that modestly supports Tesla's liability narrative but changes no thesis-level driver. It matters only as one input into the far larger NHTSA FSD probe / robotaxi-rollout risk that actually moves TSLA. Not market-moving on its own; useful color, not a catalyst.
